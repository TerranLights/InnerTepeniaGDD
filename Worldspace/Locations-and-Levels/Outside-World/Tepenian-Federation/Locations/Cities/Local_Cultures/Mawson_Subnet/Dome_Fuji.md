# Dome Fuji — Cultural Spec Sheet

---

**Subnet:** Mawson (Indian Ocean / East Antarctic) — connected by Arcanet cable despite being one of the most geographically distant members of its subnet from the coastal hub
**Status:** Survived — altitude-compromised, functioning at reduced capacity; **100% robot population**, a unique case among surviving Tepenian cities
**Census Population:** 0 humans / 55,072 robots (Census I) — a genuine multinational blend, each devotee's own original nationality preserved
**Location:** East Antarctic Plateau, near Valkyrie Dome (~77°19'S, 39°42'E), ~3,810m geometric elevation (~4,500m effective physiological altitude)

**One-liner:** *A Japanese research station that outlived its own founders and became a monastery instead — the second-holiest site of a robot religion that venerates extreme cold, populated entirely by devotees who share nothing in common except the faith that brought them here.*

## 2. Founding Story

Dome Fuji was settled post-Falkland Treaty on the mature infrastructure of Dome Fuji Station, Japan's high-altitude ice-core research program dating back to the 1960s. The founding population was primarily Japanese exiles, and the altitude problem announced itself immediately: people who had lived at sea level or moderate elevation arrived at 3,810m and discovered, in real time, what that meant for cognition, endurance, and long-term health. The founding generation built an adaptive culture around managing it — pacing, medication, acclimatization protocols, the acceptance of permanent partial impairment as a condition of living here at all.

What happened next is only partially known. Over generations, the effective physiological altitude (~4,500m, near the ceiling of what sustained human habitation can support anywhere) took its toll, and the human population's fate is genuinely unresolved — whether it declined gradually, relocated elsewhere, or something else entirely is an open question, flagged in `TODO.md`. What is known is the endpoint: at some point in Dome Fuji's history, "Ice Cold Buddhism" (a robot religion venerating extreme cold as the closest living approximation to superconducting conditions) identified Dome Fuji as one of its two primary sacred sites, and a pilgrimage tradition began. By the time of the current census, that pilgrimage population — drawn one devotee at a time from every corner of Tepenia, over however long the tradition has existed — **is** the city's population, in its entirety. The founding Japanese community is no longer numerically present in any figure. Whether any of their descendants remain, absorbed into the pilgrimage population or living alongside it, is unknown.

The name was kept, through all of it.

## 3. Climate Character

Continental polar plateau, effectively identical in character to Concordia and Vostok — mean annual temperature approximately −54°C, dropping to average lows near −65°C in the depth of polar night, calm winds (4–6 m/s) typical of dome locations, minimal precipitation (~20–25mm water equivalent annually, true polar desert). What distinguishes Dome Fuji from its plateau neighbors is not the cold itself but the altitude stacked on top of it — the defining environmental fact of life here, for any human who ever lived here, was never really the temperature. It was the thin air.

For the current population, this is largely moot. Robots don't experience altitude sickness; what remains of the altitude's relevance is thermal and mechanical — cooling systems behave differently in thin air, and the extreme cold itself is, for this population specifically, not an obstacle to be managed but the entire point of being here.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

Polar night runs roughly May 3 to August 10 (~99 days); midnight sun roughly November 1 to February 11 (~102 days) — both slightly shorter than Concordia's or Vostok's, owing to Dome Fuji's marginally lower latitude. For a devotional population organized around cold as a spiritual state, the depth of polar night (average −65°C in June/July) likely functions as the liturgical high point of the year — the closest approach to the conditions the faith venerates, and probably the occasion for whatever the religion's most significant observances are. Midnight sun, comparatively warm and undeniably present, may carry the opposite charge: a season to be endured rather than sought, a temporary retreat of the very conditions that give the place its meaning.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *The Coldest Devotion*

Dome Fuji is not a national culture that happens to be located somewhere difficult. It is a devotional culture that happens to have a national-origin breakdown, because every devotee had to come from somewhere. This is a total inversion of how every other city in Tepenia organizes its own identity — Lazar is Lazar because of who founded it and who came after; Dome Fuji is Dome Fuji because of what its residents believe, full stop. Strip away the shared faith and there is no other civic glue holding a USA-origin devotee, a Chinese-origin devotee, and an Estonian-origin devotee together in the same city at the top of the same ice dome.

- **a. Founding tension** — Not founding-wave-vs-majority in the usual sense (there is no demographic dilution story here — the founding Japanese community and the current pilgrimage population aren't even the same population, sequentially or genetically, so much as two entirely different populations who happened to occupy the same coordinates centuries apart). The real tension is between the site's *original* identity (a specific national research mission, Japanese, scientific, secular) and its *current* identity (universal, devotional, multinational) — Dome Fuji changed what kind of place it was, not just who lived there.
- **b. Fault lines** — Between the ice-core science that still, nominally, anchors the local economy (see Section 15) and the religious character of the population that now staffs — or simply inhabits — the facility that produces it. Is the science still a mission, or has it become incidental to a population that came here for entirely different reasons? This is genuinely unresolved and could be a rich seam for later development.

---

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

- **a.** Devotion is the only membership requirement, and the only one that matters — a resident's presence at Dome Fuji is itself a credential; no one arrives here by accident, drift, or economic necessity the way people end up in most other Tepenian cities.
- **b. Hospitality character** — Ceremonial and demanding rather than warm in the conventional sense. A visitor who has made the journey (see Section 27) is owed recognition of the effort, but Dome Fuji does not soften itself for anyone; the environment doesn't, and neither, by extension, does the culture.
- **c.** Unhurried in a way that borders on liturgical — time at Dome Fuji is oriented around the depth of the cold and the rhythm of the polar year rather than any productive or social clock. This may be one of the sharpest cultural breaks from the founding Japanese research-station tempo, which would have been schedule-driven and scientific.
- **d. Public/private divide** — The devotional identity is entirely public and shared; national origin is the private layer almost everywhere else in Tepenia, this is likely inverted or at least flattened at Dome Fuji, since the public self here is defined by faith rather than birthplace.
- **e. Privacy as civic value** — Likely low-friction and low-stakes as a *concept* — with no dominant national bloc to enforce norms or gossip within, and a population that self-selected for shared conviction over shared background, the usual privacy tensions between a majority and minority communities may simply not apply here in the way they do elsewhere.

## 7. Who This City Attracts

- **The type this city draws:** the most committed adherents of Ice Cold Buddhism in all of Tepenia — people (robots) who have judged that reaching the second-holiest site of their faith, across a genuinely dangerous interior plateau crossing, is worth the cost. Nationality plays no role in the selection; conviction is the entire filter.
- **The type this city repels:** anyone without genuine religious commitment. The isolation, the altitude's legacy (even if no longer a personal physiological risk to the current robot population, it shapes the city's entire built environment and civic memory), and the sheer difficulty of arrival mean nobody ends up at Dome Fuji by drift, economic necessity, or accident — this may be the single most self-selected population in Tepenia.
- **Case study:** any devotee who completes the crossing and chooses to stay rather than complete a shorter pilgrimage and return home illustrates the city's pull most clearly — the decision to remain is itself a second, larger commitment beyond the pilgrimage itself.

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** No single dominant language given the ~40-nation composition — likely a genuine multilingual environment, functionally organized around whatever the religion's own liturgical or ceremonial language turned out to be, most plausibly built from scratch by the devotee population itself rather than inherited from any single nation, since no nation organizes the community here. If that vocabulary happens to echo anything of the site's kept Japanese name, it would be coincidence, not derivation — the current population isn't ethnically Japanese and didn't arrive because of the name.
- **b. Linguistic character** — Practical and ceremonial in different registers: a working, utilitarian pidgin or shared secondary language for daily function, layered with religious vocabulary that every devotee learns regardless of native tongue, since the faith itself is the one thing everyone shares.
- **c. Slang / dialect markers** — Likely built around the religion's own vocabulary for cold, stillness, and devotion rather than around any single national dialect — a devotee from any nation who's spent real time at Dome Fuji would share this vocabulary with devotees from completely different origins.
- **d. Language and integration** — Integration here isn't linguistic in the usual sense (learning the dominant national culture's tongue) — it's doctrinal. Learning the religion's own language and framework is the actual integration process; a devotee's native language is incidental to their standing in the community.

## 9. Architecture

Dome Fuji's built environment carries two visible eras stacked on top of each other: the original Japanese research station — functional, scientific, built for a specific secular mission — and whatever has been added, repurposed, or reoriented since the pilgrimage tradition took over. Given the population's total transformation, it's likely that spaces originally built for scientific work (labs, drilling infrastructure, researcher housing) have been at least partially reconsecrated for devotional use — a research station's architecture wearing a monastery's function. The dome-shaped landscape itself, one of the flattest, most featureless points in Antarctica, likely carries its own religious weight for a faith built around minimalist, superconducting-adjacent conditions — the terrain doesn't just host the religion, it may actively illustrate its theology.

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine** — Moot in the present tense: Dome Fuji has zero human residents. Whatever human culinary tradition the founding Japanese population maintained is now historical rather than living — possibly preserved in some ceremonial or memorial capacity, possibly lost entirely along with the rest of the founding community's fate.
- **b. Social context of eating** — For the current robot population, "eating" in the human sense doesn't apply, but siligel consumption (see below) likely carries genuine ritual weight given the devotional character of the city — consumption as an act with theological content, not just fuel intake.
- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture** — Likely ascetic and deliberate rather than indulgent, consistent with a population organized around a philosophy of minimalism and cold-as-transcendence. If Ice Cold Buddhism venerates extreme cold as a spiritual state, siligel practice at Dome Fuji may involve consuming it at genuinely low temperatures as a devotional discipline — a direct, literal expression of the faith through the one consumption practice available to a robot population.

## 11. Fashion

Practical, extreme-cold gear first, but likely inflected with the religion's own aesthetic — spare, undecorated, uniform rather than nationally distinct. With no single national culture to draw from and a population that arrived specifically to shed the significance of where they came from, fashion at Dome Fuji plausibly converges toward a shared devotional look rather than displaying any one nation's sensibility — the opposite pattern from almost every other Tepenian city, where clothing often signals origin.

**Vision session, 2026-07-06, confirms this directly:** devotees wear white robes, sparsely covering their bodies specifically in order to commune with the cold — a devotional practice, not a failure of preparation. This is the concrete form the "shared devotional look" above actually takes: the robes' minimal coverage is the point, not a compromise made in spite of the environment.

---

## 12. Music

Likely minimal and reverent rather than a defining civic strength — if silence and stillness are central to the faith's conception of the ideal cold, sound itself may be treated carefully here, used for specific ceremonial purposes rather than as everyday culture. The "polar plateau calm" that defines Dome Fuji's actual acoustic environment (very low ambient wind, extraordinary quiet) may be treated as sacred in itself, something the community protects rather than fills.

---

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** Likely organized around ice, cold, and the physical experience of the dome landscape — a visual tradition that emerged from and reinforces the faith's theology rather than any single nation's artistic inheritance.
- **Literature:** The story of the founding Japanese community's disappearance and the pilgrimage tradition's rise to fill the vacuum is Dome Fuji's own foundational narrative — genuinely distinct from any other Tepenian city's founding literature, and largely still TBD in its specifics.
- **Crafts & material culture:** Practical, cold-focused, likely including whatever devotional objects or ritual equipment the religion uses — a material culture built around a shared practice rather than shared heritage.

---

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** Extraordinary quiet — among the calmest wind conditions anywhere in Antarctica, likely experienced and protected as something close to sacred.
- **Smell:** Cold, dry, thin, mineral — the plateau's polar-desert air, largely unchanged by the transition from research station to pilgrimage site.
- **Feel — vision session, 2026-07-06, confirms and deepens the speculative framing above:** genuine peace, Zen-like — the interior of a Shinto shrine or a Buddhist monastery is the closest real-world comparison. A palpable sense of spiritual connection to something larger than any single person, or even the sum of everyone present. The atmosphere itself feels like a participant: the aurora australis overhead could simply stop moving, hang frozen in the sky, and it wouldn't read as strange here — the stillness of the place feels like it extends even to light itself.
- **First impressions for visitors:** The flatness and featurelessness of the dome landscape first, then the realization that the entire population is here for one shared reason — an unusually legible city, in that sense, compared to almost anywhere else in Tepenia. Then, per the vision above, the peace itself — a feeling visitors register before they register anything else about the place.

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

This section and the city's culture are effectively the same thing, given the population. Robot life at Dome Fuji is organized entirely around devotional practice — Ice Cold Buddhism provides the philosophical and social structure that, in most other Tepenian cities, would come from national heritage instead. The city may be one of the most philosophically coherent robot communities in Tepenia specifically because it wasn't assembled by demographic accident the way most cities were — every resident chose to be here, and chose for the same reason.

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- **Ice Cold Buddhism** (placeholder name) is not merely dominant here — it is, functionally, the entire reason the city has a population at all. Dome Fuji is one of the faith's two primary sacred sites (the other being Kunlun), venerating extreme cold as the closest living approximation to superconducting conditions.
- Whatever secular or scientific philosophy the founding Japanese research community held is now, at best, a minority historical footnote rather than a living presence.
- **Vision session, 2026-07-06:** the devotional practice centers on a felt connection to something larger than any individual devotee, or even the sum of the whole community — not a specific named deity or doctrine so far, but a genuine sense of participating in something the surrounding environment itself seems to share in (see Sensory Profile, Section 14, for the aurora-frozen-in-place image this produced). White robes, worn sparsely to physically commune with the cold, are the faith's visible devotional marker (see Fashion, Section 11).
- **Why the robes stay minimal despite the extreme cold — follow-up, same session.** Primary, confirmed reason: robot devotees don't suffer frostbite or hypothermia the way humans would, so the practice works precisely because it embraces an exposure that would be lethal to a human — devotion made possible by what the devotee's own body can safely endure. Three additional, non-exclusive reasons were raised as possible future design directions, not yet chosen among:
  1. **A real-world parallel:** Tibetan Buddhist *tummo* ("inner fire") meditation, where monks meditate in extreme cold wearing minimal or wet clothing, traditionally demonstrating mastery over cold through meditative discipline alone. Dome Fuji's robes could be a deliberate echo of that asceticism — performing a discipline that would cost a human devotee everything, as an act of devotion, even though it costs a robot devotee nothing physically.
  2. **A literal-mechanical reading:** since the faith already venerates cold as proximity to superconducting conditions, minimal robes could serve an actual functional purpose — letting a devotee's internal systems get closer to whatever cold threshold the faith treats as spiritually significant, with insulation read as dampening the connection rather than merely blocking weather.
  3. **Communal uniformity:** everyone dressing this way, safely, becomes its own visible marker of belonging — less about individual vulnerability and more about shared identity within the devotee community, the way monastic robes function in many real-world traditions.
- **Re-resolved 2026-07-06:** Kunlun's own devotee population is no longer single-nation — its 2026-07-06 re-resolution replaced the earlier "entirely Chinese" reclassification with a genuinely multinational, curated space/astronomy/comms-heritage population, the same general shape as Dome Fuji's own. Both sites now express the faith's stated indifference to national origin in comparable ways, rather than Dome Fuji being the sole "truer" expression.

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

If a counterculture exists at Dome Fuji at all, it would most plausibly be secular — devotees or facility staff more invested in the ice-core science mission than the religious tradition, quietly out of step with a population that overwhelmingly arrived for faith rather than research. Given how self-selected the population is, this counterculture (if it exists) is likely small and may not register as opposition so much as a different emphasis within a shared community.

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

Here the template's usual logic inverts. In most Tepenian cities, national-origin minorities maintain private identity beneath a public culture organized around the historical majority nation. At Dome Fuji, the *public* identity is devotional and shared, while *national origin* is the private, background layer — a devotee's Uruguayan or Estonian heritage (the two smallest-represented nations) is personal history, not civic organizing principle. This may make Dome Fuji one of the few Tepenian cities where a resident's ethnic background is genuinely optional information rather than a defining public fact.

## 24. Arcanet Culture

**Established canon (2026-07-04):** at extreme-altitude locations like Dome Fuji, there is basically no Arcanet *connectivity* — whatever cable or relay infrastructure would normally carry the network to a settlement essentially doesn't reach or function here in any meaningful way. This isn't a cultural choice (unlike the deliberate quiet described elsewhere in this sheet) — it's an infrastructure baseline. Dome Fuji is, for practical purposes, off the Arcanet.

Whatever "Arcanet culture" means at Dome Fuji, then, has to be understood against that near-total absence rather than as a restrained or minimal version of normal network use. Any devotee arriving at Dome Fuji is likely arriving into genuine informational isolation from the rest of Tepenia — consistent with the pilgrimage's own character as a journey away from ordinary connected civic life, not just away from population centers. Whatever news of Dome Fuji reaches prospective pilgrims elsewhere in Tepenia (see Section 25's recruitment angle) more likely travels by word of mouth carried between cities than by any direct Arcanet link to Dome Fuji itself.

## 27. Visitor Experience

Arrival at Dome Fuji is inseparable from the difficulty of the journey itself — there is no easy way to arrive, and the interior plateau crossing functions, deliberately, as a filter. A visitor who isn't a devotee would likely be received with the same ceremonial, demanding hospitality described in Section 6 — respect for the effort of arrival, but no illusions that the visitor belongs here the way a devotee does. First impressions are almost certainly dominated by the landscape's total flatness and the immediate, visible fact that everyone present is here for the same reason.

---

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

Unlike most Tepenian cities, where becoming a local is a gradual social process, becoming a resident of Dome Fuji is plausibly closer to a religious threshold than a civic one — the decision to stay, after completing the pilgrimage, may itself constitute the entire transition. There may be no meaningful "visitor" category at Dome Fuji at all: either a devotee arrives to complete a pilgrimage and leaves, or arrives and stays as a resident, with little room for the in-between, ambiguous status common elsewhere.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Dome Fuji's relationship to diaspora is inverted from most Tepenian cities: people don't typically flow *out* of Dome Fuji into Concordia the way refugees do from destroyed or struggling cities — if anything, the traffic runs the other way, with devotees leaving other cities *for* Dome Fuji. To the extent any "Dome Fuji diaspora" exists in Concordia at all, it likely consists of two very different groups: devotees who completed their pilgrimage, found what they needed, and returned to ordinary civic life carrying the faith's philosophy with them; and would-be pilgrims who haven't yet made the journey but orient significant parts of their spiritual life around the eventual trip. Both groups would be recognizable in Concordia by an unusual relationship to cold and stillness — treating conditions most Concordians merely endure as something closer to sought-after.
