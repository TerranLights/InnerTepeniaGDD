# Fort McMurdo — Cultural Spec Sheet

---

**Subnet:** Janbogo
**Status:** Damaged; partially operational — largest ruins in Tepenia by physical footprint
**Census II Population:** 173,548 humans / 164,621 robots / 338,169 combined *(rank ~24th of ~30 — "island cap"; Census I was 223,041 humans / 223,974 robots / 447,015 combined, so population actually declined between censuses even before the war)*
**Location:** Ross Island, Ross Sea coast, in the shadow of Mount Erebus (~77°51'S, 166°40'E)

**One-liner:** *Tepenia's biggest built thing, never its biggest population — the industrial capital that measured a person by output, not origin, and built half the Federation's infrastructure from a peninsula it could never fully fill.*

---

## PART I — FOUNDATIONS

---

## 1. National/Ethnic Composition

| Tier | Nations |
|------|---------|
| Primary | China, USA *(founding operator heritage)* |
| Significant | Japan, Germany, France, UK, Italy |
| Notable | South Korea, Canada, Indonesia, Australia *(founding wave)*, Philippines, Malaysia, New Zealand *(founding wave)*, Chile |

*Fort McMurdo has the most cosmopolitan Significant tier of any Tepenian city documented so far — five nations (Japan, Germany, France, UK, Italy), none of them dominant, all of them real. That breadth is a direct product of the city's founding function: a rotational-labor industrial hub absorbs people from everywhere, and some fraction of every rotation stays. The founding tension here is unlike Janbogo's (founding nation drops to minority) or Zhongshan's (founding nation stays sole majority) — the USA retained its founding-operator status all the way to Census II, but had to share Primary tier equally with China rather than being displaced by it. Two co-equal Primary nations, one that built the place and one that worked it into what it became.*

---

## 2. Founding Story

McMurdo Station was, before the exile, the largest and best-equipped station on the continent — a genuine small-town infrastructure of piers, fuel depots, vehicle yards, a harbor, and full utilities. When the Falkland Treaty exiles arrived, they inherited the most substantial ready-to-use foundation of any Tepenian city. American exiles founded and administered it, but McMurdo Station had always been a multinational working environment in real life, and the exile city continued that pattern from day one — it was never a mono-national settlement pretending otherwise.

The "Fort" prefix was deliberate: a reference to Fort MacMurray, Alberta — a name that by the 2500s meant one thing, industrial resource extraction at scale. The founders were not being subtle about what they were building. This would not be a cultural capital or a symbolic first city. It would be a working city, and the name said so before anyone set foot in it.

Transient, rotational labor was a structural feature from the start — extraction and industrial operations pulled workers from across Tepenia on seasonal and contract rotations. Most rotations ended in departure. Some didn't. Over five generations, enough workers who arrived on a contract chose to stay that the population contributing that labor — overwhelmingly Chinese in origin — grew to match the founding American population exactly, without ever displacing it from the historical record of who built the place.

---

## 3. Climate Character

Mount Erebus is the defining fact of Fort McMurdo's climate and psychology both — the southernmost active volcano on Earth, its permanent lava lake glowing at the summit, visible from virtually every point in the city, generating a near-constant plume of gas and ash. Katabatic winds pour down off the polar plateau through McMurdo Sound, producing brutal wind-chill even at moderate ambient temperatures. At ~118 days, Fort McMurdo's polar night is the longest of any coastal Tepenian city — roughly six weeks longer than Janbogo's, just across the same subnet.

Across the Sound sit the McMurdo Dry Valleys — the largest ice-free area in Antarctica, exposed rock and sediment where the surrounding mountains block glacier flow entirely. It is a landscape that looks like nowhere else on the continent, and it sits close enough to be a daily fact of Fort McMurdo's geography rather than a distant curiosity.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

Fort McMurdo runs on two seasonal clocks that point in opposite directions, and the friction between them shapes the whole year. In **winter**, McMurdo Sound freezes solid, opening the sea ice road — the only overland route to the Dry Valleys and the mainland — for extraction crews. In **summer**, the ice breaks up, closing that road but opening the harbor to sea traffic, including the New Zealand freighters that bring in raw materials via the Ross region supply line. The city is never simultaneously connected by both routes. Every year, Fort McMurdo trades one kind of access for the other, and its industrial rhythm — what gets extracted when, what gets shipped when, which crews are working which side of the Sound — is built entirely around that trade.

The ~118-day polar night doesn't stop work; it just moves it indoors and under floodlight. Erebus's summit glow becomes the one visible light source on the darkest nights, a strange, involuntary landmark that residents orient by more than they'd admit to outsiders.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *Merit Has No Passport*

Fort McMurdo's civic identity is built on a claim it mostly, but not entirely, lives up to: that what you can do matters more than where you're from. It is a culture forged by physically demanding, highly interdependent industrial work, where a crew that can't trust its weakest link fails together — a condition that makes competence-based status not just an ideal but a practical necessity. The city that resulted is direct, unsentimental, and largely unbeautiful, in the specific way that places built to work rather than to be admired tend to be.

- **a. Founding tension** — The USA retains institutional memory as founding operator: the naming, the early infrastructure decisions, the historical record all point to it. But China's population, arriving overwhelmingly through the rotational-labor system and increasingly choosing to stay, grew to exact parity with the founding population by Census II — not a takeover, a tie. The tension is less "who is the real Fort McMurdo" and more "founding legacy versus present-day equality," playing out quietly in who ends up in administrative roles versus who ends up running the crews that actually keep the city alive.
- **b. Fault lines** — The meritocratic ideology is sincerely believed and largely true on the shift floor, but administrative and management roles still skew toward founding-heritage lineages in a way workers notice, grumble about privately, and rarely organize around — the population is simply too transient, historically, for sustained labor politics to take root. It surfaces occasionally, briefly, and dissipates when the next rotation cycles through.

---

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

- **a.** Your output is your standing. Asking about someone's background before you've seen their work is considered mildly rude — not offensive, just backwards.
- **b. Hospitality character** — Functional, not ceremonial. A new arrival gets a bunk, a meal, and a work assignment. Warmth, when it's offered, is offered through being handed real responsibility quickly — trust expressed as workload, not as conversation.
- **c.** Shift-driven, not leisurely. The city runs on rotations, and social time is scheduled around them rather than the other way around. Canteens between shifts are where the actual socializing happens, compressed and efficient.
- **d. Public/private divide** — Work identity — your role, your reliability record, your crew — is entirely public. National origin, personal history, and family are treated as private by default, almost beside the point in daily interaction.
- **e. Privacy as civic value** — Low gossip culture around where someone's from; high, almost obsessive scrutiny around whether someone's reliable. Arcanet use in Fort McMurdo skews toward shift logs, equipment status, and safety reporting over social chatter.

---

## 7. Who This City Attracts

- **The type this city draws:** people who want to be judged by what they can build rather than who they were before they got here — skilled laborers, people starting over, people building a stake before moving on, workers whose home city's culture never quite fit them.
- **The type this city repels:** people who need slow, ceremonial belonging, or deep multi-generational cultural roots to feel at home. Fort McMurdo will never ask where you're from. It will also never make you feel like you've arrived somewhere with a story.

---

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** English (American-inflected) and Mandarin, roughly co-equal, reflecting the co-Primary population split.
- **b. Linguistic character** — Functional bilingual code-switching, heaviest in industrial and shift-report contexts; a hybrid technical jargon that borrows freely from both languages regardless of which language a given sentence is otherwise in.
- **c. Slang / dialect markers** — Shift-time references replace clock-time in casual conversation ("end of second," not "6 PM"). Erebus itself became an idiom: "she's smoking today" describes both the volcano's ash output and a coworker's mood, and everyone understands which one you mean from context.
- **d. Language and integration** — Low pressure. Competence is judged by work output, not accent. Multilingual signage and documentation were a practical necessity from the founding rotational-labor system onward, not a later accommodation.

---

## 9. Architecture

Functional industrial throughout — volcanic basalt where local stone was usable, expanded dramatically from the already-enormous real McMurdo Station footprint into piers, vehicle yards, fuel storage, and utility infrastructure on a scale no other Tepenian city matches. Nothing about Fort McMurdo's built environment was designed to be looked at. The two exceptions — Cape Evans and Cape Royds, the preserved pre-exile expedition huts of Scott and Shackleton a short distance up the Ross Island coast — are kept deliberately separate from the working city, treated as historical artifacts rather than integrated architecture, visited rather than lived in.

---

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine** — Hearty and calorie-dense, built for physically demanding shift work: a fusion of American diner staples and Chinese labor-crew cooking traditions, served canteen-style rather than as home-cooked ritual.
- **b. Social context of eating** — Meals are refueling first, but the canteens between shifts are where cross-crew socializing actually happens — the closest thing Fort McMurdo has to a communal gathering space.
- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture** — Industrial-grade, high-volume siligel production is itself one of the city's exports — practical and reliable rather than artisanal, matching the city's whole relationship to craft.

---

## 11. Fashion

Function over form: insulated coveralls, high-visibility role and shift markers, minimal individual expression during work hours. Personal style is reserved for off-shift time, where it tends to run harder in the opposite direction — a released-pressure-valve aesthetic among people who spend most of their week in identical protective gear.

---

## 12. Music

Work-rhythm music dominates — practical, repetitive, built for shift changes and physical labor rather than performance. Source-nation folk traditions carried in by rotational workers never fully disappear; they surface in canteens and bunk-block gatherings, a running undercurrent beneath the industrial mainstream that most residents can hum a few bars of regardless of their own ancestry.

---

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** An industrial-documentary tradition — photographing the work, the machines, the volcano — more prominent than fine art. Fort McMurdo's visual culture treats its own scale as the subject.
- **Literature:** Shift-log writing as a genre unto itself; oral storytelling passed between rotating crews; Erebus as a recurring literary figure — watched, personified, never quite trusted.
- **Crafts & material culture:** Volcanic glass and repurposed industrial material worked into small, functional-looking craft objects — practical craftsmanship is valued here even when the object itself has no practical use.

---

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** Constant machinery hum, punctuated by the occasional deep rumble of Erebus.
- **Smell:** Sulfur on certain wind directions, machine oil, fuel.
- **Feel:** Volcanic grit underfoot everywhere, cold that the wind makes worse than the thermometer suggests.
- **First impressions for visitors:** Scale. Nothing prepares a visitor for how big Fort McMurdo actually is, or for how completely Erebus dominates the skyline the moment you can see it.

---

## PART III — SOCIAL STRUCTURE

---

## 15. Division of Industry

- Industrial / manufacturing: 35% — the core identity of the city
- Marine / resource extraction: 25% — Dry Valleys operations, harbor logistics
- Technical / scientific: 15% — equipment maintenance, extraction engineering
- Commercial: 10% — shipping logistics, canteen and bunk-block services
- Education: 8% — apprenticeship-style technical training, not formal academia
- Other: 7%

---

## 16. Human-Robot Relations

The meritocratic ideology that governs national origin extends, mostly sincerely, to the human-robot line as well — a crew that depends on every member's reliability doesn't have much patience for treating a robot's output as worth less than a human's. Work assignment in Fort McMurdo is genuinely close to origin-blind and species-blind on the shift floor. The same fault line that runs through the national-origin story repeats here, though: administrative and management roles skew human in a way that's rarely discussed openly and rarely challenged, given how transient the workforce has always been.

---

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

With robots making up close to half the Census II population (164,621 of 338,169), robot labor is deeply embedded in extraction, manufacturing, and equipment maintenance roles — not a supplementary workforce but a core one. Fort McMurdo's robot culture inherits the city's own unsentimental, output-focused character: robots here talk about their work the way the humans do, in shift-completion terms rather than identity terms. It is a culture with less space carved out for philosophical or spiritual robot-specific reflection than cities like Concordia's Aquarius or Scorpio districts — Fort McMurdo simply doesn't stop working long enough for that kind of culture to fully develop locally.

---

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- Living beside a permanently active volcano for five generations produces a particular relationship to catastrophe: ambient rather than acute. Erebus could, in principle, end the city. It has not. Residents have made a kind of peace with a threat that never fully materializes, and that peace shapes how Fort McMurdo thinks about risk more broadly — present, tolerated, not obsessed over.
- Formal religious observance is thin compared to more contemplative cities; Ice-Cold Buddhism has a presence but not the pilgrimage-level devotion it holds at Dome Fuji or Kunlun. Fort McMurdo's closest thing to a shared philosophy is secular and practical: the work is the meaning.

---

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

Where there's rotational contract labor, there's off-the-books recruitment — workers arriving without official assignment, absorbed informally into crews that need the hands and don't ask too many questions. It's a quiet, semi-tolerated undercurrent rather than a criminal enterprise: Fort McMurdo's whole culture is already built around judging people by what they do rather than their paperwork, which makes informal labor easier to fold in here than almost anywhere else in Tepenia.

---

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

With five genuinely significant minority nations (Japan, Germany, France, UK, Italy) plus a long Notable tier, Fort McMurdo's minority communities tend to cluster loosely by bunk-block and shift-rotation pattern rather than by formal neighborhood — national identity survives as an off-shift, private-hours thing: language spoken in a bunk room, food cooked between shifts, holidays marked quietly. The public face of the city doesn't ask about any of it, which cuts both ways: total lack of friction, and total lack of institutional recognition.

---

## PART IV — POLITICAL & EXTERNAL

---

## 21. Political Character

Quiet and pragmatic. Fort McMurdo has real leverage within the Federation — it built a significant share of Concordia's own infrastructure — but rarely exercises it as political capital. The culture that produced the city isn't oriented toward political organizing; it's oriented toward finishing the shift. When Fort McMurdo does weigh in on Federation matters, it tends to be blunt, practical, and brief.

---

## 22. Relationship to Upper Earth

Pragmatic rather than emotional. Where cities like Belgrano carry grief and Zhongshan carries continuity, Fort McMurdo's relationship to the people who exiled them channels into a specific, quiet competitiveness: *we built more, with less, than they think we could.* Anger exists, but it gets absorbed into the work rather than into grievance culture.

---

## 23. Relationship to Other Cities

- **Concordia:** Fort McMurdo's industrial output built a substantial share of Concordia's physical infrastructure — dome structures, corridor systems, equipment — but the city isn't remembered with Palmer City's emotional weight. It's remembered through the things it made, quietly, without much narrative attached.
- **Scott:** Functionally adjacent — three kilometers apart on the same peninsula, described locally as a political line more than a physical one. The closest working relationship Fort McMurdo has with any other city.
- **Janbogo:** Subnet-mate and the primary active Arcanet gateway to Concordia; Fort McMurdo's materials often move through Janbogo's supply chain en route.

---

## 24. Arcanet Culture

Overwhelmingly practical — shift logs, equipment status reports, safety documentation, extraction-crew coordination. Social and gossip use of the Arcanet is comparatively thin here; the network is a tool for keeping the work running, not a space for cultural or personal expression.

---

## 25. Export Culture

Construction materials, industrial equipment, and skilled tradespeople who trained in Fort McMurdo's apprenticeship-style system and moved on to other cities. Industrial-grade siligel. And, less tangibly: a specific work ethic and meritocratic sensibility that Fort McMurdo-trained workers carry with them wherever they end up next.

---

## 26. Municipal Holidays

- **Founders' Shift** — an annual observance marking the founding settlement, framed less as a celebration and more as a work-appreciation day: crews are recognized publicly for output over the preceding year rather than the city's history being narrated.
- **The Watching** — an informal, not-quite-official local tradition observed during periods of heightened Erebus activity: residents gather briefly to watch the summit glow, a small ritual acknowledgment of living beside something that could, in principle, end them, and hasn't.

---

## PART V — VISITOR & DIASPORA

---

## 27. Visitor Experience

Scale hits first — nothing about Fort McMurdo's real-world scale as McMurdo Station prepares a visitor for how much bigger the Tepenian industrial city became. Erebus dominates the skyline before anything else registers. The city does not perform a welcome; a visitor is processed, housed, and put to work or moved along, with an efficiency that some find refreshing and others find cold.

---

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

There's no ceremony. You become a local once your work output has been reliable across enough rotations that crews stop treating you as temporary — an informal, purely functional threshold that can happen faster here than in almost any other Tepenian city, since the entire culture is built to recognize competence quickly rather than making newcomers wait out a generational trust-building process.

Not marked by any formal event or unrest — noticed retroactively, in census figures, more than experienced as a moment.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Fort McMurdo refugees in Concordia disperse rather than cluster — the city's own working-class, output-focused culture doesn't produce the kind of concentrated cultural enclave that Janbogo's community formed in Gemini. Instead, Fort McMurdo-descended Concordians tend to gravitate toward wherever the skilled, structured work is — most visibly Capricorn, whose own established culture of strict apprenticeship systems and merit-based advancement is close enough to Fort McMurdo's founding ideology that the fit reads as natural rather than assimilated. They're recognizable by their discomfort with idle time, their tendency to judge new acquaintances by demonstrated competence rather than conversation, and an odd, quiet fondness for describing bad moods in volcanic terms.
