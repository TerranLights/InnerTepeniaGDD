# Janbogo — Cultural Spec Sheet

---

**Subnet:** Janbogo
**Status:** Damaged; partially operational — Concordia's primary surviving supply lifeline
**Census II Population:** 490,564 humans / 467,006 robots / 957,570 combined *(revised 2026-07-03 — reduced to 60% of its original figure as part of a deliberate population redistribution to Lazar; see `Official_Population_Census.md`. Formerly Tepenia's largest city by population; still substantial, now ranks roughly 8th.)*
**Location:** Terra Nova Bay, Ross Sea coast (~74°37'S, 164°13'E)

**One-liner:** *The city that kept a Korean name and a Korean warmth long after the Korean people who founded it became a minority within it.*

## PART I — FOUNDATIONS

---

## 1. National/Ethnic Composition

| Tier | Nations |
|------|---------|
| Primary | China, USA |
| Significant | Japan, Germany, Italy *(operator-adjacent heritage — shared Terra Nova Bay proximity to Zukelli)*, South Korea *(founding operator)*, Canada, Indonesia, Australia *(founding wave)* |
| Notable | Philippines, Malaysia, New Zealand *(earliest founding wave)*, Chile |

*Janbogo is Tepenia's clearest case of a founding national identity surviving its own demographic minority status. Unified Korea founded the city, gave it its name, and set its entire cultural template — teahouses, communal warmth, the instinct to make daily life worth living under difficult conditions. But over 130 years, the sheer scale of Chinese and American emigration potential outweighed Korea's smaller founding population, and China and USA became the numerically dominant lineages. The culture did not follow the numbers. It stayed Korean-coded. People whose ancestry is Chinese, American, Japanese, German — the demographic majority of the city — grew up inside a civic identity that was never theirs by blood and is now inarguably theirs by practice.*

---

## 2. Founding Story

The real Jang Bogo Station was Unified Korea's Antarctic presence, established well before the exile era. When Falkland Treaty exiles reached Terra Nova Bay, they built on existing infrastructure rather than starting from nothing — a practical head start that let the founding Korean population set the city's character before any other group arrived in numbers large enough to contest it.

The phonetic drift from *Jang Bogo* to *Janbogo* was not an erasure. It was the opposite: a name worn down by daily use into something that belonged to everyone who said it, without ever stopping being Korean. Janbogo kept what it was without needing to perform it — a phrase that would come to describe the entire city, not just its name.

The founding Korean population built the teahouse tradition almost immediately, transplanting a domestic and social institution into an environment that needed exactly what it offered: a reason to gather, warmth that had to be built rather than assumed, care expressed through the ritual of hosting rather than through words. This was the template. Every later arrival — Chinese, American, Japanese, German, Italian, Southeast Asian — arrived into a city where the answer to "how do we survive this place together" had already been worked out, and it looked like a teahouse.

By the time Chinese and American emigration reached the scale that would make them the long-run demographic majority, Janbogo's civic culture was no longer something the Korean founders had to defend. It was simply how the city worked. Newcomers did not assimilate into a numerical majority — for most of the city's history, there wasn't one. They assimilated into an *institution*.

---

## 3. Climate Character

Janbogo's defining climate feature is not cold — it's wind. Katabatic winds, gravity-driven air pouring off the East Antarctic interior ice sheet, make Terra Nova Bay one of the windiest locations on the entire Ross Sea coast, with storms regularly reaching 60–100+ km/h and extreme events beyond 150 km/h. The wind warms adiabatically as it descends, arriving drier and relatively less cold than ambient air — a cruel trick, since wind chill at even moderate katabatic speeds still creates lethal outdoor conditions. Janbogo's relationship with the outdoors is not "it's cold out there." It's "the air itself is trying to kill you, and it isn't even that cold."

This produces a specific psychology: the sharp, immediate line between exterior danger and interior sanctuary. Unlike Concordia, where cold is a constant, ambient pressure, Janbogo's threat is episodic and violent — a katabatic event can turn a manageable walk into an emergency in minutes. The result is a culture that takes shelter seriously not as comfort but as the precondition for being alive at all. The teahouse tradition is, at its root, a response to this: if the outside world is going to try to kill you without warning, the inside world had better be worth surviving for.

The Terra Nova Bay polynya — a persistent open-water area the katabatics themselves maintain by blowing sea ice clear — gives Janbogo something almost no other coastal Tepenian city has: genuine year-round maritime access. The same wind that makes the surface lethal is the reason ships can reach Janbogo in the dead of winter when every other coastal city is locked in ice. The city's relationship to its own weather is accordingly complicated — the wind is an enemy and also, structurally, the reason Janbogo matters as much as it does.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

Polar night runs roughly May 20 to July 23 — about 64 days, shorter than Concordia's 82, but coinciding with the year's most intense katabatic season. This is Janbogo's hardest stretch: total darkness layered on top of the worst wind of the year. The teahouses fill. Outdoor life effectively stops except for what the supply route to Concordia demands, and even that runs on tight, wind-monitored windows.

Midnight sun (mid-November to late January) is the city's productive, outward-facing season — the polynya at its most navigable, Hwy 183 traffic at its heaviest, the waterfront genuinely pleasant by Antarctic standards. Janbogo does not slow down for summer the way some cities do; it speeds up, because the weather finally allows it to.

The transition seasons are when the katabatics are most unpredictable — building through autumn, tapering through spring — and Janbogo's famous wind-warning culture is busiest here. Longtime residents read the sky and the specific quality of the light off the interior plateau with real expertise; knowing when a katabatic is building is a survival skill so ordinary that visitors mistake it for small talk.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *Borrowed Warmth, Owned Completely* — colloquially, *the Teahouse City*

Janbogo's culture is built on an inversion most cities never have to reckon with: the people who founded it are no longer most of the people who live there, and the culture never noticed the difference. Korean communal warmth — the teahouse as social infrastructure, hospitality as the highest form of care, the instinct to make ordinary life worth living under hard conditions — was so completely internalized by every wave of arrivals that it stopped being "Korean culture practiced by non-Koreans" and became simply "Janbogo." A fourth-generation Chinese-descended Janbogo resident does not feel like a guest in someone else's tradition. They feel like they're home, because they are.

This is the opposite of Zhongshan's relationship to its own demography, where the founding nation and the long-run majority are the same and the tension is about *other* people's private heritage surviving under a stable dominant culture. In Janbogo, the dominant culture's *originators* are the minority, and the majority has no separate "public face" to maintain — Korean-coded warmth culture simply is the public face, for everyone, regardless of ancestry.

- **a. Founding tension** — Korean identity is Janbogo's origin and template, but not its numbers. The tension isn't Koreans defending their culture from dilution — it's the reverse: descendants of the Korean founding population are keenly aware that the institution they originated is now carried forward mostly by people who aren't Korean at all, and there's a quiet, rarely-voiced question running underneath the city's warmth: *does it still count as ours if almost none of us are left to keep it?* The teahouse tradition answers this by simply continuing, unbothered by the question — which some Korean-descended residents find comforting and others find a little bit like grief.

- **b. Fault lines**
  - The most visible fault line is invisible to outsiders: within Janbogo, there's a quiet, informal distinction between "old teahouse families" (regardless of ethnicity, families whose hosting lineage goes back to the founding generation or close to it) and everyone else. It has nothing to do with blood and everything to do with how many generations your family has been *making* the warmth rather than just receiving it.
  - The Sejong question sits under the surface for Korean-descended residents specifically: Sejong, the other Korean-founded Tepenian city, exists on the far side of the continent, and what relationship — if any — the two communities maintained before the Planetary Split Brain severed easy contact is genuinely unresolved. Some Korean-descended Janbogo families treat this as an old wound; most others in the city have never thought about it at all.

---

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

- **a. Hosting is how you say things you don't say out loud.** Janbogo's central social norm: care, apology, gratitude, welcome, and grief are all expressed through hosting — making tea properly, making coffee properly, making sure someone isn't sitting alone. Words are secondary. If someone makes you tea unprompted after a hard day, that is the conversation. You don't need another one.

- **b. Hospitality character** — Warm, immediate, and physically expressed. Unlike Zhongshan's ceremony-without-warmth, Janbogo hospitality is unguarded: you are pulled in, seated, given something warm in your hands within minutes of arriving anywhere. This is not performance; it is muscle memory built by generations who learned that the alternative to warmth, in this specific climate, was death.

- **c. Time moves for the weather, not the clock.** Janbogo's pace is genuinely dictated by katabatic conditions — plans bend around wind windows without apology or explanation needed. A meeting that doesn't happen because of a wind warning is not a broken commitment; it's simply what the day required.

- **d. Public/private divide** — Comparatively thin. Janbogo's warmth culture extends deep into what other cities would consider private — neighbors check on neighbors, teahouse keepers know regulars' troubles, community involvement in individual hardship is expected rather than intrusive. This is not gossip culture; it's care culture, and the city genuinely does not experience the two as the same thing.

- **e. Privacy as civic value** — Present but narrow: financial and political matters are kept close, but personal wellbeing is treated as a shared civic concern rather than an individual's private business. The Janbogo Arcanet subnet reflects this — heavily used for practical coordination (supply routes, wind warnings, community check-ins), lightly used for anything resembling gossip, because gossip has less function in a culture that already checks on people directly.

---

## 7. Who This City Attracts

- **The type this city draws:** People who need to be looked after and are tired of pretending they don't. People who find warmth-through-action easier to receive than warmth-through-words. Supply workers, sailors, and traders drawn by the polynya's year-round access. People running from cities where nobody noticed they were struggling.

- **The type this city repels:** People who need formal privacy and find constant communal attention smothering rather than caring. People who process life through solitude rather than company. Anyone whose instinct is to disappear when things are hard — Janbogo will not let you.

- **Case study — Majyao Bisyugota:** Ran the single most famous teahouse in Tepenia, out of Janbogo, for years before relocating to Concordia. She is Chinese-descended, not Korean — her aesthetic is explicitly Chinese (qipao, Chinese lanterns, a musical sensibility built on Chinese traditional folk fused with Scandinavian avant-garde jazz), and her establishment served coffee alongside tea, not tea alone. She is, in one person, the entire thesis of Janbogo's culture: the demographic majority made itself the custodian of a founding tradition that was never its own by blood, and did it so completely that "Majyao's teahouse" became the nationally recognized symbol of *Korean-founded* Janbogo warmth. Nobody in Tepenia who knew the teahouse thought to question that its keeper wasn't Korean. That is exactly how thoroughly the culture transcended its founding ethnicity.

---

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** Korean persists as a heritage and ceremonial language, spoken fluently within old teahouse families and used in specific hosting rituals regardless of the host's ancestry. Mandarin and English (American-inflected) are the most widely spoken daily languages, reflecting the long-run demographic majority. Japanese, German, and Indonesian are meaningfully present within their communities.

- **b. Linguistic character:** Janbogo speech is warm and direct, without Zhongshan's deliberate slowness or performative ceremony. Korean hosting phrases — untranslated, understood by everyone regardless of their own ancestry — are woven into everyday speech the way loanwords settle into any living language: not as foreign insertions but as simply the correct word for the situation. A Chinese-descended fourth-generation Janbogo resident uses these phrases with the same unselfconsciousness as a Korean-descended one.

- **c. Slang / dialect markers:** Certain hosting phrases (offering tea, welcoming someone in from the cold, checking on someone's wellbeing) survive in their original Korean form across the entire population regardless of home language — a linguistic fossil of the founding culture embedded in everyone's daily speech, whether or not they could hold a conversation in Korean beyond that.

- **d. Language and integration:** Extremely low pressure. Multilingual code-switching is the norm rather than the exception, given how thoroughly mixed the population is. What marks someone as a true Janbogo local isn't which language they speak — it's whether they know the hosting phrases and use them correctly.

---

## 9. Architecture

Janbogo's built environment answers directly to the katabatic wind: low profiles, wind-shedding angles, minimal exposed surface facing the prevailing SSW–SW flow, heavy structural icing resistance on anything that does face the bay. The city reads, from outside, as defensive and practical — a place built by people who took the wind seriously from the first structure onward.

Inside, the character inverts completely. Teahouses and communal interior spaces are where Janbogo's aesthetic investment concentrates: warm lighting, textiles, the deliberate softening of every hard edge the exterior architecture had to accept. Korean domestic design principles — low seating, warm wood tones where wood can be afforded, an emphasis on threshold spaces that transition gradually from outside to in rather than a single hard door — persist in teahouse design specifically, even in buildings built and run by non-Korean-descended families. The threshold matters more in Janbogo than almost anywhere else in Tepenia: the physical and emotional distance between "the wind that wants to kill you" and "the warmth that's waiting" is the entire architectural point.

---

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine:** Korean foodways form the base layer — communal stews, banchan-style shared small dishes, and above all tea culture, later joined by coffee culture as the population diversified. Chinese culinary traditions, reflecting the long-run demographic majority, sit alongside rather than displacing this — Janbogo's food culture is genuinely layered rather than fused into something new the way Zhongshan's dumpling culture became one thing. You can get a proper Korean-style shared meal and a proper Chinese one in the same neighborhood, sometimes the same establishment, and nobody considers this unusual.

- **b. Social context of eating:** Central to the city's entire social contract. Sharing food and drink is how Janbogo says the things it doesn't say in words — hosting is care, and every hosting act runs through food or drink. A Janbogo resident who has had a hard week will simply find themselves fed, without having to ask.

- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture:** Practical and warmth-oriented rather than ceremonial — Janbogo siligel culture emphasizes serving siligel warm and social, alongside human hosting rituals rather than separately from them. Robot coffee culture is especially strong here, given the city's dual tea-and-coffee tradition; Janbogo is known regionally for the quality and warmth-focus of its robot coffee service specifically.

---

## 11. Fashion

Practical, layered, wind-resistant — Janbogo clothing has to survive katabatic conditions, and the city's fashion sensibility treats function and warmth as inseparable from style rather than opposed to it. Korean-influenced layering principles (functional under-layers, a more expressive and warmer outer layer meant to be seen once you're inside) persist broadly. Interior wear, specifically teahouse and hosting attire, is where aesthetic expression concentrates — soft fabrics, warm colors, garments meant to be comfortable for long unhurried hours indoors. A Janbogo local's "good clothes" are almost always their indoor clothes, not their outdoor ones.

*(Established 2026-07-05, developer vision session — see `City_Vision_Notes/Janbogo.md`.)* This same insulated-but-vivid principle extends across the whole city, not just teahouses: fashionable residents fill Janbogo's huge shielded indoor commercial and social spaces in richly colored, insulated outfits — a genuine Chinese-French design fusion layered on top of the Korean foundation, fitting given China's own co-Primary demographic weight here. France itself has only a small, occasional resident presence — not enough to register in the formal composition tiers — but French design language is independently popular as a style choice, the way a fashion tradition can be widely admired and adopted without a large population behind it.

---

## 12. Music

Warm, communal, meant to be experienced together rather than performed at a distance — folk traditions across the city's many communities (Korean, Chinese, Japanese, Indonesian, and others) are kept alive specifically as teahouse and hosting music, genres meant to accompany gathering rather than demand attention. Fusion is common and unforced: Majyao Bisyugota's own musical signature — Chinese traditional folk fused with Scandinavian avant-garde jazz — is a genuinely Janbogo phenomenon, sophisticated without losing its rootedness, experimental without abandoning warmth. The city produces music built to be played while people are talking to each other, not instead of it.

---

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** Domestic and craft-oriented rather than gallery-oriented — textiles, ceramics (particularly teaware), and interior design are where Janbogo's visual culture concentrates its seriousness, consistent with a city whose aesthetic center of gravity is the inside of a teahouse rather than a museum wall.

- **Literature:** A strong oral and anecdotal tradition — stories about specific teahouses, specific hosts, specific acts of unexpected kindness during hard winters — carried forward more through speech and community memory than published work. What written literature exists tends toward the personal and warm rather than the abstract.

- **Crafts & material culture:** Teaware and coffee service craft is taken seriously as a genuine artisan tradition — a good Janbogo teapot or coffee set is a respected, valuable object, and the craftspeople who make them are recognized figures within their neighborhoods.

---

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** The wind, constantly, as background — a near-permanent low roar during katabatic season that residents stop consciously hearing. Inside, low conversation, the specific sounds of tea and coffee preparation, warm ambient music.

- **Smell:** Brewing tea and coffee everywhere, layered with the cold mineral smell of the bay and sea spray; woodsmoke or heating elements in older teahouse buildings; the specific ozone-and-ice smell that follows a katabatic event once it passes.

- **Feel:** A city defined by contrast — the physical violence of the exterior air against the deliberate, almost exaggerated softness of interior spaces. Moving through Janbogo means constantly crossing that threshold, and the crossing itself becomes a felt part of daily life.

- **First impressions for visitors:** The wind, immediately and unmistakably. Then, within minutes of stepping indoors anywhere, being handed something warm without having asked for it.

## PART III — SOCIAL STRUCTURE

## 15. Division of Industry

- Commercial / logistics: ~30% — the port, the polynya, and Hwy 183 make Janbogo a supply and trade hub first
- Marine / resource extraction: ~20% — fishing and bay-based industry, enabled by year-round polynya access
- Industrial / manufacturing: ~15%
- Technical / scientific: ~10%
- Hospitality / communal services: ~15% — an unusually large sector for any Tepenian city, reflecting the centrality of teahouses and hosting institutions to the local economy, not just the culture
- Education: ~5%
- Other: ~5%

---

## 16. Human-Robot Relations

Janbogo's founding population — Korean robot exiles and their human partners and supporters — built the city's warmth culture together from the start, and the hosting tradition was never ethnically or species-exclusive: a robot making tea for a human, or a human making coffee for a robot, carries exactly the same social weight. The city's inversion of founding-versus-demographic-majority applies to species dynamics too, in a sense — robots were fully present at the founding, and the culture that emerged treats care and hosting as a shared responsibility regardless of who or what is doing the hosting.

130 years on, human-robot relations in Janbogo are unusually unremarkable in the best sense: nobody treats it as a subject requiring commentary, because the founding culture never made a distinction worth commenting on.

---

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

Robot residents gravitate toward the hospitality and logistics sectors that define the city's economy — teahouse keeping, coffee service, and the supply/shipping work tied to the polynya and Hwy 183. The warmth-through-action ethos suits robot residents particularly well: care expressed through competent, attentive service rather than through emotionally demonstrative language maps naturally onto how many robots already prefer to express solidarity and affection.

Robot philosophical life leans practical and relational rather than abstract — less drawn to Zhongshan-style contemplative simulation theory, more oriented toward philosophies of service, care, and communal responsibility. Ice-Cold Buddhism has some presence but is a minority current here; Janbogo's spiritual and philosophical center of gravity is warmer and more relational than the coldest-and-calmest traditions.

---

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- Korean-derived communal and Confucian-adjacent ethics of hospitality and mutual obligation form the deep philosophical substrate, practiced by residents regardless of their own ancestry
- Buddhist and Christian traditions persist within specific heritage communities (Korean, Filipino, and others) without dominating the city's overall character
- A secular ethic of "warmth as the correct response to a hostile environment" functions as something close to an unstated civic philosophy — not formalized as doctrine, but close to universally held
- Organized robot philosophy is present but secondary to the city's practical, relational spiritual center of gravity

---

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

Where Zhongshan's counterculture is loud and fast in opposition to austere formality, Janbogo's countercultural pressure runs differently: against the *demand* for constant communal warmth. A minority current — often among younger residents or newer arrivals from more private-culture cities — pushes back against the expectation that everyone participates in the hosting economy, seeking out or building spaces where being left alone doesn't require an explanation. This subculture isn't hostile to Janbogo's warmth; it's simply tired of always being on the receiving or giving end of it, and the mainstream culture, true to its own values, mostly just makes sure such people are still fed even if they're not chatted to.

---

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

The unusual thing about minority communities in Janbogo is that the numerical majority — Chinese and American-descended residents — are, in a cultural sense, the ones managing a private/public divide, not the Korean-descended minority. Publicly, everyone participates in Korean-rooted hosting culture. Privately, Chinese, American, Japanese, German, and other heritage communities maintain their own home traditions, languages, and specific foodways, layered underneath rather than displacing the shared civic warmth culture above.

The generational dynamic runs opposite to Zhongshan's slow-grief pattern: because the dominant public culture was never framed as "theirs" for the majority of residents to begin with, there's less sense of loss and more sense of genuine dual ownership — people feel Korean-coded warmth culture is authentically theirs *and* maintain their own heritage privately, without the two feeling like they're in tension the way Zhongshan's absorption dynamic does.

## PART IV — POLITICAL & EXTERNAL

---

## 21. Political Character

Practical and consensus-driven, oriented around keeping the supply lifeline to Concordia functioning rather than ideological contest. Janbogo's political energy goes into logistics, wind-safety coordination, and maintaining the polynya's strategic value — a city that has learned, through 130 years of being genuinely needed, that its leverage comes from function rather than rhetoric.

---

## 22. Relationship to Upper Earth

Complicated by a layered history rather than a single clean grievance. The founding Korean exile population carries the specific weight of Unified Korea's role in the anti-robot sentiment that produced the Falkland Treaty exile. Later Chinese and American-descended residents carry their own, separate relationships to whatever drove their ancestors' emigration. The city's dominant public attitude is less about Upper Earth directly and more about defiant continuation — the teahouse tradition, deliberately kept alive and deliberately shared regardless of ancestry, reads as an implicit answer to exile: *you tried to erase where we came from; we made it something everyone wanted to keep.*

---

## 23. Relationship to Other Cities

- **Zukelli (destroyed):** Visible from Janbogo's waterfront, ~8km north *(corrected 2026-07-03 from ~10km)* — the only surviving Tepenian city within sight of a destroyed one. This is not abstract history for Janbogo residents; it is a view. Some Italian-heritage presence in Janbogo's Significant tier likely reflects generations of ordinary Terra Nova Bay neighborly settlement rather than wartime flight — Zukelli was near its peak population when destroyed, not a city that emptied out beforehand. The grief Janbogo carries about Zukelli is closer to survivor's proximity than displaced-population guilt.
- **Sejong:** The other Korean-founded Tepenian city, on the opposite side of the continent in the Palmer subnet. Whether the two communities maintained real contact before the Planetary Split Brain, and what that means for Korean-descended families in each city today, is unresolved — an open thread both cities' files flag independently.
- **Concordia:** The most direct and load-bearing relationship of any city in Tepenia — Janbogo is Concordia's last actively functioning coastal supply link, and the Gemini district's Janbogo diaspora is Concordia's largest, most established non-native community.
- **Fort McMurdo, Scott, Dumont d'Urville, Cape Adare:** Fellow Janbogo subnet cities along Hwy 183's route; day-to-day trade and logistics partners rather than culturally distinct rivals.

## 24. Arcanet Culture

Practically oriented — the Janbogo subnet Arcanet is used heavily for wind warnings, supply coordination, and community check-ins, and comparatively lightly for social broadcasting or archival work in the way Zhongshan's is. This is consistent with a culture that handles care and community awareness through direct, in-person hosting rather than mediated communication. Notably, the subnet's physical nexus hardware sits inside Concordia's Gemini district rather than in Janbogo itself — a still-unresolved arrangement (political deal, wartime relocation, or diaspora identity anchor) that the city's own residents have their own folk theories about, none confirmed.

## 25. Export Culture

- Hosting culture itself — Janbogo-trained or Janbogo-influenced teahouse and hospitality keepers are recognizable and respected across Tepenia, Majyao Bisyugota being the most famous example
- Coffee and tea service traditions, exported wherever Janbogo diaspora settle
- Supply logistics expertise, tied to generations of polynya and Hwy 183 operations
- Fusion folk-and-jazz musical sensibilities
- A specific *type of person* — warm, attentive, uncomfortable letting someone else sit alone; recognizable in Concordia's Gemini district and beyond

## PART V — VISITOR & DIASPORA

---

## 27. Visitor Experience

The wind arrives first — sudden, loud, and immediately explaining why every door in the city seems built like an airlock. Then, within minutes of stepping into any interior space, a visitor is handed something warm without having asked, by someone who has no particular reason to know them. This is disorienting for visitors from more private-culture cities and immediately comforting for almost everyone else. The city does not perform its warmth for visitors specifically — it treats them exactly the way it treats everyone, which is itself the welcome.

---

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

There's no formal ceremony, but the marker is specific and well understood: you become a Janbogo resident the moment you start hosting someone else rather than only being hosted. The culture runs on reciprocal, distributed care, and participating fully means becoming a source of warmth, not just a recipient of it. People describe the shift as the first time they made tea for a stranger without being asked to — and realized it felt entirely natural.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Janbogo people in Concordia are the warmest presence in the room, and usually the first to notice if someone else in it isn't doing well. They carry the hosting instinct with them regardless of what their own ancestry is — a Chinese-descended Janbogo native in Concordia will make tea for a stranger with exactly the same unselfconsciousness a Korean-descended one would. This confuses people who don't know Janbogo's history and assume warmth-culture must track ethnicity; Gemini district residents mostly find this assumption mildly funny.

The Gemini district enclave is the largest, most established non-native community in Concordia, organized in part around the physical presence of the Janbogo Arcanet subnet nexus itself — an unusual arrangement that gives the diaspora a literal piece of home infrastructure inside the capital. For Gemini residents, Janbogo's ongoing damaged-but-functioning status is a specific, present-tense worry rather than settled grief: their supply lifeline back home is also everyone's supply lifeline, and its fragility is felt doubly.

Majyao Bisyugota's Concordia teahouse is the diaspora's most visible cultural anchor — proof, replicated in the capital, that the tradition travels intact regardless of who's carrying it.
