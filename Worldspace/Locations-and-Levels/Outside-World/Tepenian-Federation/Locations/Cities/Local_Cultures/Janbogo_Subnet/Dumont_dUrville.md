# Dumont d'Urville — Cultural Spec Sheet

---

**Subnet:** Janbogo
**Status:** Damaged; partially operational — a small island city living alongside both its own wreckage and the Adélie penguin colonies that never left
**Census II Population:** 134,634 humans / 177,372 robots / 312,006 combined *(rank 26th of ~32, corrected 2026-07-17 — was "25th," stale against the current Official_Population_Census.md Census II ranking table; "island cap," Petrel Island's small footprint limits growth regardless of the city's importance)*
**Location:** Petrel Island, Géologie Archipelago, Adélie Land (~66°40'S, 140°01'E)

**One-liner:** *The only Tepenian city that never had the wind or the penguins to itself — a small francophone island where neither could be conquered, only negotiated with, generation after generation.*

## PART I — FOUNDATIONS

---

## 1. National/Ethnic Composition

| Tier | Nations |
|------|---------|
| Primary | China, USA |
| Significant | Japan, France *(founding operator heritage)*, South Korea, Indonesia, Australia *(founding wave)* |
| Notable | Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines, Malaysia, New Zealand |

*France founded and named the city and gave it St. Jules — and by Census II, French heritage sits at Significant tier, well below the China/USA Primary pairing that emerged over generations of arrivals. The city's own language, food, and aesthetic sensibility developed on-site out of that founding population's daily circumstances rather than being simply carried over intact. The shape of this founding-tier tension is close to Fort McMurdo's (founding nation retained institutionally, demographic majority arrived later) but plays out at a much smaller, more intimate scale: a city of ~312,000 rather than Fort McMurdo's industrial sprawl, an island rather than a peninsula, a population where robots (177,372) meaningfully outnumber humans (134,634) — Dumont d'Urville was never a place many humans chose to live if they had another option, given the wind.*

---

## 2. Founding Story

French exiles arrived to find a station that had occupied Petrel Island continuously since 1956 — a small, exposed, wind-scoured foothold in the Géologie Archipelago, chosen for the same reason the exile city kept it: the island's rock was buildable in a way the continental ice edge wasn't. The founders inherited both the physical station and a name that mattered enormously to them — Jules Dumont d'Urville, the officer who first set foot on this coast in 1840 and named the whole surrounding land after his wife. The city kept his name, and later gave him a Saint's honorific: St. Jules.

From the first years, two facts organized daily life more than any founding charter did: the wind, which arrived with a severity the founders had not fully anticipated even having read the historical station records, and the Adélie penguins, whose colonies occupied the archipelago in numbers no arriving population could have displaced even if they'd wanted to. The city that formed here did not found itself in opposition to its environment. It founded itself in the middle of one it would never fully own.

---

## 3. Climate Character

Dumont d'Urville's wind is, by the numbers, more severe than anywhere else documented in Tepenia — more extreme than Mirny's, more extreme even than Denison's storied katabatic legacy. Sustained severe wind events are frequent here, not exceptional, and the island position removes any terrain buffer the mainland might have offered. Temperatures are comparatively mild by Antarctic coastal standards (mean annual −11.0°C), which only sharpens the point: this city's defining hardship was never really the cold. It was always the wind.

The Géologie Archipelago's channels behave unpredictably depending on ice conditions, and the pack ice along the Adélie coast varies dramatically year to year — some years opening maritime access, others closing it almost entirely. Petrel Island itself is small enough that nowhere in the city is meaningfully sheltered from any of this.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

The polar night and midnight sun here are both brief — roughly ten and twelve days respectively, bracketing the solstices rather than stretching across months the way they do further south. What structures the year instead is wind intensity and penguin breeding season, two rhythms that don't align with each other and that residents have to track separately. Winter brings the worst sustained katabatic events and closes maritime access to whatever the pack ice allows; it's also when the Adélie colonies are quietest. Summer opens the channels, brings the penguins back in overwhelming numbers for breeding season, and — since Dumont d'Urville established 2026-07-03 as the Dumont d'Urville Sea's own port — brings the Australian freighters in via the seasonal shipping window, staged through Hobart. The city's economic and ecological calendars run on top of each other without ever quite matching.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *Negotiated Ground*

Dumont d'Urville never developed a culture of conquering its environment, and it never developed Mirny's flavor of grim stoic endurance either. What emerged instead — across the wind, the penguins, the island's hard physical limits — was something closer to ongoing negotiation: an understanding that neither the wind nor the birds nor the sea were obstacles to be defeated, just permanent co-residents whose terms had to be lived with. The city's architecture, its economy, its daily rhythms are all built around accommodation rather than resistance.

- **a. Founding tension** — France founded, named, and set Dumont d'Urville's earliest civic institutions in place, but by Census II sits at Significant tier beneath a China/USA Primary pairing that arrived generations later, largely drawn by the practical opportunities of a coastal Janbogo subnet post rather than any attachment to St. Jules or Adélie Land specifically. Unlike Fort McMurdo's exact-parity resolution, here the founding nation never caught back up — the early civic template (its language, food, and aesthetic habits, independently grown on-site) persisted as the civic default anyway, the way Janbogo's own founding-era template did, simply because nothing displaced it.
- **b. Fault lines** — A small city with a robot-majority population and a founding culture that's demographically thin creates a quiet tension between who *runs* daily civic life (still disproportionately French-heritage, by institutional inertia) and who actually *lives* it day to day (a China/USA-majority, heavily robot population dealing directly with the wind and the birds). It rarely erupts into anything, mostly because there's no surplus energy in a city this exposed for internal conflict.

---

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

- **a.** You don't fight the wind or the penguins; you plan around both. Anyone who arrives determined to "tough it out" through pure willpower is gently, repeatedly corrected until they adapt or leave.
- **b. Hospitality character** — Warm but practical: a guest is fed properly and given real shelter, but the city has no patience for anyone who treats a visit here as an adventure rather than a serious commitment to a serious place.
- **c.** Paced by weather windows more than by clocks — plans are made provisionally, with wind and ice conditions as the real scheduling authority.
- **d. Public/private divide** — Physical survival information (current wind conditions, ice state, colony locations) is entirely public and constantly shared; personal and national background is comparatively private, a small-city discretion more than a policy.
- **e. Privacy as civic value** — Low population, high interdependence — everyone roughly knows everyone, but there's a specific etiquette around not commenting on how someone's coping with the wind, which residents consider a deeply personal struggle even in a city this small.

---

## 7. Who This City Attracts

- **The type this city draws:** people who want a small, specific, demanding place rather than a large anonymous one — naturalists, wind engineers, people with a particular fascination for extremity, robots for whom the wind poses less existential risk than it does for humans.
- **The type this city repels:** anyone seeking comfort, size, or a city that will ever stop demanding active attention from its residents.

---

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** French remains the civic default despite France's minority demographic standing, alongside Mandarin and English reflecting the China/USA majority.
- **b. Linguistic character** — The most distinctively francophone city in Tepenia; even residents with no French ancestry tend to pick up functional French simply through years of civic life conducted in it.
- **c. Slang / dialect markers** — Wind conditions get their own compact vocabulary, French-rooted, borrowed into every other language spoken here — a shorthand for describing exactly how bad conditions are that outsiders find bewilderingly precise.
- **d. Language and integration** — Low-pressure integration in daily terms (the wind doesn't care what language you speak), but genuine civic participation still runs through French, a soft but real barrier for newer arrivals.

---

## 9. Architecture

Low, anchored, and oriented — nothing here is built without accounting for wind loading first and aesthetics a distant second. What aesthetic sensibility survives shows up in proportion and small deliberate details, developed out of the city's own severe constraints, rather than any grand statement architecture; the city could never afford architectural ambition against conditions this severe. In damage, the pattern holds: repaired sections are rebuilt low and anchored exactly like the originals, while abandoned sections are left to the wind and, increasingly, to nesting penguins.

**Developer vision, established 2026-07-05 — a tight, bustling, spatially stratified city:** Dumont d'Urville's downtown core, on Petrel Island proper, is dense and genuinely bustling — and expensive; real estate here commands the city's highest prices, the reward for being inside the sheltered, walkable heart of the settlement rather than out past the bridge. Between the downtown buildings, dedicated heating units keep the spaces between structures genuinely livable against the wind, not just the interiors — a step beyond most Tepenian cities' purely interior-focused heating. The bridge to the mainland (see Geographic Basis in `Specs/Dumont_dUrville.md` for the engineering questions still open around whether it's permanent or seasonal) is the island's most prominent feature outside the dense downtown itself, and the city's spatial economics run outward from it: downtown is expensive and central, the far side of the bridge is cheaper residential territory, and further out still, the bridge connects to the highway network proper.

---

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine** — A simple, deliberate, technique-forward cuisine, developed independently out of extreme scarcity and extreme wind, that makes the most of limited, often penguin-egg-supplemented ingredients. *(It happens to echo French culinary technique's own emphasis on precision under constraint — a coincidence of convergent development, not an inheritance.)*
- **b. Social context of eating** — Meals are one of the few genuinely sheltered, unhurried social occasions in a city otherwise governed by weather; eating together carries real weight here specifically because so little else allows people to simply sit still.
- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture** — Practical and adapted to a robot-majority population; siligel production accounts for the wind's effect on outdoor equipment and material degradation more than most cities need to.

---

## 11. Fashion

Heavy, anchored, wind-first clothing design — a practical elegance the city developed on its own out of genuinely extreme conditions rather than aesthetics for their own sake. Layering here isn't optional or stylistic; it's load-bearing infrastructure, and residents take real pride in gear that's been properly, precisely adapted rather than just piled on.

---

## 12. Music

Small-city, intimate musical culture — quiet, small-gathering musical traditions, developed independently out of the population's small scale, persisting in private settings rather than any large performance industry the city is simply too small to support. *(It happens to echo the intimacy of French chanson tradition — a coincidence of convergent development, not an inheritance.)* The sound of the wind itself is, unavoidably, part of the city's actual sonic identity; some residents describe certain wind pitches as recognizable, almost musical, in their own right.

**Developer vision, established 2026-07-05 — "New Orleans at 1/20th scale":** this small-city intimacy took a specific, vivid form in the dense downtown core — live music inside almost every eatery, not as a special occasion but as an ordinary, expected feature of eating out. This isn't a contradiction of the population being "too small to support a large performance industry" above — it's the same intimacy expressed as density rather than absence: many small, packed, informal venues rather than one large one, exactly the texture real New Orleans is actually known for (a city of countless small clubs and bars with live music, not stadium shows). Downtown's bustling, expensive, tightly-packed character (see Architecture above) is inseparable from this — the eateries-with-stages culture is as much a spatial fact as a musical one.

---

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** A strong tradition of documentary and naturalist art centered on the penguin colonies — Dumont d'Urville has produced more wildlife-focused visual art, relative to its size, than any other Tepenian city, simply because the subject is inescapably present.
- **Literature:** Small but serious — journals and naturalist writing dominate over fiction, a city that writes down what it observes because the observing itself is most of what there is to record.
- **Crafts & material culture:** Precision wind-engineering solutions treated as a craft in their own right — a well-designed anchor point or windbreak is genuinely admired here the way other cities admire fine art.

---

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** Wind, constantly, at every intensity from background hum to genuinely dangerous roar; penguin colony noise layered underneath, year-round in breeding season.
- **Smell:** The distinct, inescapable smell of a large penguin colony — guano, salt, cold ocean — mixed with the ordinary industrial smells of a small damaged city.
- **Feel:** Being pushed, physically, by wind almost everywhere outdoors; the specific cold-plus-force sensation that no amount of insulation fully solves.
- **First impressions for visitors:** The wind, immediately and viscerally, followed almost as quickly by the penguins — visitors are rarely prepared for either, and even less prepared for how unbothered residents are by both.

## PART III — SOCIAL STRUCTURE

---

## 15. Division of Industry

- Marine / resource extraction: 25% — coastal and channel resources, penguin colony management
- Technical / scientific: 25% — wind engineering, structural maintenance against ongoing wind damage
- Commercial: 20% — port and shipping logistics (Australian freighter trade)
- Industrial / manufacturing: 15% — repair and maintenance-focused rather than large-scale production
- Education: 8%
- Other: 7%

---

## 16. Human-Robot Relations

With robots outnumbering humans by a meaningful margin, and with the wind posing genuinely different levels of physical risk to each, Dumont d'Urville's human-robot relations are shaped heavily by practical role distribution — robots handle a disproportionate share of the most exposed outdoor work, not from any formal hierarchy but from straightforward risk calculus that both populations accept without much friction. It produces a relationship that's less about philosophical equality debates and more about lived, practical interdependence under genuinely hostile conditions.

---

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

Robot culture here is defined substantially by the wind-and-penguin environment itself — a population that spends more time than most directly negotiating with extreme physical conditions and, unusually, with a large non-human animal population. There's a specific local robot tradition of quiet, careful naturalist observation of the penguin colonies that has no real equivalent elsewhere in Tepenia; some residents describe it as the closest thing the city has to a shared contemplative practice.

---

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- St. Jules veneration remains genuinely felt despite France's minority demographic standing — Dumont d'Urville's civic identity was built around him early enough, and thoroughly enough, that later arrivals absorbed the tradition rather than displacing it.
- A loosely shared local philosophy, more felt than formalized, treats the wind and the penguins as evidence that coexistence rather than dominance is the only workable relationship with a hostile environment — an ethic that colors how residents talk about nearly everything else, including their own damaged, post-war city.

---

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

Minimal by necessity — a city this small, this exposed, and this population-constrained doesn't have much surplus capacity for a genuine counterculture to develop. What exists is closer to individual eccentricity (residents who take the wind-negotiation ethic to unusual personal extremes) than any organized subculture.

---

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

The Significant and Notable tiers (Japan, South Korea, Indonesia, Australia, and the Notable-tier nations) maintain identity mostly through small, private, off-duty gestures — a city this size and this demanding doesn't leave much room for elaborate cultural infrastructure beyond the French civic default and the China/USA demographic majority. What persists tends to persist quietly, within households and small gatherings, rather than publicly.

---

## PART IV — POLITICAL & EXTERNAL

---

## 21. Political Character

Quiet, practical, and low-profile within Federation politics — a small city with genuinely limited capacity for political projection beyond its own survival. What political energy exists is directed almost entirely inward, toward maintaining the delicate accommodations (with the wind, with the sea, with its own damage) that keep the city functioning at all.

---

## 22. Relationship to Upper Earth

Distant and secondary to the city's own daily survival concerns — a small city whose civic energy is almost entirely consumed by the wind, the sea, and its own damage, leaving comparatively little bandwidth for Upper Earth grievance. The dominant feeling is less anger than a kind of weathered distance, consistent with a city that has spent its whole existence negotiating with forces bigger than itself rather than fighting them.

---

## 23. Relationship to Other Cities

- **Casey:** Connected via Hwy 2 (the Dumont Coast Highway); part of the same coastal supply and refugee-flow corridor.
- **Denison:** Geographic and thematic neighbor along the Dumont d'Urville Sea coast — both extreme-wind cities, though Denison's relationship to its wind (pride, mythology, "Home of the Blizzard") is markedly different from Dumont d'Urville's negotiated coexistence.
- **Concordia:** Indirect connection mediated through Janbogo's Arcanet infrastructure; a major refugee source for Concordia during the Long Night War, per established subnet lore.

---

## 24. Arcanet Culture

Thin and practical, consistent with the city's small population and demanding daily conditions — weather and ice condition updates dominate local Arcanet traffic, with little bandwidth left over for social or archival use beyond bare necessity.

---

## 25. Export Culture

- Wind-engineering expertise — Dumont d'Urville-trained structural and materials engineers are recognized elsewhere in Tepenia for genuinely unmatched practical experience with extreme wind loading
- Naturalist knowledge and documentation — the city's outsized tradition of penguin-colony observation and record-keeping
- A specific *type of person* — quietly unshakeable, accustomed to negotiating rather than fighting, recognizable by an almost meditative calm around conditions that would rattle most other Tepenians

---

## 26. Municipal Holidays

- **St. Jules Day (January 21)** — commemorating Dumont d'Urville's 1840 landing; the city's most significant civic observance, ceremonial despite the founding nation's demographic minority status
- **The Return** — an informal, weather-dependent observance marking the Adélie penguins' annual return to the colonies each breeding season; not a fixed date, but a genuinely anticipated, community-wide moment each year

---

## PART V — VISITOR & DIASPORA

---

## 27. Visitor Experience

The wind first, immediately and physically undeniable, followed by the penguins — visitors typically need real time to recalibrate to a city where both are simply permanent facts of life rather than emergencies. The scale is intimate compared to Fort McMurdo or Janbogo; a visitor can see most of Dumont d'Urville in a short walk, wind permitting.

---

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

Becoming a local here means internalizing the negotiation ethic — stopping trying to fight the wind and starting to plan around it, the same shift in mindset residents describe going through themselves. There's no formal ceremony, but longtime residents can tell almost immediately whether a newer arrival has made that shift or is still fighting a losing battle against conditions that don't yield to willpower.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Dumont d'Urville-descended Concordians are rare enough, given the city's small population, to be individually distinctive rather than forming any recognizable enclave — Pink Lucy herself is the clearest known example. What marks them, when they do appear, is exactly the negotiated-coexistence temperament the city cultivates: an unusual, almost unshakeable calm in genuinely difficult conditions, a practical rather than combative relationship to hardship, and — if the naturalist tradition holds — a tendency to notice and track environmental detail that other Concordians walk past without registering.
