## 2. Founding Story

Casey Station was Australia's largest and best-equipped Antarctic station before the exile era — modern infrastructure, substantial capacity, nothing like Denison's legendary-but-minimal heroic-age heritage. When the Falkland Treaty exiles arrived, they inherited a genuinely functional base and the comparatively straightforward task of turning a well-run research station into a city, rather than building civilization from scratch on a site whose only prior claim to fame was nearly killing an expedition.

Casey's coastal position on the Budd Coast, with reasonably sheltered anchorage by Antarctic standards, made it an accessible arrival point during the exile period — it received a wider, more mixed early wave of settlers than more geographically isolated founding sites, setting up the broad, evenly-spread composition tiers that persisted for generations.

The founding character that emerged wasn't built around surviving a legendary hazard (Denison's story) or around a moral compact (Esperanza's story) — it was built around function. Casey worked, from day one, because the infrastructure it inherited worked, and the culture that grew up around it inherited that same practical, unglamorous competence.

---

## 3. Climate Character

Casey sits just north of the Antarctic Circle — a genuine threshold position, with the sun rising every day of the year but winter days shrinking to barely five hours. Mean annual temperature around −9.3°C, with katabatic winds off the interior plateau combining with coastal storm systems for a climate that's real but, critically, "less extreme than Janbogo" by its own established character — and dramatically less extreme than Denison's near-constant gale-force reality.

This comparative moderation matters for Casey's whole civic identity: the climate is a manageable operational fact, not a defining hardship to be mythologized. Where Denison built pride around its wind, Casey simply built around its weather the way any competent city builds around whatever conditions it has — without turning it into the point.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

The Antarctic Circle threshold position gives Casey a genuinely liminal winter light quality — never full polar night, never quite full daylight, low-angle sun tracking the horizon for a few hours around the solstice. Summer brings nearly nineteen hours of daylight and, critically, the navigable shipping season (roughly November–March) that determined the city's supply windows and its role as a coastal arrival/departure point.

Seasonal rhythm here is organized around maritime access more than wind-window logistics (Denison's pattern) — ships arrive and depart on a genuine seasonal cycle, and Casey's role as a junction city means its rhythm is tied as much to travel patterns along Hwy 110 and Hwy 2 as to the weather itself.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *The Threshold City* — colloquially, *the Last Stop* (in both directions)

Casey's culture is defined by transit, not by hardship or mission or heritage. Sitting at the junction of Hwy 110 (the inland route to Concordia) and Hwy 2/DCH (the coastal route to Dumont d'Urville), Casey was the city everyone passed through — refugees, supply convoys, ordinary travelers — moving in either direction. It never celebrated itself the way Palmer City did, never mythologized its environment the way Denison did. It served a function and did it well, and that function shaped everything about how the city actually felt to live in.

- **a. Founding tension** — Not founding-nation-versus-demography (the tiers are broad and evenly spread, no single nation dominant enough for that dynamic) and not mythology-versus-mundanity the way Denison's is. Casey's real tension is between being a *destination* and being a *waypoint* — a city genuinely lived in by hundreds of thousands of people, with its own full civic life, that is nonetheless known across Tepenia primarily as the place you pass through on your way somewhere else. Residents live full lives in a city whose external reputation reduces it to a highway junction.
- **b. Fault lines**
  - Between residents who embrace the transit identity (pride in being useful, in knowing the road, in the specific hospitality of a good layover) and those who resent being defined by what the city does for other people's journeys rather than what it is on its own terms.
  - Between the pragmatic, function-first civic culture and the quieter, less externally visible full community life that exists underneath the "junction city" reputation — Casey has its own culture, its own institutions, its own texture, that the constant flow of travelers rarely stops long enough to notice.

---

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

- **a. Be useful, be quick, be honest about the route.** Casey's cardinal social value: information about road conditions, weather, and safety is shared immediately and accurately, because travelers' lives depend on it. Withholding or fudging travel information is a serious social breach.
- **b. Hospitality character** — Efficient and warm without ceremony — a good Casey welcome gets a traveler fed, rested, informed, and back on the road with what they need, fast. Not cold, but not lingering either; the city understands most guests need to keep moving.
- **c. Time is dictated by the road and the sea ice cycle** — Practical and schedule-driven, more like a transit hub than a contemplative or family-paced city.
- **d. Public/private divide** — Standard and unremarkable; Casey's transit function means public civic life (the junction, the resupply economy) and private residential life are fairly cleanly separated.
- **e. Privacy as civic value** — Moderate; travelers passing through are given practical assistance without much personal inquiry, consistent with a culture used to serving people who are, by definition, moving on.

---

## 7. Who This City Attracts

- **The type this city draws:** People suited to logistics, hospitality, and information work — natural hosts to a constant stream of strangers. Also people who like the specific rhythm of a threshold life, always near the edge of departure or arrival.
- **The type this city repels:** People who want a city entirely defined by a strong singular cultural identity (Denison's mythologized extremity, Zhongshan's austere coherence) — Casey's identity is real but diffuse, organized around function rather than a strong unifying aesthetic or principle.

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** English and Mandarin (Primary tier), with strong Korean presence (leading Significant tier — corrected 2026-07-13, previously misstated as Japanese) alongside French, Australian-English, Russian, Japanese, and Indonesian.
- **b. Linguistic character:** Practical and efficient — Casey speech favors clarity and speed over ornamentation, shaped by a population constantly exchanging time-sensitive travel and safety information.
- **c. Slang / dialect markers:** Road and junction vocabulary bleeds into general speech — describing a decision as being "at the junction" (a choice point with no easy way to un-choose) is recognizably Casey phrasing.
- **d. Language and integration:** Low pressure — a city built on constant contact with travelers of every background has no patience for gatekeeping around accent or fluency.

---

## 9. Architecture

Built on substantial inherited modern Australian station infrastructure, expanded pragmatically around its junction function — waystations, resupply depots, maintenance facilities, and transit lodging dominate the built environment more than any strong residential or ceremonial architectural tradition. What aesthetic identity exists is functional-modern rather than heritage-driven, a contrast to Denison's blend of legendary-hut reverence and cutting-edge wind engineering.

The nearby Wilkes Station ruins — a genuine pre-exile American research site, abandoned decades before the Federation existed — sit as a curiosity rather than a civic landmark, a piece of history that predates Casey's own story rather than feeding into it.

---

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine:** A genuine Pacific-corridor fusion — Chinese, American, Japanese, French, Korean, Russian, Indonesian, and Southeast Asian influences, organized around fast, substantial, traveler-friendly food more than elaborate tradition. Casey's cuisine is judged by whether it properly refuels someone about to get back on the road.
- **b. Social context of eating:** Communal but efficient — shared meals at waystations and resupply points double as information exchange, travelers and locals trading news over food that's meant to be good but not lingered over.
- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture:** Practical and logistics-oriented — siligel resupply is a core part of Casey's junction economy, treated with the same efficient professionalism as any other transit good.

---

## 11. Fashion

Practical travel-and-transit gear dominates — layered, durable, built for people either about to travel or constantly serving those who are. Less a distinct aesthetic signature than a functional uniform shared across the city's transit economy; Casey clothing reads as competent rather than expressive.

---

## 12. Music

Broad, eclectic, shaped by constant exposure to travelers from every corner of Tepenia passing through — Casey's musical culture absorbs and remixes influences faster than more insular cities, without settling into one dominant tradition the way Zhongshan or Denison did. Music here functions as much as social lubricant for strangers as it does cultural expression.

**Developer vision, established 2026-07-05 — a genuine music scene, not just background noise.** Casey hosts a massive, genre-spanning music scene — Classic Rock, Jazz, Blues, and Acoustic Folk all thriving side by side, the concrete expression of the eclecticism already described above. Its center of gravity is **Splinters**, on the old peninsula — Tepenia's largest and most famous bar, a full city block, with a stage, a dance floor, and rows of seating along the side-flanks and rear. Splinters is famous not just in Casey but across cities along the Dumont d'Urville Sea and Indian Ocean coastlines — travelers passing through Casey (which is most of what Casey is for) carry its reputation onward. The name itself is a piece of rediscovered history: residents found records and entries referencing the real, historical "Splinters" — the actual social bar at the real pre-exile Casey Station — and revived the name for their own, far grander establishment.

---

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** Modest and functional; Casey has not produced a strong independent visual arts tradition, its creative energy going instead into the practical craft of running a junction city well.
- **Literature:** An oral tradition of road stories and travel accounts — Casey residents accumulate an unusually broad secondhand knowledge of the rest of Tepenia, simply from hearing travelers' stories over the years.
- **Crafts & material culture:** Practical transit-economy craft — resupply equipment, travel gear, waystation design — is where Casey's real craftsmanship concentrates.

---

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** The specific ambient noise of a junction — vehicles, loading and unloading, multiple languages overlapping — layered over a real but comparatively moderate wind presence.
- **Smell:** Diesel and equipment smell from the transit economy; salt air off Vincennes Bay; food smells from waystations, more varied than most single-culture-dominant cities.
- **Feel:** Liminal in the literal sense — a threshold city both geographically (Antarctic Circle) and functionally (junction of two highways), a place that always feels slightly in-between.
- **First impressions for visitors:** The junction itself, immediately legible even to newcomers — two roads meeting, a clear sense of a decision point. Then the specific, efficient warmth of a city that's hosted a lot of strangers and is good at it.

## PART III — SOCIAL STRUCTURE

---

## 15. Division of Industry

- Transit / logistics / resupply: ~30% — the dominant sector, reflecting the junction economy directly
- Commercial: ~20%
- Technical / scientific (inherited Australian Antarctic Division capacity): ~15%
- Maritime: ~15%
- Industrial / manufacturing: ~10%
- Other: ~10%

---

## 16. Human-Robot Relations

Built on the same shared-founding logic as most Tepenian cities, inflected by Casey's transit function: robots and humans work side by side across the resupply and hospitality economy with little functional distinction, since the junction's constant demand for competent, reliable service leaves little room for anything but practical cooperation. Casey's human-robot relations are unremarkable in the healthiest sense — nobody has the bandwidth, in a city this busy, to make an issue of it.

---

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

Robot residents concentrate in logistics, hospitality, and maintenance roles — practical service culture, similar in spirit to Janbogo's warmth-through-action but without the ceremonial weight; Casey's robots take pride in efficient competence rather than in warmth as an end in itself. Philosophically pragmatic, with little independent tradition beyond "do the job well."

---

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- No dominant organized tradition — Casey's broad, evenly-spread composition and constant travel-driven contact with outside influence keeps any single belief system from becoming civic default
- A loose, shared pragmatism ("the road doesn't wait for philosophy") functions as the closest thing to a unifying local ethic
- Heritage religious practice (across all represented nations) persists privately and without friction, given the city's low-pressure integration norms

---

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

Given how diffuse and function-driven the mainstream culture already is, Casey doesn't have a strong counterculture-versus-mainstream divide the way more culturally coherent cities (Zhongshan, Denison) do — if anything, the "counterculture" is simply whichever traveling influence is currently passing through in unusual concentration, absorbed and moved on from quickly.

---

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

With tiers this broadly spread, no single heritage community carries an outsized public/private burden — Japanese, French, Korean, Russian, Indonesian, Australian, and Southeast Asian communities all maintain private heritage practices comfortably, none of them positioned as "the" minority relative to an overwhelming majority the way some other cities' compositions create.

---

## PART IV — POLITICAL & EXTERNAL

---

## 21. Political Character

Practical and function-first, organized around keeping the junction operational — road maintenance, resupply logistics, and traveler safety dominate Casey's civic priorities over ideological contest. A city whose entire self-understanding is "we serve a function" doesn't generate much appetite for politics that don't bear on that function.

---

## 22. Relationship to Upper Earth

Comparatively muted — Casey's civic energy is absorbed by its logistics function rather than by grievance politics, and its constant exposure to travelers from every corner of Tepenia gives it a broader, less locally-fixated perspective on the Federation's situation than more insular cities.

---

## 23. Relationship to Other Cities

- **Denison (Janbogo subnet):** No established in-fiction relationship between the two cities. Noted here only because their founding-nation composition is nearly identical despite very different resulting cultures — a data point, not a canon connection. See `../Janbogo_Subnet/Denison.md`.
- **Concordia:** The inland terminus of Hwy 110; Casey is the last coastal city before the long interior drive, and the first city after it — a genuinely load-bearing relationship, not a symbolic one.
- **Dumont d'Urville coast cities:** Connected via Hwy 2/DCH — but that route runs directly through Casey, so its destruction blocks Hwy 2 at the source rather than merely disconnecting it *(corrected 2026-07-05)*. There is no rerouting through Janbogo or anywhere else; the Dumont d'Urville coast's overland connection to the rest of Tepenia is genuinely severed.
- **Zhongshan, Mirny, Davis:** Fellow Mirny subnet cities; Casey's junction role makes it a practical hub for the whole subnet's internal movement, distinct from Zhongshan's insular austerity or Mirny's subnet-hub administrative role.

---

## 24. Arcanet Culture

Practically oriented around road conditions, supply status, and travel coordination — Casey's Arcanet presence functions as a genuine logistics network, the digital equivalent of its physical junction role. Less socially or archivally focused than Zhongshan's, less hazard-monitoring-focused than Denison's — a distinct third specialization among the cities covered so far.

---

## 25. Export Culture

- Logistics and hospitality expertise — Casey-trained transit and resupply specialists are recognized across Tepenia for genuine competence at moving people and goods efficiently
- Broad, well-traveled cultural fluency — Casey residents who leave tend to be unusually comfortable navigating unfamiliar cities, a direct result of a lifetime around travelers
- A specific *type of person* — practical, efficient, warm without ceremony, comfortable with constant change and strangers

## PART V — VISITOR & DIASPORA

---

## 27. Visitor Experience

The junction itself registers first — an immediately legible sense of being at a decision point, two roads meeting. Then the specific, efficient hospitality of a city that has hosted an enormous number of strangers and long ago perfected how to do it well: fed, informed, rested, moved on, without wasted time but without coldness either.

---

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

Marked less by ceremony than by a shift in function — you're a visitor while Casey is serving you, and a resident once you're the one doing the serving: working the resupply economy, hosting other travelers, becoming part of what makes the junction work rather than someone passing through it.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Casey-descended people in Concordia are recognizable by an unusual comfort with strangers and change, a direct inheritance of a life spent around constant transit — they tend to be good at quickly reading a new situation, efficient without being cold, and genuinely well-traveled in knowledge even when they haven't personally been everywhere, simply from a lifetime of hearing travelers' accounts. Their grief for Casey is quieter and less concentrated than diaspora from more singularly-defined lost cities — not because the loss means less, but because Casey's own culture never demanded big, visible statements of identity, and that reticence travels with its people even into loss.
