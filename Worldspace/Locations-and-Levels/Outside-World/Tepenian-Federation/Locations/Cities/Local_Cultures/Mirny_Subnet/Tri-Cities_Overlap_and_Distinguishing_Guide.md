# The Tri-Cities — Overlap & In-World Distinguishing Guide

**Established 2026-08-16.** A different axis of analysis than `Tri-Cities_Region.md`'s Enneagram read or
`City_Enneagram_Personalities/Distinguishing_Overlapping_Profiles.md`'s cross-city groupings. Those two answer
"what does each city's collective personality read as" and "how does this city differ from an unrelated city
sharing its Enneagram triple." **This file answers a narrower, more practical question: where do Zhongshan,
Sinheung, and Shirayuki — three cities that share a founding mechanism, a physical location, and even their
population makeup — actually overlap with *each other*, and what should a writer or designer lean on to keep
them from blurring together in actual dialogue, character writing, or level design?**

Built entirely from the three cities' own 32-section Cultural Spec Sheets (`Local_Cultures/Mirny_Subnet/
{Zhongshan,Sinheung,Shirayuki}.md`) and their Enneagram reads (`City_Enneagram_Personalities/Mirny_Subnet/`).
Every overlap below is real and specific, not a generic "they're all Antarctic cities" observation.

---

## Overlap 1 — Zhongshan and Sinheung share an Instinctive (Gut) Major Theme

**The overlap:** two of the three cities land on the same Enneagram Major Theme. Per each city's own dedicated
read (`City_Enneagram_Personalities/Mirny_Subnet/Zhongshan.md` and `Sinheung.md`), both are Instinctive
(Gut)-centered — oriented around presence, autonomy, and *being* rather than analyzing or performing. This
kind of partial overlap is expected and unremarkable on its own (with only three Major Themes across 36 cities,
some repetition is inevitable) — it's flagged here specifically because it's a different *kind* of overlap
than the others in this file: not a shared population, event, or piece of infrastructure, but a shared
temperamental register, and it deserves its own concrete distinguishing answer rather than being left as "well,
they diverge on the other two axes."

**Why the shared Major Theme alone isn't enough to tell them apart in practice:** Hornevian and Harmonic Group
differences (Zhongshan: Withdrawn/Competency; Sinheung: Assertive/Reactive) explain the *shape* of the
divergence in the abstract, but a writer drafting dialogue or blocking a scene needs something more concrete
than "one is withdrawn and competent, the other is assertive and reactive." Two "confident, physically present"
characters can still read as interchangeable if that's all a scene direction says.

**How to distinguish it in-world — concrete, writable tells:**

- **Economy of movement and word vs. visible effort.** A Zhongshan Gut-type character defaults to stillness and
  precision — saying less and meaning more, the same instinct behind the city's own linguistic marker
  ("someone from Zhongshan who says a situation is *complicated* means it is a disaster"). A Sinheung Gut-type
  character defaults to visible motion and demonstrated effort — competence has to be *seen* to count, the same
  instinct behind the city's outbuilt, legible-from-a-distance architecture.
- **How their anger actually surfaces** (Gut types are anger-based at core — the overlap is real at this level,
  and the divergence is entirely in the *expression*). Zhongshan's version is cold and permanent: "not rage...
  something colder and more settled... quiet, permanent contempt" — a Zhongshan character who's been wronged
  goes quiet and simply, irreversibly closes the non-prying boundary to you. Sinheung's version is immediate and
  wants to be seen: a Reactive harmonic group wants its intensity *acknowledged*, not smoothed over — a
  Sinheung character who's been wronged says something, pushes back audibly, and expects the offense to be
  named.
- **How competence gets proven.** Zhongshan proves itself by simply doing the work correctly and never remarking
  on it (Competency) — the craftsman who never explains why the recipe is right. Sinheung proves itself by
  making the work visible and countable (Assertive + the fabrication-chamber economy) — the fabricator who
  wants the shipment numbers to speak for themselves, loudly.

**Practical write-up rule:** when a scene puts a Zhongshan character and a Sinheung character in the same room,
lean on the felt-vs-shown axis directly — let the Zhongshan character under-narrate their own competence and the
Sinheung character over-demonstrate theirs. That single behavioral contrast does more work than restating either
city's Enneagram triple ever could.

## Overlap 2 — The same three national populations, in all three cities

**The overlap:** China, South Korea, and Japan each appear in *every one* of the Tri-Cities' population
tables — not just as a token presence, but at real tier weight:

| | Zhongshan | Sinheung | Shirayuki |
|---|---|---|---|
| **China** | Primary (35.83%) | Significant | Significant |
| **South Korea** | Significant | Primary (34.62%) | Significant |
| **Japan** | Significant | Significant | Primary (36.27%) |

This is the single most consequential overlap in the cluster: a Chinese-heritage character, a Korean-heritage
character, and a Japanese-heritage character could each plausibly come from *any* of the three cities. Ethnicity
alone tells a writer nothing about which of the Tri-Cities someone is from.

**How to distinguish it in-world:** the tier a nationality holds *in that specific city* determines the shape
of the character's lived civic experience, not just a population statistic:

- **Primary-tier in your own city** (e.g., Chinese in Zhongshan): your heritage *is* the public civic identity.
  You don't maintain a private/public split — there isn't one to maintain.
- **Significant-tier in one of the other two** (e.g., Chinese in Sinheung or Shirayuki): your heritage lives
  in the private/home sphere per that specific city's own social contract (see Overlap 2, below) — and *which*
  city shapes how that private life actually feels, not the heritage itself.

**Practical write-up rule:** never write a Tri-Cities character's personality off their ethnicity alone. Two
Korean-heritage characters — one Primary-tier in Sinheung, one Significant-tier in Zhongshan or Shirayuki — should
read as having grown up in genuinely different civic worlds, even though they might celebrate the same
holidays at home.

## Overlap 3 — The same public-face/private-heritage structure, three different textures

**The overlap:** all three cities independently developed the identical sociological shape — one nation's
identity as the public civic face, every other nation's heritage preserved through private, at-home practice.
Each city's own spec sheet states this almost interchangeably:

- Zhongshan: "The city's public face is Chinese; what people maintain in private is theirs."
- Sinheung: "The same public/private bargain found elsewhere in the Larsemann Hills cluster, except here the
  public face is Korean, not Chinese."
- Shirayuki: "The Significant tier... and the extensive 13-nation Notable list maintain identity through
  private, personal practice."

Structurally identical. If left undistinguished, three characters describing "keeping my grandmother's language
alive at home" would sound the same no matter which city they're from.

**How to distinguish it in-world — the mechanism enforcing the boundary is different in each city:**

- **Zhongshan: enforced by taboo.** The non-prying norm makes asking about someone's private heritage "a
  significant social error." The boundary holds because *nobody asks* — quiet, unspoken, felt as a kind of
  slow, accumulating grief across generations who "sometimes feel their heritage being absorbed by osmosis."
- **Sinheung: enforced by indifference, not taboo.** Privacy itself is explicitly *low* here — "people here
  know each other's business." Minority heritage stays private not because prying is forbidden, but because
  the city's whole public register (production-floor vocabulary, output-as-worth) has no venue for it and
  nobody's especially interested either way.
- **Shirayuki: barely enforced at all.** The boundary is soft because the city's public identity (art, music,
  fashion, "genuinely easy to make friends") is itself a shared creative language anyone can step into
  regardless of heritage — minority background isn't suppressed so much as simply not the city's organizing
  axis in the first place.

**Practical write-up rule:** a scene of a minority-heritage character being asked about their background should
play completely differently depending on the city — an actual faux pas in Zhongshan, a shrug in Sinheung, a
genuine, low-stakes point of interest in Shirayuki.

## Overlap 4 — The same founding mechanism (the Jeju-do court)

**The overlap:** all three cities' sites were settled by the same body — the International Court of Diplomacy
at Jeju-do, with Japan, Korea, and the Sinian Federation coordinating as peers — as part of one coordinated
regional settlement, not three unrelated decisions.

**How to distinguish it in-world — already present in each city's own file, worth stating as one framework:**

- **Zhongshan — Confirmed.** China's presence at the real Zhongshan Station already existed; the court simply
  ratified a claim that was already true. Relationship to the founding moment: *validation of something
  already theirs.*
- **Sinheung — Inherited, not found.** Korea's claim was allocated onto an *already-operating* Russian
  station — a home secured by treaty, on ground that still, in some residual sense, reads as having been
  someone else's. Relationship to the founding moment: *a claim that has to keep proving itself.*
- **Shirayuki — Allocated to emptiness.** Japan's claim was assigned to an unoccupied site nobody had settled
  yet — no prior claimant, nothing to inherit or confirm, a genuinely blank slate. Relationship to the
  founding moment: *a place decided for you, made into a place you'd choose.*

Same court, same year, same regional negotiation — three founders with three entirely different emotional
relationships to how they actually got their ground.

## Overlap 5 — Identical Long Night War status and unification trajectory

**The overlap:** all three cities are "Damaged; partially operational," resolved together specifically *because*
they're close enough to each other that differing fates made no physical sense. All three also share the same
three-stage amalgamation timeline (legal separation → de facto physical merge ~2688 → full legal unification by
the ~2780s, per `Tri-Cities_Region.md`'s own founding-and-amalgamation section) — the identical war experience
and the identical eventual endpoint.

**How to distinguish it in-world:** since the *events* are literally shared, the only real distinguishing lever
left is how each city's already-established temperament colors an identical shared history:

- **Zhongshan** would process both the war damage and the eventual unification the way it processes everything
  — without drama, quietly, competently. A Zhongshan account of the war reads like: *this happened; we dealt
  with it; we don't discuss it further than that.*
- **Sinheung** would fold the same events into its output-as-legitimacy identity — surviving and rebuilding
  read as *further proof* the city's claim to its own ground was worth defending.
- **Shirayuki** would reframe the same shared trauma the way it reframes everything — toward whatever's
  genuinely good that came out of surviving it together, consistent with its Positive Outlook harmonic group.

**Genuine content gap, flagged rather than papered over:** Zhongshan's own spec sheet has already developed a
specific angle on this — a diaspora "divided between those who fled and those who stayed," carrying unspoken
guilt on both sides (§32). Sinheung's and Shirayuki's files don't yet have an equivalent developed. Worth
building out in a future pass rather than assuming the same fled/stayed-guilt dynamic applies identically to
all three — it may not, given how differently each city relates to belonging in the first place.

## Overlap 6 — Shared physical infrastructure (airport, highway junction, boundary zones)

**The overlap:** one shared Tri-Cities Airport, one shared highway tri-junction (Hwy 4/22/110), and — per
Sinheung's own architecture section — literal boundary zones where the three cities' territories physically
adjoin.

**How to distinguish it in-world — this is the one overlap with a ready-made, already-canonical answer:**
Sinheung's own spec sheet states directly that at the cluster's edges, "three distinct building traditions
[meet] at negotiated boundary zones, none blending into the others." That's a genuine, concrete level-design
tool: a player physically walking across the cluster should *feel* the transition —

- **Zhongshan's core:** dense, bedrock-integrated, unornamented facades, aesthetic care concentrated in details
  a stranger wouldn't notice at a glance.
- **Sinheung:** industrial silhouettes deliberately outbuilding the inherited Russian station structures,
  legible and busy from a distance.
- **Shirayuki:** clean streets, art and sculpture as the built environment itself, a genuine outdoor gallery.

**Practical rule:** the boundary zones themselves are worth treating as their own micro-locations — three
architectural languages meeting without blending is a distinct, already-described sensory beat a level designer
or scene-writer can use directly, not something to invent from scratch.

## Overlap 7 — Zhongshan sits on Sinheung's supply corridor without participating in it

**The overlap, worth flagging precisely because it could easily be mis-written:** Sinheung's raw materials
arrive "trucked in from Mirny (via the Hwy 110/Hwy 4 tri-junction at Zhongshan)" — meaning goods bound for
Sinheung's fabrication economy physically pass at or near Zhongshan. It would be an easy mistake to write
Zhongshan as economically entangled in Sinheung's fabrication industry given the physical proximity.

**How to distinguish it in-world:** Zhongshan's own Division of Industry has no fabrication-chamber
manufacturing in it at all — its economy is technical/scientific, precision manufacturing (its *own* craft
tradition, not Sinheung's), and Prydz Bay fishing. Zhongshan is geographically adjacent to the corridor, not
economically part of it. A Zhongshan character would have essentially no personal stake in, or working
knowledge of, Sinheung's chamber-fabrication industry despite living within sight of the trucks passing
through — worth remembering specifically because the physical closeness makes the opposite assumption tempting.
