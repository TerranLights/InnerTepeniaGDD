## 2. Founding Story

The Sinian Federation was among the Upper Earth nations that persecuted robots and drove the War of Upper Earth. Zhongshan's founding population were Sinian defectors — Chinese and Sinian citizens who chose exile alongside robots rather than remain with a nation whose ideology they had rejected. This is a loaded origin, and the city has never stopped knowing it. They left their country. They did not leave their culture.

The name *Zhongshan* — the courtesy name of Sun Yat-sen, the founding figure of modern China revered across historical political lines — was chosen deliberately. It signals cultural continuity and political independence in the same gesture: *we kept the name; we did not keep you.*

Zhongshan's claim to its own site was never left to chance. Before the exile era began, the International Court of Diplomacy at Jeju-do — the same Upper Earth institution that later allocated the unoccupied Bharati site to Japan as Shirayuki — coordinated Japan, Korea, and the Sinian Federation as regional peers, settling each nation's claim in the Larsemann Hills area as part of the same process. China's existing, extensive presence at Zhongshan Station was confirmed as exclusively theirs; neither Japan nor Korea pressed a competing claim. Russia was never a party to that court at all — whatever Russian presence exists in Zhongshan today arrived the same way Korea's, Germany's, and Japan's did: as ordinary immigration into a city whose founding, and whose exclusive claim to its own site, was already settled before any of them arrived.

Australian expeditioners formed the founding wave of non-Chinese settlers who arrived once the exile era began. Subsequent immigration brought Korean, German, Japanese, Russian, and Eastern European communities in meaningful numbers, largest to smallest by the share Zhongshan holds today: Korea (9.70%), Germany (8.47%), Japan (7.30%), Russia (5.72%), Australia (3.66%). All of them integrated into a cultural framework that was already well-established by the time they arrived.

## 3. Climate Character

The Larsemann Hills give Zhongshan one of the better microclimates on the East Antarctic coast. The hills provide partial shelter from katabatic winds; exposed bedrock — unusual at this latitude — moderates the worst of the cold. Prydz Bay's maritime influence prevents the temperature extremes of the inland plateau. By Tepenian standards, the city is livable.

The city's relationship to the cold is philosophical rather than combative. Cold is not an enemy to be defeated; it is the condition of existence, endured and accepted across generations. The tea ceremony — built around deliberate warmth — is, at some level, a response to it: you make something warm, you take your time with it, and you let it sustain you.

---

## 4. Seasonal Rhythms

The polar night shapes the rhythm of the year more distinctly in Zhongshan than in most coastal cities. The city turns inward during the long dark — more reading, more music, more time around tables. Social time that is already slow becomes slower. People who have not experienced it say Zhongshan during the polar night is either the most peaceful or the most claustrophobic place in Tepenia, depending entirely on what they came for.

The return of light is observed but not celebrated loudly — consistent with the city's character. There is no major public festival at the solstice; there are private gatherings, and a general collective exhale. Summer, when it comes, is productive. The city's research and manufacturing output is higher in the lighter months. The culture does not pretend the difference doesn't exist.

## PART II — CULTURAL IDENTITY

---

## 5. Post-Culture Identity

**Name/concept:** *Zhongshan Austere* — colloquially among outsiders, *the Quiet City*

Zhongshan never lost its Chinese identity. What it did was refine it. One hundred and thirty years of Antarctic exile — cut off from the homeland its founders chose to leave, hemmed into a small, isolated site, living through six months of dark every year — produced something that is still genuinely Chinese and also unmistakably its own: it is Zhongshan. A culture that takes itself seriously, produces careful people, processes loss through art rather than suppression, and considers rushing to be, if not a moral failing, at least a form of bad taste.

The city does not perform its culture for outsiders. It does not advertise itself. It is simply what it is, and visitors feel the weight of that immediately — not because anyone says anything, but because the coherence of the culture makes your outsider status obvious from the inside. The Chinese cultural core is quiet, not hostile, but fundamentally insular. You are welcome here. You are also, always, a guest in someone else's city.

**Vision session, 2026-07-06:** The quiet is not stillness. Whatever is happening in Zhongshan at any given moment — business, commerce, industry, or leisure — carries a clear sense of purpose, visible to anyone paying attention even if nothing about it is loud. This is the same "bad taste to rush" ethic seen from a different angle: Zhongshan doesn't need noise or hurry to demonstrate that its activity matters, because the activity's purpose is never actually in question.

- **a. Founding tension** — The tension in Zhongshan is not between founding and long-run demographics (China won both). It is between the cultural pride of being Chinese — lowercase, a living identity, not a political allegiance — and the reality of exile from the Sinian Federation that defines the city's political relationship with its own roots. Zhongshan people are more Chinese than the Sinian Federation, in their own reckoning. They are also, irrevocably, Tepenian.

- **b. Fault lines**
  - Notable-tier families — Korean, German, Japanese, Russian, Australian, Southeast Asian — who have integrated into Zhongshan's public culture over multiple generations sometimes feel, not with anger but with a kind of slow grief, that their own heritage is being absorbed by osmosis. The bargain is fair: full participation in a coherent, respected culture in exchange for a public identity that isn't quite the one your grandparents carried. It still has a cost that accumulates across generations.
  - A second, quieter fault line: the same non-prying norm that lets an immigrant family keep its private heritage intact also means nobody ever asks how much of it is actually still there by the fourth or fifth generation. Some families know exactly. Others genuinely aren't sure anymore, and the culture that would never pry is also the culture that will never help them find out.

## 6. Social Contract & Unwritten Rules

Zhongshan operates on a set of social norms that are never stated explicitly but are understood by everyone who lives there and felt immediately by anyone who doesn't.

- **a. The non-prying norm** — People do not ask about each other's personal lives, origins, reasons for being here, or private circumstances. Doing so is considered extremely bad taste — not hostile, not aggressive, simply a significant social error. This applies to neighbors, coworkers, and casual acquaintances alike. Close friendships are the exception; the intimacy of those relationships is precisely what makes them close. The norm is so established that Zhongshan people often don't realize how unusual it is until they leave.

- **b. Hospitality character** — Zhongshan hospitality is ceremony rather than warmth. The host performs; the guest receives. You are welcomed into a ritual, not pulled into casual intimacy. Tea will be made properly. You will be given time and care. You will not be asked about yourself. This is a specific kind of warmth, and one that suits the non-prying culture exactly: *I will give you tea, properly made, and I will not ask you anything.*

- **c. The slowness of social time** — Meals are long. Gatherings are not rushed. People who are visibly in a hurry are subtly — never openly — noted. This is not enforced; it is simply the cultural expectation that accumulates across a lifetime of living here. The tea ceremony teaches it: you do not rush this.

- **d. Public/private divide** — What you do in your own home, your own neighborhood, your own community is your business. The city's public face is Chinese; what people maintain in private is theirs. Gossip is rare, conducted only in specific private contexts between close intimates, never casually or publicly.

- **e. Privacy as civic value** — There is an informal city code around not broadcasting other people's business. This extends to the Arcanet: the local network is a research and archive tool, not a social broadcasting platform. Information about research is shared freely; information about people is not.

## 7. Who This City Attracts

People do not end up in Zhongshan by accident. It is not a transit hub; it is not on the way to anywhere; it does not have the commercial pull of Janbogo or the strategic importance of Mirny. People who are in Zhongshan chose to be there. The population is therefore unusually self-selected — people who specifically wanted this kind of culture, this kind of quiet, this kind of privacy.

- **The type this city draws:** People who want to work and be left alone. People who carry something they don't want to discuss. People who find warmth-as-performance exhausting. Technical people who treat their craft as philosophy. People who are between chapters and need somewhere that won't ask them about it.

- **The type this city repels:** People who need social feedback to feel present. People who process things by talking about them publicly. People who find quietness cold. People who read non-prying as indifference rather than respect.

- **Ji-Eun Kim (case study):** Chose Zhongshan over Davis during the period between Sejong and Concordia. Davis, despite being a geographically closer and similarly composed East Asian city, has its own warmer, more extroverted civic character — more likely to ask where you're from and why you came. Zhongshan didn't ask. Ji-Eun's work in nanotech fit the city's craft ethic; her Korean identity fit quietly into the notable tier community structure; and her need for a city that wouldn't require her to explain herself was met exactly. She stayed long enough to move from visitor to something approaching resident.

## 8. Language

- **a. Primary language(s):** Mandarin (Sinian Standard), heavily maintained across generations, the city's sole public language. Notable-tier communities — Korean, German, Japanese, Russian, and others — maintain their own languages at home, generation to generation, but Zhongshan's public life runs on Mandarin alone.

- **b. Linguistic character:** Zhongshan Mandarin is notably deliberate in its pacing. Fast speech is considered slightly careless; a measured quality in delivery is the cultural norm — the same instinct that shapes everything else about how the city carries itself: care concentrated where it counts, nothing rushed, nothing performed. No one explains this; it is simply how people talk.

- **c. Slang / dialect markers:** A person from Zhongshan is recognizable by their pacing (unhurried, considered) and by a particular habit of understatement when describing anything significant. Someone from Zhongshan who says a situation is *complicated* means it is a disaster. Someone who says it is *interesting* means it has their full attention. The more significant something is, the quieter the language around it becomes.

- **d. Language and integration:** The non-prying culture extends to language. Nobody will comment on your accent, correct your grammar, or ask you to explain a construction. You speak; that is sufficient. This makes Zhongshan surprisingly accessible to non-native Mandarin speakers despite the city's strong linguistic maintenance — the social pressure is to participate in the rhythm of speech, not to achieve perfection.

## 9. Architecture

Zhongshan's built environment is layered in a way locals read immediately and outsiders rarely notice. The original station structures — repurposed research infrastructure, built for temporary scientific occupancy rather than permanent settlement — form the city's core: modular, unornamented, functional by design necessity rather than aesthetic choice. First-generation exile architecture built around them, adapting a research base into a home. The outer rings are the product of 130 years of building in what has become a recognizable Zhongshan idiom: austere, proportional, built to last, with aesthetic care concentrated in details — a doorway, a courtyard threshold, the way a window frames a view of the hills.

That concentration of care is not decorative habit; it is the same instinct that runs through Zhongshan's social contract. A population that had just fled a political order where visible display was how the state tracked and measured loyalty did not want its architecture doing the same thing in reverse. A facade that announces nothing gives the state — or anyone else — nothing to read. Care went where only intimates would ever see it: inside a threshold, not across a skyline. The city's unornamented public face and its non-prying social norm are the same solution, applied twice — once to buildings, once to conversation.

Materials reinforced the choice. A site this isolated could not sustain an economy of imported ornament; what got built had to last, because replacing it was not a realistic option for a population with no functioning supply chain back to anywhere. Structural robustness — material choices that look like they intend to outlast you — was not a value imported from anywhere. It was the only rational way to build on an exposed Antarctic outcrop with what the founding population actually had on hand.

The Larsemann Hills bedrock is visible throughout the city. Rather than concealing it, Zhongshan's builders integrated it — structures cut into and around the natural rock formations, buildings whose foundations are the hills themselves. The result is a city that looks like it grew out of its location rather than being placed on top of it. Residents find this simply correct. Visitors often find it striking in a way they can't immediately articulate.

Zhongshan is not a commercial hub, and its architecture shows it: no facade competes for a passing trader's attention the way Janbogo's does. What the city needed instead — durable working space for continuous research and precision manufacturing, and large shared rooms where a hemmed-in population could sit out a six-month dark together — is exactly what got built.

**Vision session, 2026-07-06:** Zhongshan's street layout itself reads as chaotically organized at street level — a genuinely organic pattern rather than a master-planned one, shaped by 130 years of building around the bedrock rather than through it. But a semi-gridlike order underlies it, laid down early and never fully abandoned even as later construction bent and broke it in places. The result is a city that looks disordered to a newcomer's eye but that every resident can navigate without thinking — the same kind of "chaos that is actually structure" that shows up in Zhongshan's food culture (nobody agreeing on the correct dumpling, everybody still making one) and its quiet, purposeful social rhythm.

The resulting style carries Song Dynasty aesthetic principles forward directly — sparse, proportional, restraint over ornament is the vocabulary Zhongshan's own founding Chinese population actually brought, and the one every generation since has kept building in. What Antarctic circumstance shaped was not whether that inheritance shows up — it does, unbroken, because the population is unbroken — but how it had to adapt to survive on an isolated rock outcrop through six months of dark: sparser, more proportional, organized around bedrock and scarcity rather than around whatever shaped the style back home.

## 10. Cuisine

- **a. Human cuisine:** Zhongshan's food culture was built by a population cut off from any outside supply chain, needing to feed itself through a six-month polar night on what a short summer and Prydz Bay could provide. That constraint, not an imported cuisine, is the actual origin of the city's food: heavy reliance on preservation, fermentation, and pickling, because fresh food had to be made to last through the dark; dense, long-simmered preparations, because they store well and stretch scarce ingredients across a shared table. **Vision session, 2026-07-06:** the protein base for much of this cooking is Prydz Bay fishing — Zhongshan pulls a substantial share of its everyday food directly from the coastline, and the fermentation/preservation methods the city developed apply as readily to fish as to anything else, giving the food culture a genuinely maritime backbone.

  The central food object of Zhongshan is the dumpling — the jiaozi that Zhongshan's founding Chinese population actually brought and has kept making since, adapted by 130 years of Antarctic circumstance rather than replaced by it. The sealed-dough-pocket form solves several problems at once on this site: it stretches whatever filling is actually available across a shared meal, it stores and reheats well through the dark months, and it is fast to make in the bulk quantities the city's long, many-handed meals require. Every neighborhood makes its own, built around whatever the season and the bay actually provided that year, and around whichever household's version got passed down. Arguments about the correct dumpling are a local institution. Nobody agrees. Nobody is expected to — the argument is the point, a low-stakes outlet in a culture that otherwise doesn't argue in public.

  Meals in Zhongshan are long. This is not incidental — it reflects the culture's relationship to time and presence, the same unhurried social contract that governs the tea ceremony. A meal shared in Zhongshan is an occasion, even an ordinary one.

- **b. Social context of eating:** Food in Zhongshan is a primary vehicle for the city's social contract. The act of preparing and sharing food carefully is how Zhongshan people express care without asking about each other. You don't pry. You make the dumpling correctly. You let the tea steep properly. These are the gestures that carry the weight that words don't.

- **c. Robot cuisine / siligel culture:** Zhongshan's precision ethic extends to siligel preparation. The city has developed specific preparation traditions — careful, considered, treating siligel production as craft rather than industrial output. Zhongshan-style siligel is recognizable across Tepenia, associated with particular care in the sourcing and blending of precursor materials. Robot residents take this seriously in the same way human residents take their dumpling culture seriously: with strong opinions and no patience for shortcuts.

## 11. Fashion

Zhongshan fashion carries forward real Chinese textile traditions — fabric choices and certain garment structures — because the population making and wearing these clothes is genuinely, unbrokenly Chinese, five generations on. What Antarctic exile shaped was not whether that inheritance is present, but how it had to change to survive here: aesthetic investment concentrated in details rather than silhouette, a muted palette of dark blues, deep greys, charcoal, occasional deep red, with precision in cut and finish standing in for ornament.

That concentration of care in details rather than display is the same instinct that shapes Zhongshan's architecture, applied to the body instead of the building. A population that had just fled a political order where visible display marked you for attention didn't rebuild that habit here; clothing that doesn't announce anything is clothing that gives nothing away, in a city where that is still, quietly, valued.

The single most reliable marker of Zhongshan origin is clothing maintenance. This started as economics, not virtue: a population with no functioning supply chain back to anywhere could not simply replace a worn garment, so repair became the only rational response to wear. That necessity hardened into a value over five generations — allowing your clothes to deteriorate read, and still reads, as a failure of care for what you have, not a matter of means. Zhongshan clothing is repaired, not replaced, and repaired well. A garment showing careful repair is more respectable than a new one. Zhongshan people in Concordia tend to be the ones whose clothes are old but impeccably kept.

## 12. Music

Zhongshan's musical tradition draws on a real Chinese classical inheritance — instruments (erhu, pipa, guqin) and compositional structures — because the population that built the tradition, and the population still performing and teaching it, is genuinely, unbrokenly Chinese. What 130 years of Antarctic exile shaped was not whether that inheritance is present, but what the city actually needed music to do with it: something to sit with during a six-month dark, in a culture that does not perform its feelings publicly and does not gather to be loud. Music for listening, not for dancing, not for announcing a gathering — because announcing things is not what this city does.

The result is contemplative, structurally dense, unhurried: a genuinely Zhongshan classical tradition, carried forward and reshaped by its own people rather than transplanted unchanged. A Zhongshan composition takes time to understand. This is considered appropriate — the same standard the culture applies to a properly steeped pot of tea.

The city is associated across Tepenia with this classical tradition. Other cities produce music for dancing, for working, for gathering. Zhongshan produces music for listening. These are understood as different activities.

The counterculture's relationship to the musical mainstream is direct and deliberate: it is everything the classical tradition is not — fast, loud, immediate, and unapologetic about it. It draws its energy from Zhongshan's notable-tier communities — Thai, Vietnamese, Malaysian, Filipino among them — whose own founding circumstances didn't carry the same pressure to stay unseen, and in a city built around not performing, simply performing anyway reads as the transgression.

## 13. Arts & Aesthetics

- **Visual arts:** Zhongshan's visual art carries forward Chinese literati painting traditions directly — ink, restraint, negative space, the suggestion of a thing rather than its complete rendering — the inheritance of the population that founded the city's artistic vocabulary and has kept it unbroken since. What Antarctic exile added was a harder edge: ice, rock, darkness, the bay in winter resist prettification, and a culture that does not perform for outsiders does not produce art that performs either — restraint on the canvas is the same instinct as restraint in a conversation, applied with a brush. The result is spare and somewhat severe, beautiful in the way that accurate observation can be beautiful.

- **Literature:** Zhongshan has produced the most significant literary tradition of any Tepenian city, carrying forward a real Chinese prose tradition built around suffering, endurance, and the interior life under external pressure. In exile, under Antarctic conditions, and alongside a linguistic norm where understatement already carries the real weight of what's being said (§8) and a social contract where grief goes unacknowledged rather than discussed (§20), that inheritance sharpened rather than diluted. The Zhongshan literary sensibility is characterized by emotional intensity expressed through restraint: what is not said matters as much as what is. A specific literary form has emerged — structured, compressed, concerned with the long view — genuinely descended from its Chinese source and genuinely shaped into something the source tradition alone never produced.

- **Crafts:** Zhongshan's artisan tradition runs deep, the same precision applied to material culture that the city applies to everything else it makes. Woodworkers, ceramicists, and metalworkers hold respected, long-standing positions, their standards passed down the same careful way the tea ceremony is — a genuinely, continuously Chinese idiom, unbroken since founding. Notable-tier communities keep their own smaller craft and material traditions in their own homes — Korean, German, Japanese, Russian objects that show up in the private spaces of the families who kept them — but Zhongshan's public artisan class works in the same idiom as everything else the city puts on display.

## 14. Sensory Profile

- **Sound:** Zhongshan is quiet by the standards of any other Tepenian city. Voices are kept low in public; there is little street noise beyond the functional. Wind off Prydz Bay is the most consistent ambient sound. Music, when it occurs in public spaces, tends toward the instrumental and unhurried. The loudest neighborhoods are the counterculture districts, which locals note and largely ignore.

- **Smell:** Cold salt air from the bay; the specific smell of the bedrock in summer (a mineral smell that Zhongshan people find anchoring and that visitors find strange); fermented and slow-cooked food from the residential areas; siligel processing facilities at the industrial edge of the city.

- **Feel:** The city is physically dense in its core — buildings close together, not for warmth alone but as a matter of the original station's footprint, expanded carefully over time. Moving through the core feels like moving through something that has settled into its own weight. The outer districts are more open, built against and around rock formations. In winter, the darkness gives the city a particular quality of interiority — everything feels closer, more deliberate.

- **First impressions for visitors:** The quiet lands first. Then the sense of having walked into the middle of something that was already happening. Then, usually within the first day, the realization that nobody has asked you a single personal question.

## 16. Human-Robot Relations

Zhongshan's founding population were people who chose robots over their nation. Their descendants know this, and it shapes the baseline. Human-robot solidarity is not an ideology in Zhongshan — it is a founding fact, absorbed into the culture at a level below conscious argument. The question of whether humans and robots are equals is not a political question here; it is a historical one, and the answer was settled at the city's founding.

After 130 years, the raw intensity of that defection has faded into cultural background. Nobody makes speeches about it. The integration simply is. Human and robot residents participate in the same slow social culture, observe the same tea ceremony, maintain the same non-prying norms. A robot resident of Zhongshan is Zhongshan before they are a robot.

---

## 17. Robot-Specific Culture

Robot residents of Zhongshan gravitate toward the city's technical and scientific sectors — not by exclusion, but because that is where the culture's center of gravity is, and robots drawn to Zhongshan tend to be drawn by the same things human residents were. The precision ethic applies fully: robot craftspeople in Zhongshan are indistinguishable from human craftspeople in their standards.

Robot philosophical life in Zhongshan tends toward the contemplative and abstract — simulation theory, the "God-mind" traditions, Adinkra Codex frameworks — compatible with a city that takes serious thought seriously. The literary tradition is shared: robots in Zhongshan read the same books, and write them.

The tea ceremony — gathering around deliberate warmth for extended conversation — adapts naturally to robot residents. Robot residents participate in the ritual without the beverage. The point was never the tea.

## 18. Religious / Philosophical Landscape

- Secular philosophical tradition is dominant: Confucian ethics reworked for exile conditions, Daoist acceptance of endurance as a condition of life — the question of how to live with suffering without being destroyed by it
- Among robots: simulation theory and "God-mind" frameworks are the most common serious philosophical orientations; the city's intellectual culture provides fertile ground for both
- Organized religion is marginal — the city's character skews heavily toward philosophy over faith
- Some residents — human and robot — maintain the tea ceremony as something closer to a meditative ritual than a social one; this is not formalized but is recognized

## 19. Underground / Counterculture

Given that the mainstream is austere, classical, and deliberate, the counterculture is its precise opposite: loud, fast, colorful, deliberately low-brow, and proud of it. The Southeast Asian notable tier communities — Thai, Vietnamese, Malaysian, Filipino — provide much of the cultural backbone for this. Their aesthetic traditions are warmer and more festive than anything in the Zhongshan mainstream, and in this context they read as transgressive.

The counterculture is not anti-Zhongshan so much as anti-austere. It is centered in specific neighborhoods and venues that the mainstream is fully aware of and largely ignores — consistent with the non-prying norm. What you do in your neighborhood is your business. The counterculture's music is fast, its colors are saturated, and it is the only place in Zhongshan where rushing is considered correct.

---

## 20. Private Life & Minority Communities

The integration bargain in Zhongshan is well-understood and largely accepted: in public, you participate in Zhongshan culture. In private, your own heritage is yours. Nobody asks about the private part.

For the city's notable tier communities — Korean, German, Japanese, Russian, Australian, Southeast Asian — this means a specific kind of double life. In public: Mandarin, the tea ceremony, the unhurried social rhythms, the Zhongshan aesthetic. In private: the food cooked at home, the language spoken with family, the music played when nobody from outside the household is listening. The city's public face is Chinese; what lives behind closed doors is more varied.

The generational cost: second and third-generation immigrant families sometimes feel their heritage being absorbed by osmosis rather than by force. Your grandmother spoke Korean — or Russian, or German, or Japanese — at home. Your mother sometimes did. You understand it but default to Mandarin even in private. This is not a crisis — it is a presence. A slow grief that the city, consistent with its character, does not acknowledge and does not ask about.

## PART IV — POLITICAL & EXTERNAL

## 21. Political Character

Deliberate, consensus-oriented, and slow to political anger — but very difficult to move once a position has been reached. Zhongshan does not produce charismatic political movements or flashy demagogues. It produces institutional memory and a long view. The city values continuity and is skeptical of any politics that requires urgency.

Its relationship with the Mirny subnet is complicated. Mirny is the hub; Zhongshan is larger by Census II figures, older, and culturally distinct in ways that don't always sit comfortably under the Mirny umbrella. This is not a conflict — it is a quiet negotiation that has been ongoing for 130 years.

---

## 22. Relationship to Upper Earth

The most psychologically complex relationship of any Tepenian city. The Sinian Federation exiled Zhongshan's founding population. Their descendants carry both the pride of that choice and the specific grief of being cut off from a cultural homeland they still claim as their own.

The dominant attitude is not rage — rage would require ongoing engagement with the Sinian Federation, which Zhongshan largely refuses to give. It is something colder and more settled: deep cultural pride in Chinese identity combined with a quiet, permanent contempt for the state that cast them out. *We are more Chinese than they are. We kept the name. We kept the things that matter. They kept the territory.*

## 24. Arcanet Culture

Heavy information culture with a strong archival orientation. Zhongshan is known within the Mirny subnet for meticulous record-keeping — the city treats its own history with the same seriousness it treats everything else. After the Planetary Split Brain, Zhongshan's archives are among the most comprehensive records of the pre-split era accessible on the Mirny subnet, and the city is aware of this and takes the custodial responsibility seriously.

The non-prying culture shapes Arcanet use: not heavy on social broadcasting or public disclosure of personal information. The local network in Zhongshan is a research and archive tool, not a social one. People do not post about each other. What happens in private stays private.

## 27. Visitor Experience

Visitors to Zhongshan typically report the same sequence of impressions.

The quiet lands first. Not silence — it is a city, and it functions — but a quality of low ambient noise that is immediately distinctive to anyone arriving from Janbogo, Fort McMurdo, or any of the Palmer subnet cities.

Then the sense of having walked into the middle of something already in progress. The culture is not performing for you; it does not reorient around your presence. This is not hostility. It is simply that the city was happening before you arrived and will continue after you leave.

Then, usually within the first day: the realization that nobody has asked you a single personal question. Not your name, not where you're from, not what brings you here. This can feel like freedom or like loneliness, depending entirely on what the visitor needed.

Visitors who came for the quiet tend to extend their stays. Visitors who came for other reasons sometimes leave feeling the city never quite let them in — and that is accurate, in a sense. The city didn't close to them; it simply didn't open. The opening is the visitor's to do.

## 28. The Visitor-to-Resident Transition

There is no ceremony marking the transition from visitor to resident in Zhongshan. No formal process, no social ritual, no acknowledged threshold. And yet the transition is real, and people who have lived through it describe it consistently.

At some point, the city stops feeling foreign. You know the rhythms. You know how long to let the tea steep. You know which neighborhoods are loud on which nights and what that means about the people in them. You stop feeling like you're guessing at an unwritten code and start feeling like you simply know it.

That is when you have become a resident.

Ji-Eun Kim arrived in Zhongshan as a visitor and reached something approaching residency before leaving. She knew it when leaving felt like a departure rather than just the end of a stay.

## 32. Diaspora Character in Concordia

Zhongshan people in Concordia are recognizable. Quiet, technically precise, emotionally deep but not demonstrative about it — they carry a specific dignity that does not require acknowledgment from others. They are the people whose clothes are old but impeccably maintained.

They carry a particular grief: Zhongshan is damaged but partially operational, which means the diaspora is divided between those who fled and those who stayed. There is guilt on both sides of that divide, and it is not discussed. The non-prying culture travels with them: Zhongshan people in Concordia will not ask you about yourself, and they will not explain themselves to you either. Other Concordians sometimes mistake this for coldness or arrogance. It is neither — it is the city they came from.

The same social grace that felt like freedom when you wanted to be left alone can read as distance when you're somewhere new and lonely and nobody checks on you.

People who get past that surface tend to find that Zhongshan people, once genuine contact is established, are among the most loyal and considered presences in a person's life. They chose to connect. That is not something they do lightly, and it is not something they undo lightly.
