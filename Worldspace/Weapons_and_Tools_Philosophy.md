# Weapons & Tools Philosophy

**See also, added 2026-08-16:** `Weapon_Item_Catalog.md`, in this same folder — the item-level layer this file
doesn't cover (named tool/weapon archetypes within each category below, translating every Fallout: New Vegas
weapon type into Tepenia's own vocabulary, plus new Tepenia-original items FNV's setting never needed).

**Cross-media reference.** This document is philosophy, not mechanics — no damage types, no stats, no AP costs. It exists so the TV series (set during the Second Interwar Period, 2564-2812, well before Inner Tepenia's own post-Long-Night-War present) can depict these categories in their original, unweaponized civilian identity, while Inner Tepenia itself explores what happens to the same objects once a war forces them into a different role. See `project_tepenia_multimedia_expansion` context: this repo is the shared canon source multiple downstream projects (novel, TV) will eventually pull from, the same way `Orbital-Infrastructure/` already is.

## Core Philosophy

Tepenia was a unified country before the Long Night War. Humans and robots never had an internal reason to arm against each other — there was no civil war, no robot uprising, no factional bloodshed baked into the founding of the nation. Every category below exists first and foremost because Antarctica itself is trying to kill everyone who lives there, and second, in exactly one case, because of a possible external threat from Upper Earth.

That means almost everything in the taxonomy is, at its root, **not a weapon.** It's a tool someone needed to survive, build, extract resources, or care for another person (human or robot). Combat use — the thing Inner Tepenia's own systems are built around — is something that gets layered onto these objects by circumstance, not something they were designed for. The TV series gets to show the "before": the tools doing the job they were actually built for, in a still-unified, still-functioning nation.

**The one deliberate exception:** small and long guns exist because of genuine, if largely theoretical, defensive preparedness against Upper Earth. Even that is institutional and outward-facing — a customs post, a border watch, a rare piece of state equipment — not something an ordinary Tepenian household would own or need. It's the one category where the show can play with an object actually *being* what it looks like, rather than something repurposed.

## Reading the Same Object Across Two Eras

A structural-stress trigger used to bring down an unstable ice shelf before it kills a work crew, and the same tool used decades later to collapse a corridor on an enemy during the war, are *the same object.* The TV series and the game are allowed — encouraged — to use this. An item that appears in an early episode doing exactly the mundane thing it was built for can resurface in the game, generations later, doing something its Second Interwar owner never would have imagined. That continuity is a genuine cross-media hook, not just flavor text: it's dramatic irony available to any viewer who later plays the game, and vice versa.

## Category Philosophies

### Conventional

**Small Guns / Long Guns** — the odd ones out. Genuinely designed with violence in mind, but institutional, rare, and deliberately kept at arm's length from daily civilian life. The show's version of this category is a customs officer's sidearm, a border post's rack of rifles, maybe a ceremonial or historical piece — never a household object. Dramatic potential: a society that maintains the *capacity* for violence while having almost no lived experience of it.

**Heavy Weapons** — descend from convoy-escort and demolition equipment, not personal armament. The Second Interwar version of this is collective: protecting a Rastra convoy or a settlement, not a person. Philosophically, this is infrastructure defense, closer in spirit to a fire brigade than a soldier.

**Blunt Melee** — the most mundane category that exists. Ice axes, pry bars, sledgehammers. Every household, every work crew has these. No narrative weight needed to justify their presence in a scene — they're just there, the way a hammer is just there in any home.

**Bladed Melee** — tools of craft and provision: ice-core drills, flensing knives, industrial saws. These exist because someone needs to cut, harvest, or build something, not because someone needs to hurt someone. A show scene built around one of these is a scene about *work*.

**Thrown/Explosives** — avalanche-control charges are a very Tepenian inversion of what "explosives" usually signal: controlled destruction used to *prevent* a bigger disaster, not cause one. This is proactive safety work, potentially even a specialized, respected profession.

**Unarmed** — the body itself as the tool. For robots, this touches directly on frame/body philosophy (see `Robot_Biology_and_Culture/`) — there's no exposed mechanical striking surface, just a reinforced frame under synthetic skin doing physical labor the way any body does.

### Energy-Adjacent

**Thermal Weapons** — in their original identity, these are coolant-tending tools. This is arguably the single most intimate category in the entire taxonomy: a human tending a robot companion's coolant system, or a robot performing careful self-maintenance (see Griselda Alejandra Chávez as a direct precedent — her decades of self-maintained frame upkeep as an expression of self-sufficiency and care, not survival necessity). The show should lean into this as tenderness, not utility.

**Arc Weapons** — Arcanet and electrical maintenance work. Unglamorous infrastructure labor keeping the nation's communication backbone alive. A lineman, an Arcanet relay technician — background-of-society characters whose tools happen to become something else generations later.

**Cell-Fed Weapons** — the closest thing to "high-tech" in the pre-war set, but still fundamentally vehicle and fabrication-chamber maintenance work. Worth remembering that energy cells are already economically real (see `National_Economy_and_Currency.md`) — a technician handling these during the Second Interwar is handling something closer to currency than ammunition, which is a genuinely different relationship to the object than the game's combat framing.

### Tepenia-Original

**Structural Ordnance** — civil engineering in the most literal sense: glacier management, controlled structural venting, the work that keeps a domed or ice-adjacent settlement standing at all. This could open an episode — routine hazard work presented as exactly that, routine, with no combat undertone whatsoever.

**Drain Weapons** — pre-war, this is coolant-siphon and diagnostic *tending*, not draining. The inversion here is total: what the game frames as an attack (removing a resource from an unwilling target) is, in its original form, an act of trust — a caretaker relationship between two people, human or robot, one of whom is looking after the other's literal ability to function. This is the category with the widest gap between its show identity and its game identity, and that gap is worth writing toward deliberately.

**Signal Weapons** — pre-war, this is Arcanet diagnostics and network administration: the quiet, largely invisible labor of keeping the whole nation's communication grid running. There's real dramatic potential here beyond pure mundanity, though — a network technician is also someone positioned to notice things nobody else would, which opens the door to espionage-adjacent tension during the Second Interwar's own political pressures, without ever needing to become combat.

## Notes for the TV Series Specifically

- For most of its run, the show predates the weaponization entirely — only the Conventional guns category carries any inherent violent connotation, and even that should read as rare and institutional rather than commonplace.
- **The final season depicts the Long Night War itself.** That changes the structural payoff: this isn't just a cross-media wink for players of the game — the show gets to stage the actual pivot on-screen, within its own runtime. Everything established earlier in the series (coolant-tending as caretaking, avalanche charges as civic safety work, ice-core drills as livelihood tools) exists specifically so the final season can re-stage the same objects, in the hands of the same kind of ordinary people, now doing something none of them were built for. The earlier seasons aren't just table-setting for the game — they're table-setting for the show's own ending.
- Deliberately seed two or three "Chekhov's tool" objects early — items shown in unmistakably mundane use in an early season, then recognizable in the final season's war depiction, and recognizable again, generations later, in the game. A three-stage payoff (mundane → weaponized on-screen → weaponized in-game) is a stronger structural device than a two-stage one, and it's unique to having both a show and a game share the same universe.
- Consider whether the final season's weaponization sequence should show a specific character's tool cross over — the same coolant-tending kit, the same demolition charge — rather than a generic montage. A named object with an established civilian history lands harder than an anonymous prop when it turns into a weapon.

## Open Questions

- What Tepenia's actual outward-facing defensive structure looks like (the org that would own Small/Long Guns) is still unresolved — see `project_per_city_weapons` memory. This matters more for the show than the game, since the show could plausibly depict that institution directly, and its final season needs an actual military/defense apparatus to depict once the war starts.
- Now that the final season is confirmed to depict the war directly, worth deciding (once the show's actual outline exists) how many episodes that gets, and whether the transition from peacetime tool to wartime weapon happens gradually across the season or as a single sharp break tied to a specific inciting event.
