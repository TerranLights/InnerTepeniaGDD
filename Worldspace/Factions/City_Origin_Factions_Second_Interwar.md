# City-Origin Faction Possibilities — Second Interwar Period (2564 – late 2700s)

**Purpose:** Organic group tensions that exist *within* Tepenian cities while those cities are living, functioning settlements during the second interwar period — the roughly 200-year span between the Falkland Treaty (2564) and the Long Night War (2812). These are not displaced people processing loss; they're residents of real, ongoing cities, disagreeing about things that matter to them on their own terms, on their own turf.

**Method:** Each faction here is derived from a specific city's actual established local post-culture (see `Local_Cultures/*.md`), never from which real-world nation originally founded its station — see `feedback_no_national_stereotypes` in project memory for why. Founding nation can still be historical fact, but it is never the explanation for present-day behavior.

**What this document is NOT:** Anything about the Long Night War, Concordia, or displacement. That's a separate, later layer — see `City_Origin_Factions_PostWar_Refugee.md`. This document is about who these people are while their cities still stand.

**Split from the original `City_Origin_Factions.md` on 2026-07-04**, once it became clear that document's factions were entirely post-war/Concordia-refugee framed. The factions below are the ones that translated cleanly to second interwar, in-city life with light reframing; the ones fundamentally bound to having fled to Concordia went to the post-war file instead.

## The Founding Argument — Palmer City and Cape Adare

**Cities:** Palmer City (first Tepenian city, established 2564); Cape Adare (first permanent Antarctic habitation, 1899/pre-exile)

Two cities, both still standing throughout the second interwar period, share a specific kind of identity claim: the claim to the founding of something. They're claiming different foundings, and they have different relationships to what "founding" means.

**Palmer City** was the first Tepenian city — where the exiled robots first set foot in 2564 and said "here." Its residents hold this the way families hold genealogies: not as power but as precedence. They were first in the sense that matters to them — first in the context that actually is Tepenia. Pre-exile Antarctic history is, in their view, a different story.

**Cape Adare** was where the first humans permanently wintered on the continent in 1899, and its residents hold the deeper historical clock. The Tepenian story is one chapter; the human-Antarctic story begins centuries earlier and Cape Adare is where it starts. From their view, Palmer City's "first" is a parochial claim that ignores the longer history they're part of.

This is not a war. It's more like a long-running family argument about which branch of the tree gets to write the family history — conducted in real time, across two living cities, throughout the second interwar period. Both communities regard the other with a mixture of respect and mild competitive irritation. They collaborate on historical preservation projects and disagree about framing. Scholars from both cities publish work that is read and argued with by scholars from the other, decade after decade.

What makes this genuinely interesting is that both cities are, in their own ways, conservative in a philosophical sense — oriented toward the past, toward record-keeping, toward the maintenance of founding identity. They share values and methods while disagreeing about the object of those values. A Palmer City archivist and a Cape Adare archivist have more in common with each other than with most of their fellow Tepenians, and both of them know it, and neither of them quite wants to admit it because it would soften an argument they both find useful.

**What they argue about:** Which date is the real anniversary of Antarctic civilization. Whether the pre-exile history "counts" as Tepenian history or is simply Earth history that happened on the continent. Whether founding precedence confers any kind of moral authority, or whether it's simply a historical fact with no normative weight. (Palmer City says: no authority, it's just true. Cape Adare says: no authority, it's just older and therefore truer in a different way. This distinction matters enormously to both parties and is nearly invisible to everyone else.)

## Competence Without Commentary — Troll, Davis, Mawson, Abowasa, Mirny, Sanay, and Dumont d'Urville (plus one dissenter: Denison)

**Cities:** Troll, Davis, Mawson, Abowasa, Mirny, Sanay, Dumont d'Urville, Denison (dissenting)

*Expanded 2026-07-04: cross-referenced against every Tepenian city's own local post-culture, this exact pattern — competence as the real currency of belonging, performed quietly rather than talked about — turned out to recur independently at four more cities, each with its own distinct angle on what the competence is *for*. One more city (Denison) shares the same underlying value system in reverse: it explicitly rejects the premise the other seven have all arrived at.*

Seven cities, unrelated by founding nation, each independently arrived at a culture where demonstrated competence — not talk about it, credit for it, or heritage behind it — is the real currency of belonging. They disagree, sharply, about what that competence is supposed to be *for*. An eighth city thinks all seven of them are simply wrong.

**Troll** holds that competence is leverage. Troll's civic identity is control of the one intercontinental-capable airfield in the subnet, a structural chokepoint that makes "who currently holds the runway" the real belonging test in the city, cutting cleanly across any other line of identity. Cold-competence is boasted about, not complained of, because it's the currency that lets you hold the thing that matters. Competence that isn't leveraged into control is, to Troll's mind, wasted.

**Davis** holds that competence is humility. Davis's civic namesake was a support figure who enabled other people's glory and never sought his own, and the city inherited that as an actual operating principle: self-promotion is mildly distasteful, hospitality is thorough rather than warm, and the highest compliment in Davis is that the work spoke for itself and nobody had to explain it. Where Troll converts competence into leverage, Davis treats converting it into anything beyond the work itself as slightly beneath you.

**Mawson** holds that competence is inheritance. Two centuries into the second interwar period, Mawson's founding population has substantially shifted, and yet its civic identity has survived the demographic change intact, because what Mawson actually reveres is institutional continuity and competence itself, transcending whoever happens to be the population carrying it forward at any given time. Competence, for Mawson, is the one thing that outlasts everything else, including the people who first demonstrated it.

**Abowasa** holds that competence is intimacy management. Abowasa is the only Tepenian city founded jointly by two populations at once, and it's small enough that the daily negotiation between them happens household by household, over shared meals, because there's no anonymity to hide behind. Competence here isn't about infrastructure, humility, or institutional survival — it's the unglamorous, constant work of getting along with the specific handful of people you can't avoid, day after day, without either side ever fully setting old asymmetries down.

**Mirny** holds that competence is threshold-stewardship. Mirny sits precisely on the Antarctic Circle, and its founding-nation heritage (Russian) has long since become a numerical minority beneath a larger Chinese-heritage population — two permanent, overlapping thresholds, geographic and demographic, that the city has never needed to resolve and doesn't intend to. "Competence over romance" is the literal civic motto: hardship is managed, not mythologized, and the thresholds themselves are simply accounted for rather than argued over. Where Mawson reveres institutional continuity as its own virtue, Mirny doesn't revere the threshold at all — it just keeps standing on it competently, which is a subtly different thing.

**Sanay** holds that competence is the refusal of exceptionalism. Sanay took real structural war-era damage and actively rejects any narrative that would frame it as an untouched refuge or a special case — its bedrock stability is a genuine, literal advantage over its ice-shelf neighbors, but claiming credit for good luck is exactly the kind of talk this whole faction despises. Repair and upkeep are skilled, respected work, done the same unglamorous way whether or not anyone's watching, precisely because Sanay refuses to let its structural advantages become a story about itself.

**Dumont d'Urville** holds that competence is negotiation with the non-negotiable. Where Abowasa's daily negotiation is with a co-founding human population, Dumont d'Urville's is with permanent wind and an un-displaceable penguin colony that will never accommodate the city in return — competence here means accommodation rather than conquest, working around a constraint that isn't going anywhere rather than mythologizing the struggle against it. Its robots take the most wind-exposed work by plain risk calculus, not hierarchy or sacrifice-narrative — another version of the same "don't perform it, just do it" instinct.

All seven recognize the same underlying value in each other immediately — none of them respect empty talk, credentialism, or performative struggle — but they have genuine, sometimes sharp disagreements, conducted through trade contacts, Arcanet exchange, and the occasional cross-city visit, about which application of quiet competence is the *real* one, and which of the others are, respectively, a little too controlling (Troll), a little too self-effacing (Davis), a little too content to just persist rather than actually lead (Mawson), a little too small-scale to matter beyond one city (Abowasa), a little too comfortable with permanent ambiguity (Mirny), a little too allergic to ever claiming an actual advantage (Sanay), or a little too passive in the face of things that might, with enough effort, actually be changed (Dumont d'Urville).

**Denison dissents entirely.** Denison's civic identity is built on the *opposite* premise: pride in extremity, performed openly and often. Reciting the city's own wind statistics to visitors is a genuine, beloved social ritual, not an embarrassment. From Denison's point of view, the other seven cities' refusal to talk about their own toughness isn't humility — it's a missed opportunity, a kind of false modesty that quietly robs a community of the chance to actually celebrate what it's overcome. Denison finds it suspicious that competence-without-commentary always seems to double as an excuse never to throw yourself a parade.

**What they argue about:** Whether competence that isn't converted into control (Troll's view) is being wasted or being kept honest. Whether Davis's refusal to take credit is integrity or a failure of nerve. Whether Mawson's institutional continuity is a genuine achievement or just inertia dressed up as virtue. Whether Abowasa's household-scale diplomacy is the truest form of the shared value, precisely because it has no larger stage to perform for, or whether it's simply too small to generalize into a real position. Whether Mirny's comfort with unresolved thresholds is wisdom or avoidance. Whether Sanay's refusal to claim its own bedrock advantage is integrity or its own quiet form of showing off. Whether Dumont d'Urville's accommodation of the un-negotiable is realism or a failure of ambition. And, running under all of it, whether Denison has a point — whether the whole "don't talk about it" ethos secretly costs these cities something real, a form of collective pride they've simply decided not to let themselves have. Denison's neighbor and demographic near-twin, Casey, makes this argument uncomfortable in a different way: same population, almost the same city on paper, and yet Casey never once considered performing its own hardship the way Denison does — proof, to Denison's mind, that its own pride is a genuine choice, not something the demographics made inevitable.

## The Methodologists — Neumayer, Rothera, and Halley

**Cities:** Neumayer, Rothera, Halley

These three research cities share a scientific self-identity and a systematic worldview, and disagree, with real stakes, about what scientific rigor actually consists of — an argument conducted across two centuries of the second interwar period via Arcanet data-sharing, published findings, and joint expeditions, long before any of it has anything to do with a war.

**Neumayer's position:** Rigor is measurement, and measurement is close to sacred. Neumayer's civic culture treats precision and instrumentation with near-religious reverence — even hospitality here means giving someone precise information, not performed warmth. Neumayer scientists believe expertise requires depth before breadth: master one discipline correctly, on its own rigorous terms, before attempting to synthesize across disciplines. They're skeptical of interdisciplinary work that hasn't fully honored whatever it's drawing from, and they consider any research culture that tolerates imprecision to be, at bottom, not serious.

**Rothera's position:** Rigor is output, distributed so nothing critical depends on one point of failure. Rothera's civic identity is built on spreading production and infrastructure across the whole landscape rather than concentrating it — usefulness earns belonging, not heritage or performance. Applied to research, this becomes a methodology deeply skeptical of Neumayer's single-discipline depth: better a research program with redundant, cross-checked, distributed lines of evidence than one narrow, brittle line of perfect precision that fails completely if the one measurement is wrong.

**Halley's position:** Rigor is whatever survives being wrong about the ground under your feet. Halley's entire civic identity is organized around not fighting impermanence — the ice shelf moves every year, so the city designs for relocation as routine business rather than crisis. Applied to methodology, this produces scientists unusually comfortable designing studies that build in the possibility that the measurement position itself will change, and unusually comfortable treating a conclusion as provisional rather than final. Where Neumayer wants certainty and Rothera wants redundancy, Halley wants a methodology that doesn't break when the premises underneath it literally move.

**What they argue about:** Whether depth of mastery (Neumayer) or distributed redundancy (Rothera) produces more trustworthy results. Whether Halley's comfort with provisional, revisable conclusions is genuine methodological sophistication or a local adaptation that shouldn't be generalized to research done on stable ground. Whether Neumayer's standard of rigor is actually rigor or a kind of perfectionism that mistakes precision for truth — a question Neumayer considers insulting and both Rothera and Halley consider at least worth asking.

## Coherence vs. Contrast — Juan Carlos and Sejong

**Cities:** Juan Carlos, Sejong

*Expanded 2026-07-04 (formerly "The Tertulia — Juan Carlos"): Juan Carlos's own faction text already referenced Sejong as its defining contrast case — this just gives Sejong its own voice in the argument it was already implicitly having.*

Juan Carlos's defining civic institution is the tertulia — a hosted gathering, not an open one: there's a guest list, an implied register of conversation, and a real, unapologetic exclusivity to who's invited on a given night. Not everyone is invited to every tertulia, and this isn't considered rude; it's considered how a tertulia works. The gatherings run long, start late, and are organized around argument rather than consensus — but the argument itself is the point, not winning it. You're not there to be right. You're there to make the evening's argument more interesting than it would have been without you.

What makes Juan Carlos's version of this genuinely its own, rather than an imported social form, is the specific condition that let it develop over the second interwar centuries: relative isolation. Juan Carlos had fewer crowded neighbors than a city like Sejong, which meant its inherited social tradition had room to become *coherent* — refined into a stable, confident institution — rather than being constantly renegotiated against a dozen adjacent cultures at once. The tertulia isn't a transplant that survived the trip to Antarctica unchanged. It's what a hosted-argument tradition becomes when it's given a couple of centuries of relative quiet to think.

**Sejong's position is the mirror image.** Where Juan Carlos got quiet and became coherent, Sejong got crowded — a dozen national research communities sharing one ~80km island — and never had the luxury of coherence at all. Sejong's civic art form isn't the tertulia; it's diplomatic and coordination skill, an actual industry sector, because knowing what you are has always required knowing what surrounds you and constantly renegotiating the boundary. Sejong doesn't have Juan Carlos's confident, settled institution — it has fluency in contrast itself, the ability to know its own identity only in relation to whoever's currently adjacent. Sejong would say Juan Carlos's coherence is a kind of luxury that isolation bought, not a superior cultural achievement; Juan Carlos would say Sejong never got the chance to find out what its own tradition might have become on its own terms.

**The internal tension, on both sides:** Juan Carlos's tertulia culture is a culture of coherence, but the city's terrain — genuinely rugged, with limited usable ground on Livingston Island — caps how large that culture can ever get, and there's a live, mostly good-natured disagreement within Juan Carlos about whether the tertulia's exclusivity is a feature earned by the format or a limitation quietly imposed by geography and rationalized into a virtue after the fact. Sejong's own internal tension is different: cooperative coexistence with its many neighbors sits uneasily alongside real competitive tension over King George Island's uniquely mild, favorable terrain — the very desirability that drew everyone together also makes them rivals for the same good ground.

**What they argue about:** Whether the tertulia's guest-list exclusivity is a deliberate cultural achievement or a geographic constraint wearing a cultural explanation. Whether a tertulia that "wins" too cleanly — where one person's argument leaves the room with nothing left to say — was actually a good tertulia, or a failed one. Whether Sejong's constantly-renegotiated, contrast-dependent identity is a genuinely richer way to know yourself than Juan Carlos's settled coherence, or just what happens when isolation was never available as an option. Whether either tradition could survive being transplanted into the other's conditions.

## The Archivists' Debate — Cities of Memory

**Cities:** Belgrano, Palmer City, Cape Adare, Scott, Port Lockroy

*Reworked and expanded 2026-07-04: Belgrano's original position in this faction was explicitly Salagéa Aparast's post-war rescue mission, which doesn't exist during the second interwar period — she and her mission belong entirely to the post-war file. Rebuilt below around Belgrano's actual second interwar-era identity: a disciplined, function-over-comfort aviation hub, not yet in decline. Port Lockroy added as a fifth position, since its own established culture — continuous, layered memory as a lived way of being rather than a project — turned out to be a genuine, distinct answer to the same question the other four are already arguing about.*

These five cities share an orientation toward the past — toward preservation, documentation, and the weight of what came before — but they disagree, calmly and at length, across the second interwar centuries, about what an archive is FOR.

**Belgrano's position:** The archive is a duty roster. Belgrano's civic identity is disciplined, function-over-comfort, Air Force–descended — the kind of place where maintenance logs are audited and nobody questions why. Belgrano's archivists apply the same standard to historical record-keeping: an archive isn't a passion project or a rescue mission, it's a maintained system, checked and cross-referenced on a schedule, because that's simply how Belgrano keeps anything. Completeness isn't urgent here — it's routine, in the same unglamorous way the airfield's own maintenance rotations are routine.

**Palmer City's position:** The archive is a claim. The founding city's records are not just historical documentation — they are evidence for a position about what Tepenia is and who has the right to define it. Palmer City's archivists are often doing their work with a specific audience in mind: they want the founding record to be legible to future decision-makers. The archive is advocacy with dates attached.

**Cape Adare's position:** The archive is continuity. The deepest historical clock in Tepenian Antarctic life belongs to Cape Adare — the first wintering, the first permanent habitation. Cape Adare's archivists are less interested in advocacy than in the simple maintenance of an unbroken record. They would argue that Palmer City's advocacy purpose subtly distorts what gets preserved and what doesn't, and that Belgrano's rigid maintenance-schedule approach mistakes discipline for actual historical judgment.

**Scott's position:** The archive is a testimony. Named after a martyr who died with dignity and wrote about it, Scott approaches preservation through the specific lens of the last statement — what a person chooses to say when they know they're running out of time or resources. Scott's archivists are most interested in endpoint documents: what was said under real pressure, at the edge of something. They consider this the truest form of archiving because it strips away everything except what the person needed to say.

**Port Lockroy's position:** The archive is a way of life, not a project. Port Lockroy has already lived through military installation, then museum, then living exile city, with each era layering over the last rather than erasing it — nothing gets demolished without real consideration first. Its residents don't schedule preservation the way Belgrano does, or advocate through it the way Palmer City does, or wait for endpoints the way Scott does; they just live inside a place that's already three eras deep and keep adding to it. Port Lockroy finds the other four faintly strange for treating archiving as something you *do*, on purpose, at particular times, rather than something a place simply *is* if you let it accumulate honestly.

**What they argue about:** Whether disciplined routine (Belgrano) or advocacy purpose (Palmer City) produces a more useful archive. Whether Cape Adare's continuity-above-all approach is patient wisdom or a failure to ever take a position. Whether Scott's focus on endpoints fetishizes crisis rather than honoring ordinary, undramatic life. Whether Port Lockroy's lived-in, unscheduled approach to memory is the most honest version of archiving anyone here has found, or simply what happens when a city has never had to fight for its own preservation and so never developed the more deliberate habits the other four needed. Whether an archive should be for future historians or for the people whose stories it contains.

## The Crossroads People — Cities of Intersection

**Cities:** Sayowa, Esperanza, Janbogo, Casey, Princess Elisabeth

*Expanded 2026-07-04: two more cities turned out to share this exact "identity organized around betweenness" pattern, each with a genuinely distinct angle — Casey's is purely functional (a waypoint, not a cultural intersection), Princess Elisabeth's is the one version of this pattern nobody ever tried to resolve.*

These five cities share a specific quality: their identity, throughout the second interwar period, was organized around being the place where different things met rather than around being a thing itself.

**Sayowa's version:** The smallest of the three, and the most purely crossroads-identified. Sayowa sat at the intersection of the Atlantic and Pacific cultural corridors. Its residents navigated two different cultural streams simultaneously as a daily reality. The specific cognitive flexibility this produces — the ability to hold two frameworks without having to choose between them — is Sayowa's most distinctive cultural export. Sayowa people tend to be extremely comfortable with contradiction, extremely skeptical of people who insist there is one right answer, and occasionally insufferable about it.

**Esperanza's version:** The Peninsula gateway. Everyone came through Esperanza on their way to or from somewhere. The city's crossroads character was temporal as well as spatial — it was a city of arrivals and departures, and its residents developed a specific quality of provisional intimacy: the ability to form genuine connection quickly, because connection that waits for permanence never forms in a transit city. Esperanza people are often described as immediately warm but difficult to know deeply, which is partially a misreading — they form deep connections, but they form them faster than most people and are then surprised when the other person hasn't kept up.

**Janbogo's version:** Janbogo's crossroads character was less about being between cultures than about being the place where everything happened. The largest city, the most powerful node, the place everything ran through. Janbogo people have a specific quality of cosmopolitan centrality — they are not between things, they are the thing everything converges on. This produces a cultural confidence that can read as arrogance and is occasionally arrogance, but is more accurately described as the worldview of people who grew up in a city where being from here meant something specific and large.

**Casey's version:** Function without mythology. Casey was the last coastal city before Concordia's long interior drive on Hwy 110 — a genuine, load-bearing waypoint, not a cultural intersection like Sayowa or a temporal gateway like Esperanza or a center like Janbogo. Casey's civic art form is accurate, honest travel information, delivered quickly and without ceremony; integration into the city required no particular accent or fluency, just usefulness. The tension Casey carries that the other three don't: real pride in being useful sitting right next to real resentment at being known, Tepenia-wide, only as somewhere people pass through on their way to somewhere else — a genuine destination full of its own civic life, defined entirely by other people's journeys.

**Princess Elisabeth's version:** The crossroads nobody resolved. Geographically closer to the Mawson subnet's Dome Fuji than to its own Halley subnet cluster, and demographically split between Atlantic and Indian Ocean immigration currents, Princess Elisabeth never settled which larger cultural body it actually belongs to — and, unlike Sayowa, never turned that ambiguity into a point of pride either. It simply never came up for a final answer. Where Sayowa actively built an identity around being the hinge, Princess Elisabeth just quietly is one, without particularly noticing.

The tension between Sayowa and Janbogo as crossroads communities is interesting because they represent opposite versions of the same quality. Sayowa's crossroads identity is about navigating between larger things — being the hinge. Janbogo's crossroads identity is about being the center that everything rotates around. The Sayowa community finds Janbogo's version of cosmopolitanism slightly mistaken — centrality is not the same as openness. The Janbogo community finds Sayowa's version slightly precious — a small city that made a virtue of its smallness. Casey finds both arguments a little indulgent — from its point of view, a crossroads doesn't need a cultural theory of itself, it just needs to keep the road open — and Princess Elisabeth mostly stays out of the whole debate, since it never framed its own condition as an identity to defend in the first place.

**What they argue about:** Whether genuinely cosmopolitan identity requires having been at the center or requires having navigated the margins. Whether the crossroads identity is a stable thing to build a community around or whether it eventually requires choosing a home. Whether Esperanza's provisional-intimacy culture is warmth or avoidance of depth (Esperanza people find this question somewhat insulting). Whether Casey's purely functional self-image is admirably honest or a failure of imagination about its own worth. Whether Princess Elisabeth's total lack of a resolved identity is a fourth, quietly successful way to hold the crossroads condition, or evidence that not every city needs this particular argument at all.

## The Saint Cities — Communities Named After the Dead

**Cities:** Mawson (Saint Douglas Mawson), Scott (Saint Robert Falcon Scott)

These two cities carry the names of specific historical figures, and in each case the figure's story has become part of the city's identity in ways that are not always straightforward — a live cultural inheritance carried and argued over throughout the second interwar centuries, independent of anything that happens later.

**Mawson:** Saint Douglas survived. He lost his companions. He walked back to base alone, or nearly alone, across impossible distance, in impossible condition. The Mawson community carries this as a foundation story, but it is an ambivalent one — Saint Douglas didn't survive through sheer will, he survived partly through luck and partly through the deaths of others. Mawson's relationship with endurance is therefore not uncomplicated triumph. It is endurance-with-cost, survival-with-grief. The Saint is not a pure hero; he is a witness to loss who also happened to live.

**Scott:** Saint Robert died. He wrote about it with extraordinary clarity and without self-pity. "We shall stick it out to the end, but we are getting weaker" is the city's founding testimony, and it is a testimony of dignity in failure rather than triumph. Scott has inherited this as its primary orientation toward mortality: it is not the worst thing to die, if you die having done the thing correctly, having written it down, having not blamed the wrong people. This produces a community that is, in some ways, more comfortable with failure than most — not because they court it, but because their founding figure demonstrated that failure endured with integrity is not shameful.

**What they argue about:** Whether the founding figure's story is a model to emulate, a story to honor, or a fact about origin that has no special normative weight. Whether Scott's dignity-in-death tradition makes it unusually wise about mortality or unusually comfortable with failure in ways that might not always serve it. Whether Mawson's extraordinary survival-with-cost or Scott's extraordinary death-with-dignity is the more admirable response to failure — and whether "admirable" is even the right lens for either.

The two communities recognize each other as "the cities named after the expedition era" and share a historical consciousness that most of Tepenia doesn't bother with. They also disagree, sometimes sharply, about what lesson the expedition era is supposed to teach.

## Fort McMurdo — Life at the Center of Everything

**Cities:** Fort McMurdo

*Reworked 2026-07-04 (formerly part of "The McMurdo Problem — Communities of Lost Function," a post-war faction about McMurdo having lost its logistics-hub role). During the second interwar period, McMurdo hasn't lost anything — it's actively, currently the dominant logistics center, and that dominance is its own source of tension.*

Fort McMurdo was the de facto capital of second interwar Tepenia. Everything went through it — supply, personnel, information. For the first decades of Tepenian settlement and every decade since, McMurdo's logistical function has been the backbone of how the continent operates. Its residents know this, and their identity is not just "we are from McMurdo" but "we are the people who keep everything running" — a claim that gets more true, not less, the longer the second interwar period goes on and other cities' dependence on McMurdo's infrastructure deepens.

That centrality is not comfortable for everyone, including some of McMurdo's own residents. Being the city everything runs through means being the city that takes the blame when a shipment is late, the city whose labor everyone else depends on without necessarily respecting, the city that can never afford a bad season because too many other cities are counting on it.

**The internal tension:** between residents who take pride in being indispensable — the busier and more depended-upon McMurdo is, the more it matters — and residents who resent that indispensability, arguing that McMurdo does the unglamorous, thankless work that lets more celebrated research cities (Neumayer, Halley, Rothera) get the recognition while McMurdo just keeps the supply lines open.

**What they argue about:** Whether being indispensable is a source of civic pride or a trap that keeps McMurdo doing thankless infrastructure work while other cities get to be interesting. Whether other cities' dependence on McMurdo should translate into more influence for McMurdo in subnet-wide decisions, or whether that's not how logistics work and never will be. Whether McMurdo's identity would survive losing its centrality — a question nobody in the second interwar period expects to actually have to answer.

## The Coldest Devotion — Kunlun and Dome Fuji

**Cities:** Kunlun, Dome Fuji

*New 2026-07-04: a genuinely novel pattern with no overlap in any faction above — religious devotion, rather than heritage, competence, memory, or geography, as the organizing principle of civic life. Both cities are also unusual in being effectively 100% robot populations, which sharpens rather than resolves the tension below.*

Kunlun and Dome Fuji are the two holy sites of Ice Cold Buddhism, a faith organized around transcendence through extreme cold, and both cities' entire civic rhythm — ceremonial hospitality, liturgical time kept by the depth of the season's cold, national origin treated as irrelevant private trivia — is built around devotion to it. They also embody two flatly incompatible readings of what the faith actually requires of a devotee.

**Kunlun's position:** Purity is the highest devotion. Kunlun is the higher, colder, harder-to-reach of the two sites, and its population is entirely, exclusively Chinese — the only single-nation composition anywhere in Tepenia. To Kunlun's devotees, this isn't a contradiction of the faith's claimed indifference to nationality; it's a demonstration that the hardest, most complete form of devotion happens to have taken a specific shape here, the same way any extreme discipline narrows toward a single lineage of practice. Arriving at Kunlun at all is treated as a genuine trial — the altitude and isolation are themselves part of the teaching, and the faith's purity and the city's ethnic uniformity are, in this reading, simply the same fact seen from two angles.

**Dome Fuji's position:** Universality is the highest devotion. Dome Fuji's devotee population is genuinely multinational, and its residents take this as the truer expression of a faith that explicitly claims not to care where you're from — a religion that quietly became mono-national at its holiest site has, in Dome Fuji's reading, contradicted itself in the exact place it should least be able to afford to. Dome Fuji's own founding character was originally a secular Japanese scientific mission (long-term ice-core research); the devotional culture grew up alongside and gradually absorbed it, and whether the science is still a living mission or now just inherited liturgy is itself unresolved — but either way, Dome Fuji reads its own multinational composition as proof that devotion doesn't require a single bloodline to be genuine.

Neither city particularly wants this argument settled. Pilgrims and researchers who travel between the two (a rare thing, given both sites' near-total isolation from the Arcanet and from each other) carry the disagreement back and forth, and it has never produced a schism — mostly because both cities are too isolated, and too occupied with the genuine hardship of simply persisting at their respective altitudes, to organize a real theological confrontation over it.

**What they argue about:** Whether a faith's holiest site is the one closest to its founding hardship (Kunlun) or the one most faithful to its stated universal claims (Dome Fuji). Whether Kunlun's ethnic uniformity is a coincidence of history or a genuine, if uncomfortable, theological data point. Whether Dome Fuji's blended, scientifically-adjacent devotional culture is a purer expression of the faith's ideals or a dilution that happened to also be more comfortable to live inside. Whether the question can or should ever be resolved, given how rarely anyone is in a position to actually litigate it.

## The Farthest Point — Signy and Byrd

**Cities:** Signy, Byrd

*New 2026-07-04: total, structural isolation as the organizing civic fact — distinct from the crossroads factions above, which are about betweenness, and distinct from Competence Without Commentary, which is about competence as a value system. Signy and Byrd aren't between anything, and their isolation isn't primarily about proving anything to anyone — it's simply the base condition their civic life had to be built on top of.*

Signy has no road and no highway; it is geographically closer to the South Atlantic than to any other Tepenian city, and carries the weakest Arcanet link in the Federation. Byrd was founded underground, tunneling into a buried, abandoned station, before it ever had a surface city at all, and — following the removal of its former subnet neighbors from canon entirely — is now genuinely, permanently alone in a way its own founders never quite planned for. Both cities treat self-sufficiency as an assumed baseline rather than an aspiration, and both normalize competence rather than praising it. But they hold their isolation differently.

**Signy's position:** Isolation is a condition to be lived inside, not solved. Signy's founding character was scientific, but its lived reality is total practical self-sufficiency drawn from the genuine biological richness of the surrounding Scotia Sea — wildlife and weather cycles set the civic rhythm more than any clock does. Signy doesn't experience its own isolation as a wound; it's simply chronically behind on news from the rest of Tepenia, and has made a stable, working peace with that.

**Byrd's position:** Isolation is a founding act, inherited and then made total. Byrd's civic self-image was built around the deliberate, expedition-minded risk its robot founders took tunneling into a dead station on the strength of old maps alone — a genuine piece of robot civic memory, not an accident of geography. Most of Byrd's actual population arrived later, indirectly, via redistribution, without ever having chosen Byrd specifically — so the city's founding self-image (deliberate remoteness, chosen on purpose) sits over a population that mostly just ended up there. When its former subnet neighbors were removed from the map entirely, Byrd's founding self-image of remoteness stopped being an aspiration and simply became true.

**What they argue about:** Whether isolation borne by wildlife-rhythm adaptation (Signy) or isolation borne by expedition-founding memory (Byrd) produces the more genuine self-sufficient culture. Whether a founding story built on deliberate risk-taking (Byrd) still means anything once most of the population arrived by accident rather than choice. Whether Signy's weak-but-present Arcanet link makes its isolation more bearable (something is still coming through, eventually) or crueler (just enough contact to know exactly what you're missing) compared to Byrd's total silence.

## The Larsemann Hills Compact — Soyuz, Zhongshan, and the Japanese City (cf. Bharati)

**Cities:** Soyuz, Zhongshan, Japanese Diplomatic Partition (cf. Bharati)

*New 2026-07-04, resolving a gap flagged earlier the same day: three cities sharing one small ice-free oasis for two centuries — a genuinely second interwar-specific dynamic (sustained close-proximity negotiation over finite shared space) that a post-war framing wouldn't produce at all, since it depends entirely on all three cities being alive and adjacent at once.*

Soyuz, Zhongshan, and the Japanese city occupy the same small Larsemann Hills oasis, and two centuries of daily proximity shaped each of their civic identities as much as, or more than, their separate founding stories did. None of them can fully explain who they are without reference to the other two.

**Soyuz's position:** Ambition, chosen daily against the pull of the cluster. Soyuz named itself after a spacecraft rather than any inherited place or founder, a deliberate act of forward-motion self-definition — and its founding population is itself dual (Russian and Australian, together from the start). Living in constant, close-quarters negotiation with two immediate neighbors sharpened this ambition into something concrete: cluster-boundary diplomacy became a genuine daily skill, not an occasional inconvenience, and Soyuz takes real pride in having chosen its own forward-looking character rather than just absorbing the oasis's shared circumstances.

**Zhongshan's position:** Reserve as the only sustainable stance. Zhongshan's defining civic law is a strict non-prying norm — nobody asks about your past, ever, and hospitality is ceremony rather than personal warmth. In a cluster this small and this permanent, Zhongshan's read is that privacy is the only thing that makes proximity survivable over two centuries; if everyone's business were everyone else's business at this density, the oasis would have consumed itself in friction generations ago. Zhongshan's own Sino-Russian synthesis wasn't planned either — it emerged the same quiet, non-confrontational way its reserve did.

**The Japanese city's position:** Chosen character, grown over an imposed origin. Unlike its two neighbors, this city didn't choose to exist here at all — it was assigned by an Upper Earth diplomatic decree, a place decided for its population rather than found or founded by them. Its response to the cluster's proximity pressure was neither Soyuz's assertive ambition nor Zhongshan's protective reserve, but patient, deliberately broad cultivation — supporting genuinely varied research, art, and creative life rather than one dominant civic type, partly *because* an imposed origin left room to grow into whatever its people actually wanted, unconstrained by any inherited founding myth to live up to or push against.

**What they argue about:** Whether Soyuz's daily-chosen ambition is a genuine achievement or simply what happens when you have two strong-willed neighbors to define yourself against. Whether Zhongshan's reserve is wisdom earned through two centuries of proximity or a habit that's quietly cost it some of its own smaller heritage communities, who feel slowly absorbed rather than protected by the non-prying norm. Whether the Japanese city's imposed, decree-based origin is a disadvantage it overcame or, in the end, the freest founding condition of the three, precisely because it came with no inherited story at all.

## Grown Together — Lazar

**Cities:** Lazar

*New 2026-07-04, standalone (no clean second city shares this specific pattern yet — flagged as a possible future faction if a partner city emerges).*

Lazar has no single founding story. It coalesced from two separate settlements — a long-running Russian station and an improvised overflow site that took shape beside it — that physically grew into each other over the second interwar centuries and were eventually renamed to fit neither original identity. The result, now Tepenia's largest city, treats integration as the unremarkable default rather than an achievement to point to, and offers something genuinely rare in Tepenia: real urban-scale anonymity, with five roughly equal Significant-tier nations and no single cultural template anyone has to measure themselves against.

**The internal tension:** Lazar's fault line isn't founder-versus-majority, the shape almost every other city in this document carries — it's origin-versus-origin, the old coalesced core against the newer expansion that's grown up around it since. Neither side claims to be more authentically Lazar than the other, since neither of the two original settlements was ever "the real one" to begin with — but there's a live, low-grade sense among older-core residents that the newer expansion doesn't fully grasp what it took to grow two separate places into one, and a matching sense among newer residents that the old-core's attachment to that memory is starting to calcify into its own kind of gatekeeping.

**What they argue about:** Whether a city with no single founding story is freer to become anything, or permanently missing something every other Tepenian city takes for granted. Whether the old-core/new-expansion divide is a meaningful cultural distinction or a distinction that only the old core still cares about. Whether Lazar's scale and anonymity are what make it work, or what's quietly eroding the thing that made the original merger meaningful in the first place.

## Standing on Warmer Ground — Marambio

**Cities:** Marambio

*New 2026-07-04, standalone (previously considered for "The Tertulia Communities" and explicitly excluded, since its actual pattern doesn't share a throughline with any other researched city).*

Marambio holds two civic identities in genuine, unresolved tension: a transient, logistics-paced aviation hub, and the site of a 34-million-year-old fossil record proving Antarctica was once warm — a paleontological archive quite literally standing on deep time. Neither identity cancels the other, and the city has never tried to make them agree.

**The internal tension:** between the transient aviation-worker population, for whom movement and short-term presence are the civic norm, and the smaller, slower-paced resident scientific community, whose entire professional life is organized around timescales so long that a human lifetime barely registers. These are two fundamentally different relationships to time, coexisting in one small city, each barely able to make sense of the other's sense of urgency (or lack of it).

**What they argue about:** Whether Marambio is, at heart, a transit hub that happens to sit on something scientifically remarkable, or a scientific site that happens to also process a lot of aviation traffic — residents genuinely disagree about which identity is the "real" one and which is incidental. Whether the aviation workers' short-term, practical urgency or the researchers' deep-time patience is the more honest response to living somewhere this physically temporary (even the ice, after all, isn't permanent) — a disagreement that has never once been resolved, mostly because neither side has ever needed the other to agree.

## The Long Table — Zukelli

**Cities:** Zukelli

*New 2026-07-04, standalone (Zukelli's post-destruction faction, "The Burning Witness," lives in the post-war file — this is its distinct, living, pre-destruction civic identity).*

Zukelli's central social institution is the shared table. Communal eating isn't a preference here — it's a civic obligation, the primary way the city presents itself both to its own residents and to the world, warm and competitively affectionate in a way that leaves little room for privacy or reserve. Food and gathering are Zukelli's public-facing identity in the same way infrastructure is Rothera's or precision is Neumayer's.

Zukelli's other defining fact is its neighbor: Janbogo, 8km away across the same bay, larger and more powerful. The relationship is a genuine friendly rivalry, mutual mythology built up over the second interwar centuries — two cities that know each other's business, compete without real hostility, and each secretly believe their own version of the shared story is the more accurate one.

**The internal tension:** Zukelli's own founding-nation-versus-majority question — like Sanay's, Mirny's, or Lazar's, but never as fully settled — stayed live and unresolved throughout the second interwar period. Where Janbogo eventually settled into a stable equilibrium about who its city really belongs to, Zukelli's version of the same argument was still actively being had, without any clear resolution in sight.

**What they argue about:** Whether the shared table is genuine warmth or a kind of performed intimacy that leaves people who want privacy with nowhere to go. Whether the friendly rivalry with Janbogo is a healthy sibling dynamic or a comparison Zukelli can never actually win, given the size difference. Whether the still-unresolved founding-versus-majority question is a sign of a city still honestly working something out, or a sign of a city that's simply never gotten around to it.
