# City-Origin Faction Possibilities — Post-War Refugee Communities (Concordia)

**Purpose:** Organic community groupings among Tepenians displaced to Concordia after the Long Night War (2812) — real people processing loss, displacement, and identity in a city that isn't home, decades into the current game era (≈2822–2827). These factions are fundamentally *about* having fled to Concordia; they don't exist, and can't be reframed to exist, at any earlier point in Tepenian history.

**Method:** Each faction here is derived from a specific city's actual established local post-culture and the specific psychological reality of what it means to have come from that place — never from real-world national stereotypes (see `feedback_no_national_stereotypes` in project memory).

**What this document is NOT:** A description of these cities' second interwar-era, pre-war living culture — see `City_Origin_Factions_Second_Interwar.md` for that layer. This document is specifically about what happens after displacement.

**Split from the original `City_Origin_Factions.md` on 2026-07-04.** The factions below are the ones fundamentally bound to post-war Concordia refugee context — they were moved here essentially unchanged, since they were already correctly written for this scope; only their home document changed.

## The Continuity Schism — Mirny Communities

**Origin cities:** Mirny

*Corrected 2026-07-04: this faction was originally built on the premise that Mirny was destroyed in the Long Night War, with a second, Framheim-specific angle attached. Neither holds up. Mirny's established status is "damaged; partially operational" — the city survives — and Framheim no longer exists in canon at all (see `City_Origin_Factions_Second_Interwar.md`'s standby-premises note for where its two pieces went). The schism below is rebuilt around what's actually true: Mirny survives, damaged, connected to Concordia only by the physical Hwy 110 highway spur, not any special Arcanet link (Mirny and Concordia are different subnets, and the Planetary Split Brain severed that connection like every other inter-subnet one).*

Mirny Station was never abandoned for a single day in its first century of existence. This was not luck — it was a collective decision, renewed every winter, every crisis, every near-miss. The unbroken occupation was the city's identity in a way that went beyond pride: it was the proof that something could be sustained against everything the continent could throw at it.

Then the Long Night War damaged the station badly enough that, for the first time in a hundred years, its continuous operation was genuinely in question. Mirny survived. It is still there, still partially operational, still connected to Concordia by the same Hwy 110 spur that always linked them. But "partially operational" is itself the wound: the record that was never broken came closer to breaking than it ever had, and some of the people who called Mirny home left for Concordia in the years since — down that same highway, whether pushed out by the damage or pulled by opportunity elsewhere.

The Mirny community in Concordia has been living with a philosophical fracture ever since, and it runs deeper than ordinary homesickness. The question is not "how do we recover?" — Mirny itself is still recovering, back home, reachable by road but informationally as isolated from Concordia as anywhere else in Tepenia (there is no working Arcanet link between the two; the highway is the only thing that ever connected them, and it's just as silent to real-time news as everyone else's isolation). The question is "what does it mean that the unbroken thing was, however briefly, broken — and what does it mean that we're not there for whatever comes next?" Specifically:

**The Continuants** hold that the century of continuity survived because the station survived. The interruption was real, but it was a wound, not an ending — and a century-long practice doesn't lose its meaning because one winter was worse than the others. They maintain the same scheduling traditions, the same shift structures applied to whatever work they do in Concordia, the same winter observances, on the theory that the practice is what they're loyal to, not a perfect unbroken record that was always more myth than fact anyway.

**The Rupturists** — a minority but a vocal one — argue that the Continuants are engaged in a kind of self-deception. Mirny's entire identity, for a century, was that it was *never once* interrupted — not that it recovered well from interruptions. That specific claim is no longer true, and no amount of continuing the old schedules changes it. Worse, in the Rupturists' view: doing it from Concordia, at the far end of a highway from a station that struggles to stay partially operational without you, is closer to nostalgia than continuity. They believe the honest thing is to acknowledge that something ended — not the city, but the claim the city made about itself — and stop performing a continuity that the people who stayed behind are the ones actually still fighting for.

The internal tension between these two positions generates constant low-level friction. The Continuants find the Rupturists cold and defeatist. The Rupturists find the Continuants unwilling to reckon with what actually happened. Both sides contain people in genuine good faith. Neither side has the argument fully won.

**What they argue about:** Whether maintaining the old schedules in Concordia is preservation or performance, given the actual station is still standing and still doing the work. Whether leaving Mirny at all — even for good reasons, even after the damage, even by the same road that's always connected the two cities — is itself a break in the unbroken line, regardless of what happens back home. Whether the fact that Hwy 110 could, in principle, still be driven (unlike the severed Arcanet link) makes the separation feel more like a choice than a true isolation — and whether that makes it harder or easier to live with.

## The Burning Witness — Zukelli Communities

**Origin cities:** Zukelli (exclusively)

Zukelli was not a failing city. It was not a city in decline, or a city people were already leaving, or a city that had seen better days. It was at 72.5% human retention — near peak — when it was destroyed. The survivors did not evacuate from a dying city. They fled a living one.

This specific fact shapes the Zukelli community in ways that other destroyed-city communities don't share. There is no softening narrative available. There is no "well, things were already changing" or "people had already started to leave." The city was full. Then it wasn't.

The other fact that shapes them: Janbogo is 8km away across the water. Janbogo survived.

The Zukelli community is not unified in how it holds this. It has at least three distinct orientations:

**The Witnesses** take the position that what happened to Zukelli must be remembered exactly as it was, without comfort, without narrative smoothing, without the gradual drift of collective memory toward a version of the story that's easier to live with. They are not primarily angry — they are vigilant. Memory, for them, is a moral obligation. They are the ones who write things down, who correct each other's recollections when they soften, who get tense when someone phrases the destruction in the passive voice. "The city was destroyed" is not acceptable. Something destroyed it. That specificity matters.

**The Separatists** — not from Janbogo, but from the wider Tepenian refugee community in general — have developed a quiet but real cultural insularity. Not hostility, exactly. More like: they find that the company of people who didn't lose what they lost requires a constant low-level performance of okayness that they find exhausting. Within their own community they don't have to explain. They don't have to answer "how are you holding up?" with anything except the truth. They tend to socialize almost exclusively within the Zukelli community, which outsiders sometimes read as clannishness but which is more accurately described as the social cost-benefit calculation of people who are tired.

**The Integrationists** have reached, or are working toward, a position where Zukelli is part of their identity but not the whole of it. They have Tepenian friends, Concordia friends, friends from other refugee communities. They don't hide being from Zukelli — it comes up, it matters — but they don't organize their entire social life around it. The Witnesses sometimes regard them as having moved on too quickly. The Integrationists sometimes regard the Witnesses as having built a community around keeping a wound open.

**What they argue about:** Whether proximity to Janbogo culture is a betrayal of Zukelli identity or simply a practical reality. Whether the children born after the destruction are "from Zukelli" or just Tepenian. Whether the effort to document and preserve the city's memory is for the dead or for the living — and whether those are the same project. Whether anger at Janbogo (for surviving, for being close, for being okay when Zukelli wasn't) is legitimate grief or a dead end.

**What they don't argue about:** That it was full when it burned.

## The Boneyard Times — Belgrano Communities

**Origin cities:** Belgrano

*Moved here 2026-07-04 from `Local_Cultures/Halley_Subnet/Belgrano.md`, which had blended this content directly into the city's living, second interwar-era cultural identity — the only Tepenian city whose Local_Cultures file did this. That file has been corrected to describe Belgrano's actual second interwar identity only (130 years of a hard, disciplined, functionally-minded aviation hub, still standing as a going concern); this is the separate, later chapter of the same city's story. *Renamed 2026-07-04 from "The Bone Yard" to "The Boneyard Times"* — the name refers to the *era*, not just the place: this is what Belgrano's post-war period itself came to be called, not merely a nickname for the city.*

Belgrano survived the Long Night War intact — spared the direct destruction that erased Zukelli and Denison *(corrected 2026-07-05 — this line previously also named Sinheung here; Sinheung was never actually destroyed, only damaged, see `TODO.md`)*. Then, in the years afterward, it declined. Slowly. Whatever combination of infrastructure failure, population loss, and severed supply lines did it, the city that survived the war did not survive the peace. This period is what came to be known as **the Boneyard Times** — not a dead city, but a dying one that stopped dying at some equilibrium point and simply kept existing, inhabited by people who chose to stay or arrived because leaving wasn't an option.

The people who remained inherited the founding culture's discipline without its institutional backing — they still function like an airbase crew, because that's the only civic template Belgrano ever had, but there's no chain of command left to answer to. It's discipline as habit and inheritance now, not discipline in service of an organization that still exists.

*(Established 2026-07-04:)* Belgrano's second interwar-era mechanic subculture — the garage-and-engine-repair competence that made it the birthplace of the Rastra in the first place — becomes one of the Boneyard Times' few genuinely load-bearing skills. With no institution left to requisition new equipment, keeping old vehicles and machinery running at all depends entirely on people who still know how to work on them, Rastras included. If anything earns real standing in the Boneyard Times independent of the founding discipline itself, it's this.

**The central, unresolved question:** whether a culture can be said to persist at all once the population sustaining it has collapsed by an order of magnitude. Belgrano's remaining residents largely believe it can. Visitors from healthier cities aren't always sure.

The community holds at least two live fault lines:

**Those who remember peak Belgrano** (aging residents, some robots with long service records) carry grief for something lost — a going concern that used to mean something beyond its own survival.

**Those born into the Boneyard Times** never knew the city as anything but this, and carry no such grief, because they never had the thing to lose. To them, the era isn't a fall from anything — it's just what Belgrano is.

A second, related divide: **"staying is loyalty"** versus **"staying is just what happened to you."** The former frame their presence as a deliberate choice to keep the city's memory and function alive; the latter are simply people who never found a way out and have made peace, more or less, with where that left them. *(Established 2026-07-04:)* Whatever the "staying is loyalty" group called themselves — not yet established — those who remembered peak Belgrano gave them a specific nickname: **the Boneyarders**, a name that referenced their flat refusal to leave. Whether "Boneyarders" was ever fully embraced by the group it named, or stayed something applied to them from outside, is open.

**What they argue about:** Whether the exact cause of the Boneyard Times (infrastructure failure, population loss, severed supply lines, some combination) matters at all now, or whether the how is beside the point next to the fact of it. Whether the discipline that survives here is genuine continuity of Belgrano's founding character, or just muscle memory with nothing left to serve. Whether grieving what Belgrano was (the older generation's position) or simply living in what Belgrano is (the younger generation's position) is the more honest way to inhabit it. Whether "Boneyarders" is a name its bearers would claim proudly or one they'd rather shrug off as somebody else's word for their choice.

*Notable figure still open: whoever shaped the Boneyard Times itself — someone whose choices during Belgrano's post-war deterioration determined who stayed and how the remaining community organized itself. Not yet named.*

## The Question of the Unbroken Name — Destroyed City Internal Politics

**Origin cities:** All four destroyed/ruined communities — Zukelli, Belgrano, Palmer City, Casey — each face this question independently *(corrected 2026-07-05 — Sinheung was removed from this faction; it was never actually destroyed or ruined. Sinheung was reclassified 2026-07-03 from "Destroyed" to damaged/partially operational, effectively co-located with its neighbor Zhongshan, but this faction's own text was never updated to match — see `Specs/Sinheung.md` and `TODO.md`. Sinheung is a normal damaged-but-functional city, like Troll, Neumayer, Abowasa, or Sanay, not a member of this displaced/refugee category at all.)*

Every destroyed-city community eventually confronts the same question, on their own timeline, in their own way: when does a person stop being "from Zukelli" (or Belgrano, or Palmer City, or Casey) and become simply Tepenian?

This is not a question with an obvious answer. It is not even, on examination, a single question — it is several:

**The identity question:** Is your city of origin a permanent fact about you, or is it a diminishing weight that you can and perhaps should set down? The people who hold it as permanent are not wrong that it shaped them. The people who want to set it down are not wrong that carrying it indefinitely has costs.

**The community question:** If individuals from a destroyed city begin to identify primarily as Tepenian, does this dissolve the community? And if the community dissolves, is that a loss to be grieved or a natural conclusion to be honored? Some destroyed-city communities have a specific fear that their city will be "erased twice" — once by the war, and once by the gradual drift of their survivors into the wider Tepenian community.

**The generational question:** The children born after the destruction — and especially the grandchildren — have no personal memory of the city. They know it through stories and preserved materials. Are they "from" the destroyed city? Are they obligated to carry the identity? Do they have the right to decline it? Different communities answer this differently. Zukelli communities tend toward: the children are from Zukelli, full stop, because the community's continuity is the city's continuity. Casey communities, still working through the surprise of having been destroyed at all, tend toward a more genuinely unsettled answer — the question of obligation hasn't fully resolved for them the way it has for Zukelli.

**The anniversary question:** How long do you hold the anniversary? Every destroyed-city community maintains some form of observance around the date of destruction. But the observances change over generations — they attenuate, formalize, or sometimes intensify. The communities that intensify their anniversaries over time are sometimes criticized (by outsiders and occasionally by insiders) for cultivating grief rather than processing it. The communities that let the anniversary formalize into something more ceremonial and less raw are sometimes criticized for moving on. Neither criticism is entirely fair.

Each of the four destroyed-city communities is living through this question on its own timeline, and they are at different points in the negotiation. Belgrano is perhaps furthest along — its archival mission gives the community purpose that is forward-oriented even while it is backward-facing. Palmer City is perhaps most stuck — the founding-site identity makes the city difficult to set down even partially, because setting it down feels like abandoning a claim that nobody else will make if Palmer City doesn't. Casey is perhaps most conflicted, because the destruction was unexpected (Casey was not a primary target) and the community has not fully resolved whether to integrate that surprise into its identity or to treat it as an arbitrary wound with no meaning.

## The McMurdo Problem — Communities of Lost Function

**Origin cities:** Fort McMurdo (primary); Esperanza (secondary)

*Note 2026-07-04: Fort McMurdo's second interwar-era counterpart faction ("Fort McMurdo — Life at the Center of Everything," in `City_Origin_Factions_Second_Interwar.md`) describes the same city *before* it lost this function — worth reading alongside this one for the full arc from dominant hub to displaced community.*

Fort McMurdo was the de facto capital of real-world Antarctica. Everything went through it. For the first decades of Tepenian settlement, McMurdo's logistical heritage was the backbone of how the continent operated. Its residents knew this. Their identity was not just "we are from McMurdo" but "we are the people who kept everything running."

That function is gone.

The McMurdo refugee community carries a specific loss that is not the loss of a city but the loss of a role. The city still exists in memory. What doesn't exist anymore is the position it held — the logistical center, the place everything ran through. Other cities have absorbed those functions. The McMurdo community's expertise and institutional knowledge are real and valuable, but they are no longer uniquely necessary in the way they once were.

This produces a community organized around a kind of quiet professional grief alongside the geographic displacement grief. McMurdo people tend to find each other through work — through shared expertise in logistics, supply chain, Antarctic operational history — and the community is therefore more work-organized than most refugee communities, which tend to organize around neighborhood or city of origin.

The internal tension is between:

**The Institutionalists**, who believe the McMurdo tradition lives on as long as the knowledge is being applied somewhere, by someone who knows where it came from — who believe that function, not place, is what they are preserving.

**The Specificists**, who feel that decontextualized McMurdo knowledge is just logistics expertise, and that what they've actually lost is the specific thing that made it McMurdo — the scale, the centrality, the position in the network. They are not bitter about this, exactly. But they are precise about it.

Esperanza has a version of this. The Peninsula gateway function is gone. Esperanza was the arrival point, the city of first landing, the place where "hope" was not just a name but a function. Refugees no longer arrive through Esperanza. The Esperanza community's loss is therefore also partly a loss of function — the city of arrivals no longer receives anyone.
