## Overview

Fives retreat into the mind because, at some early point, the outside world felt either neglectful or
overwhelming — and the mind was the one place that felt genuinely safe and controllable. Both books converge on
the same underlying logic: having concluded that relationships can't be counted on to provide, a Five resolves
to need as little as possible from anyone, hoarding whatever time, energy, and privacy they have rather than
risking depletion by opening up to others. Knowledge becomes the substitute currency — mastery over some
specific domain offers the competence and control that connection was supposed to provide but didn't. The real
irony both books point to: no amount of expertise, however genuine, actually resolves the underlying fear,
since the fear was never really about knowledge in the first place.

**In the stories both books use as touchstones:** Homer's *Odyssey* gives Fives Hermes and Circe — both
keepers of guarded knowledge who share what they know only strategically, and only with those they judge
worthy of it. Circe transforms Odysseus's men into pigs (a symbol of greedy, unguarded appetite) but cannot
enchant Odysseus himself, protected by Hermes's countermeasure — the one man whose mind "no magic can enchant."
Having proven he can resist being consumed by greed and entrapment, Odysseus earns Circe's respect and her
guidance into the underworld: knowledge, rightly guarded and rightly used, becomes the very thing that makes
the next stage of the journey possible. Dante's *Inferno* gives Fives the Miserly, locked in an eternal circle
with their opposite number, the Prodigal, pushing enormous weights against each other while each side screams
at the other — "Why hoard?" against "Why waste?" Dante adds a pointed detail: the Miserly are literally
unrecognizable, their preoccupation with holding on having erased whatever made them individually visible in
life — a stark image of what happens when self-protection becomes so total it costs the self it was meant to
protect.
