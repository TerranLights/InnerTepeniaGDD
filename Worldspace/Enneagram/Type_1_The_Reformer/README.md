**Sources:** *The Wisdom of the Enneagram* (Riso & Hudson) — gold standard — cross-referenced with *The

**Also called:** The Teacher, The Activist, The Crusader, The Moralist, The Perfectionist, The Organizer.

**The Rational, Idealistic Type: Principled, Purposeful, Self-Controlled, and Perfectionistic.**

## Overview

Ones want to improve the world and hold themselves to that standard first. The name "Reformer" points at a
real, lived sense of mission — Ones across history have left comfortable lives because something felt like it
mattered more than comfort. Underneath the principled surface is a mechanism both books describe the same
way: an impulse rises, a strict internal critic intercepts and judges it, and only what survives judgment is
allowed through. Ones often talk themselves into believing they're purely rational, "head" people — but the
drive underneath is instinctual and passionate; the rationality is the control system built to manage it, not
the source. The result is someone who reads as controlled, orderly, even rigid, while privately experiencing
themselves as sitting on a lid they don't dare lift.

This is also, structurally, the archetype of the superego itself — the internalized parental voice of "right"
and "wrong" that every person carries to some degree, given its fullest, most defining expression in this one
type.

**In the stories both books use as touchstones:** Homer's *Odyssey* gives Ones the Phaeacians — a disciplined,
courteous, architecturally-perfect seafaring people who help Odysseus home, but whose very perfectionism reads
to others as "bloodless do-gooding," and whose fate (their ship turned to stone by an offended Poseidon) shows
that flawlessness is no protection against forces indifferent to virtue. Dante's *Inferno* gives Ones "the
Wrathful" — shades so consumed by the anger a One normally keeps locked down that they're left permanently
mud-drenched and biting each other in a swamp, a picture of what the type's core passion looks like with every
restraint finally gone.
