## Overview of this range

This part holds the last three of the book's nine type chapters, **Seven (The Enthusiast), Eight (The Leader) and Nine (The Peacemaker)**, in the same template as the earlier chapters: a one-page **profile** (Healthy, Average, Unhealthy, Key Motivations, Examples), an **Overview** (the type's place in its triad, its core problem, its parental orientation, its Jungian counterpart), the **nine Levels of Development** (healthy 1 to 3, average 4 to 6, unhealthy 7 to 9, each level named and described), **The Dynamics** (direction of disintegration, level by level, then direction of integration), **The Major Subtypes** (the two wings, each described healthy, average and unhealthy) and **Some Final Thoughts**. Seven closes the **Thinking Triad** (Five, Six, Seven): anxiety, here handled by fleeing outward into stimulation, with a fear of deprivation rooted in a disconnected nurturing-figure. Eight and Nine belong to the **Instinctive Triad** (Eight, Nine, One), whose shared themes are resistance, repressed aggression and the wish to keep the environment from affecting the self: Eights dominate it, Nines ignore it. Eight's parental orientation is "ambivalent" (a role complementary to the nurturing-figure); Nine's is a bond with both parents. The authors tie each type to a Jungian type (Seven: extroverted sensation; Eight: extroverted intuitive; Nine: introverted sensation) and end each chapter with an ironic reversal: each type brings about the thing it fears most. Citations are `[s.K]` (s.19 Seven, s.20 Eight, s.21 Nine). The chapters stress that the levels are tendencies along a continuum, not verdicts on persons. [s.19, s.20, s.21]

# CHAPTER 9 — TYPE SEVEN: THE ENTHUSIAST [s.19]

## The Seven in profile (the book's summary page) [s.19]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Highly responsive, excitable, enthusiastic about sensation and experience; the most extroverted type; stimuli bring immediate responses and everything is invigorating. Lively, vivacious, eager, spontaneous, resilient, cheerful. Easily become accomplished achievers, generalists who do many things well, multitalented; practical, productive, usually prolific, cross-fertilizing areas of interest. **At their best:** assimilate experiences in depth, becoming deeply grateful and appreciative; awed by the simple wonders of life, joyous and ecstatic; intimations of spiritual reality, of the boundless goodness of life. |
| Average | As appetites grow: acquisitive, materialistic, "worldly wise," constantly amusing themselves with new things and experiences (sophisticate, connoisseur, consumer); money, variety and trends matter. Hyperactive, unable to say no to themselves; uninhibited, doing and saying whatever comes to mind (storytelling, flamboyant exaggeration, wisecracking, performing). Fear boredom, so keep in perpetual motion but do too many things: superficial dilettantes. Conspicuous consumption and every form of excess; self-centered, greedy, never feeling they have enough; demanding, pushy, yet unsatisfied, crude, jaded; addictive, hardened, insensitive. |
| Unhealthy | Offensive and abusive in going after what they want; impulsive and infantile, they do not know when to stop; addictions and excesses leave them debauched, depraved, dissipated escapists. In flight from self they act out impulses rather than face anxiety or frustration: out of control, erratic mood swings, compulsive (manias). Finally energy and health are spent: claustrophobic, panic-stricken; often give up on themselves and life: deep depression and despair, self-destructive overdoses, impulsive suicide. |

**Key Motivations:** want to be happy and satisfied, to have a wide variety of experiences, to keep their options open, to enjoy life and amuse themselves, to escape anxiety. [s.19]

**Examples** (the book's list, abridged): John F. Kennedy, Leonard Bernstein, Steven Spielberg, Malcolm Forbes, Marianne Williamson, Elizabeth Taylor, Mozart, Arthur Rubinstein, Federico Fellini, Richard Feynman, Timothy Leary, Robin Williams, Jim Carrey, Bette Midler, Elton John, Liza Minnelli, Jack Nicholson, Joan Rivers, Howard Stern, Liberace, John Belushi; fictional "Auntie Mame" and "Martha" in *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?* [s.19]

## Overview of the Seven [s.19]

- **Opening epigraph (Mullen on Kierkegaard):** a life of "diverse hedonism" is empty even on its own terms, boredom being its unavoidable enemy; people reject the lesson because they tell themselves they would use pleasure with restraint; throwing oneself into indulgence says "all I am is a potential for pleasure," which no one can believe in earnest, so such a life rests on self-deception. The authors then distinguish three kinds of people given pleasures: the truly happy who know their blessing; those merely amused and distracted, grabbing at pleasure instead of experiencing it deeply; and those with everything who are bitter and disappointed. [s.19]
- **The universal problem the Seven exemplifies:** how to "use" the world to best advantage: enjoy pleasure without living for pleasure; possess the good things of life without being insensitive to others; live in the world without getting lost in it. [s.19]
- **In the Thinking Triad** (with Five and Six). The Seven's strongest asset, an agile, quick, mercurial mind, is also the root of the problem: the more anxious they are, the more their minds race and spin them into widening unfocused activity. In Sevens thinking is tightly linked to doing: an appealing idea is acted on as soon as possible, so the mind runs "about two steps ahead." Unlike Fives, Sevens focus thought on this world and what they want to do in it; anticipating possibilities feels good and wards off painful emotion. They react to everything with immediacy, so doing breeds more exciting ideas and more doing. [s.19]
- **Experience is their guide.** Everything desirable exists outside themselves, in the world of things and experiences; identity and self-esteem rest on a steady stream of stimulating ideas and impressions. They are experience-oriented rather than introspective or person-oriented: extroverted, practical, urbane. [s.19]
- **Healthy versus average versus unhealthy in one line:** healthy Sevens focus on producing something in the environment and so do many things well; average Sevens shift from productivity to possession and consumption, stay busy to keep stimulation high, and so cannot appreciate what they have; unhealthy Sevens are dissipated escapists acting impulsively and out of control. [s.19]
- **Jungian correspondence:** Sevens correspond to the **extroverted sensation type**. The authors quote Jung (value of objects lies in the intensity of the sensation they give; unsurpassed realism; "concrete enjoyment" as the whole aim) and say it fits Sevens exceptionally well: no type is more practical or widely accomplished, but if appetites get the better of control they consume more than they can appreciate. [s.19]
- **Anxiety and insecurity (the Triad's shared problem), Seven form.** Fives fear they cannot cope with the external environment and retreat from it; Sevens are "almost the exact opposite": fearful of coping with their *inner* environment (grief, loss, anxiety), so they flee outward. They control anxiety by keeping attention full of exciting things to look forward to (a new adventure, book, workshop, relationship). Introspection draws them inward and makes them more anxious; extroversion represses anxiety, at least temporarily. [s.19]
- **The anticipation trap.** The more they fill their minds with anticipated future fun, the less they are present in the current experience, so it cannot satisfy them. Example: someone at the pyramids thinking of dinner in Cairo and of showing the photos at home "misses" the pyramids. Less enjoyment brings anxiety, which brings more activity; the vicious circle becomes addictive staying-in-motion. The more they flee, the bigger the thrills needed. Their happiness is precarious because they neither interiorize experiences nor control appetites; eventually "nothing makes them happy," and they become enraged and terrified, feeling life has cruelly deprived them. [s.19]
- **Parental orientation.** As young children Sevens were **disconnected from the nurturing-figure** (the person who mirrored them, cared for them, gave affection and sense of personal value; often the mother, but a father or older sibling in some families). They felt frustrated and unable to depend on getting what they needed, so they compensate by getting things for themselves. The figures usually did not intend this; poverty, war, orphaning, long illness, absence at a critical stage, or a child's naturally large need for contact and stimulation may have shaken the expectation that good things would be given. **Fear of deprivation** becomes the fundamental motivation, and the flip side is demanding that all desires be satisfied: possessing what they think will make them happy symbolizes the nurturing and well-being always just out of reach. [s.19]
- **Appetites and aggressions.** Average Sevens want instant gratification and few limits; the lengths they go to make them "aggressive personalities," though insecure, so aggression is enlisted against anxiety. They put others in the position of having to set limits on them; self-control comes from outside (people who must say no, or reality). Frustration enrages them because it raises memories of childhood deprivation. Healthy Sevens serve genuine needs rather than every desire, are productive, accomplished and unusually happy because they truly assimilate experience. Unhealthy Sevens become greedy and selfish, like addicts needing a bigger "fix," and end in panic because nothing inside anchors them: the type that affirms life most when healthy becomes the one most terrified by the conditions of life. [s.19]

## Analyzing the Healthy Seven [s.19]

**Level 1: The Ecstatic Appreciator.** [note: the source text heads this "Level 2: The Ecstatic Appreciator" by an apparent typo; it is the first level, the very healthy Seven, and the next heading repeats "Level 2".] [s.19]
- Faith in reality lets them contact the environment without trying to make it supply anything. They stay present moment by moment instead of escaping into the mind's chatter; each moment becomes nourishment and profound joy; whatever life holds is enough *if assimilated*. Reality deeply experienced makes them ecstatic and impels them to affirm life as it is ("I love life unconditionally!"—Rubinstein, quoted).
- They let the mystery and precariousness of existence register, and celebrate rather than fear its fragility; they may perceive a metaphysical reality for the first time, something holy to revere, and respond with wonder, thanks and praise.
- Tremendous gratitude starting with the fact of existence; they find something good in everything; the inner or spiritual life becomes real; even the dark side and death lose terror. Paradox: when they embrace life most, they are least attached to it.
- Everything is a gift, not a thing existing for their gratification; they stop putting conditions on life ("I'll be happy when I get that new car"). Faith in the essential goodness of life ("consider the lilies of the field"); whatever they have is enough to make them profoundly happy if used rightly; real goods (things of enduring value) rather than apparent goods.
- They are continually surprised by reality, which cannot be exhausted; the ecstasy can recur; "They cannot be deprived." [s.19]

**Level 2: The Free-Spirited Optimist.** [s.19]
- Some anxiety enters: they begin to lose faith in life's abundance and fear there will not be enough; they begin to *anticipate* future experience instead of fully assimilating the present, one step removed from immediacy and less satisfied. (Still extremely positive and energetic.)
- Open to experience as no other type; the sensory world excites them; the charged tangible world closes off access to unconscious experience since they respond instantly to external reality.
- They make psychological happiness (euphoria) their life's goal; delightful, vivacious company; contagious high spirits ("Energy is eternal delight."—Blake).
- Remarkably quick, avid minds: curiosity like Fives'; with intelligence, gifts for languages, wit and repartee, grasp of form and structure (music, color, shape, design); excellent memory (everything registers "like light on film"); they learn fast, which later becomes a problem (they may fail to develop gifts when things come too easily). Their inner life is "a catalogue of their experiences"; they think about doing things rather than abstract concepts.
- Positive, youthful, resilient ("like a phoenix"; "make lemonade"). Free-spirited, spontaneous, not wed to plans ("see what comes up"), adventurous, able to laugh at themselves and not self-conscious when they do something badly. Enormous physical vitality; every contact with the world invigorates them and enlarges their capacity for more. [s.19]

**Level 3: The Accomplished Generalist.** [s.19]
- They worry they will not sustain happiness; fear of deprivation is near, so they become realistic and pragmatic: to have freedom and means they must be productive, and at Level 3 their creative output is amazing.
- Vitality and enthusiasm plus practical sense make them extraordinarily productive, multitalented generalists, "bridge builders" between areas of experience, cross-fertilizing interests. As a group, "probably the most capable of the personality types"; often more accomplished than their years suggest; extroversion means no introspective rumination to stall action; they acquire skills "as if by osmosis."
- Examples in the text: several languages, instruments, a profession, cooking, skiing, art and theater; a Seven scanning want ads who sees a word-processing requirement will learn word processing. Skills beget skills (a pianist accompanies a singer, becomes a vocal coach, then directs an opera company; a storyteller to siblings becomes a writer, then screenwriter or actor).
- A lasting pleasure: introducing their enthusiasms (a painting, dish, music, idea) to others. They accomplish much because they prioritize, set limits and make necessary sacrifices; but anxiety starts to cause restlessness and the fear that what they are doing is not satisfying enough. [s.19]

## Analyzing the Average Seven [s.19]

**Level 4: The Experienced Sophisticate.** [s.19]
- Fearing that focusing on one or two things means missing others, they want more of what made them happy ("eyes bigger than their stomachs"): all appetites increase; they try everything at least once so as not to feel deprived. The difference from healthy Sevens: interest moves from producing and creating to consuming and entertaining themselves; they place a premium on freedom and lament there is not enough time; they want more experiences and possessions, believing more will make them happier.
- Core problem: anxiety of missing something more exciting ("the grass is always greener"); the party image of two overheard conversations: they try to follow both and get less from each. They do not linger long enough to assimilate anything.
- They usually label the anxiety **"boredom"** and talk about boredom more than any other type; "bored" often means too little stimulation to keep painful feelings from emerging, so they "change the scenery."
- They accumulate experience: the people to consult about movies, restaurants, workshops; love to talk and tell stories; travel and compile lists of places visited; buy more books than they finish (stacks of partially read books).
- Still productive but less focused; fill schedules, keep backup plans; good at multitasking and enjoy juggling variety.
- In some, the quest becomes love of acquisition, but the acquiring is the pleasure (shopping, catalogues, fantasizing about the item), and the object loses its excitement once bought. With funds they become worldly sophisticates and connoisseurs with "class"; with less money they acquire less but are no less acquisitive. Jung is quoted again on the lower levels of the type: a lover of tangible reality, jolly or a refined aesthete, "good company," conspicuously well adjusted to reality; what comes from inside seems "morbid and suspect."
- Bons vivants: banter, gaiety, excellent hosts (cocktail and dinner parties), know food and caterers, fashionable "within their means (or a little beyond)," their dream is to have so much money they need not worry about it. Style varies with money, education, group (theater versus movies; Europe three times a year versus once; nightclubs versus ball games and malls; "diamonds versus rhinestones"). Their motto "I want it all!" yet no prize delivers what it promises. Danger: appetites outgrow discrimination; they become consumers instead of connoisseurs. [s.19]

**Level 5: The Hyperactive Extrovert.** [s.19]
- They fear any moment in which nothing is going on, because anxiety may register; sophistication gives way to uninhibited constant activity to maintain stimulation and therefore their sense of self: "I do, therefore I am." Perpetual motion, "a centrifugal flight from themselves."
- They cannot say no to anything; cram a day full; everything done fast (eat, talk, think fast); life at 120 mph, so 100 would be boring. The pleasure principle governs: if not fun, they lose interest and move on. Gregarious, outspoken, flamboyant; favorite adjectives "wonderful," "fabulous," "sensational."
- A distinct **oral quality**: talking, eating, drinking, smoking, laughing, wisecracking, gossiping, often all at once; the most talkative type; irreverent, joking about everything as defense; many professional comedians are Sevens; their comedy springs from underlying insecurity and anxiety; nothing subtle; may shock but refreshing to some.
- Poor conversationalists: want to be the center of attention, do not listen ("Oh, that's nice. Now, let me tell you what I did today."), interrupt, jump topics; even heated arguments create stimulation.
- They are working harder to repress growing pain; they also want the environment and other people to entertain them and will "jump-start the environment" with razzle-dazzle.
- Hyperactivity breeds superficiality: not perceptive about experiences (one-line reviews: "The food was all right, but I've had better"). Capacity for accomplishment decays into facility: **dilettantes** (sing without practicing, French then Russian, needlepoint then painting then photography then piano), "jacks-of-all-trades" and masters of none; they quit when effort or concentration is needed.
- **Tangential thinking:** repressed anxiety turns quickness into ricocheting free association; focus and attention span suffer; good ideas hatched but few realized unless someone else manages them. They know on some level they are scattering their force.
- **Neediness:** they fear being alone (feelings that might emerge; their banter would be strange without an audience, revealing how hollow it has become). Work suffers (no concentration for routine; long lunches, shopping, vacations from exhaustion). They cannot sit still, even on vacation (a seaside cabin would drive them crazy "unless the cabin were in downtown Rio"); stereo and television on together with the telephone, sometimes even asleep.
- **Assimilation is largely missing:** they count movies, not what the movies meant; ironically they become less interesting and more infantile; relentless activity is tiresome to others. [s.19]

**Level 6: The Excessive Hedonist.** [s.19]
- The pressure of grief and fear nearly erupts; extreme anxiety about any frustration; they demand more of everything, now ("I want more—and I want it now!"), no tolerance for discomfort or inconvenience; greedy, pushy, insisting others cater to them. (Not content with a Cadillac, they need a Rolls and a Jaguar.)
- Money is highly valued; they spend mostly on themselves, running up debt, since credit cards remove delay; those without money make acquiring it top priority, possibly by marrying money; love does not stand in the way.
- Conspicuous consumption and ostentatious display; **"Greed is the capital sin of the Seven,"** shown as gluttonous pursuit of pleasure to the point of obscenity; sophisticate becomes garish and vulgar. Excess touches everything including health and youth measures (tanning to leather, cosmetic surgery, fat farms, tummy tucks, drying out at a sanatorium; women overdress and overdo makeup and jewelry; men look nouveau riche and flashy). Excessiveness is a hallmark because they consciously put no limits on themselves; profligate; "I have mine, so what do I care about anyone else?"
- They still need people (cannot stand being alone) but others are incidental: relationships are kept while they add to enjoyment and dropped without regret (marriages last a year or two); jealous of those with more; self-centered; do not want others dependent on them; "hard as nails" under the veneer.
- Unwilling to face consequences: leave messes for others to clean up; deny responsibility ("It's not my problem"); resistance to their own pain makes them insensitive to others'; guilt and remorse are bad feelings to avoid. Poor parents (children interfere with lifestyle; may choose abortion or adoption).
- Because they are used to immoderation, nothing works except in excessive dosage: promiscuity, drugs (cocaine and alcohol named), costly toys; addictive: dependent on any source of stimulation (television, going out, sex, drugs).
- The pity: they lack real happiness; hardened and insatiable ("literally 'unsatisfiable'"); pursuit of pleasure is now a flight from pain; others notice the anguish and desperation driving their "fun." [s.19]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Seven [s.19]

**Level 7: The Impulsive Escapist.** [s.19]
- They notice (or fail to heed) that excess brings misery; if avoidance of anxiety wins, or pain already exceeds what they can cope with, they discharge anxiety by acting it out in aggressive, irresponsible ways. Not reflective, they cannot work out why they are unhappy; they strike out at whoever seems to have denied them.
- Continued motion is now flight "like a surfer riding the crest of a wave before it breaks"; **dissipated escapists**; anything goes: sexual escapades, drinking and drug binges, increasingly depraved practices seeking new thrills; they cannot and do not want to center themselves or make real contact. Even the appearance of enjoyment is gone: jokes have an angry edge; hostile, bitter, joyless, going through the motions; more anxious about being alone, pressuring others to join their debauchery ("If you don't want to get drunk with me, get the hell out of here.").
- **Addiction:** "uppers" to enjoy themselves and "downers" to relax and lose consciousness without anxiety; by now they have almost certainly abused every stimulant and tranquilizer within reach.
- They fear **falling asleep** (the "window of vulnerability" when the unconscious opens): run to exhaustion, stay up days, use sleeping pills or alcohol, then jolt themselves awake with caffeine or stronger stimulants. Like Sixes and Fives, unhealthy Sevens become hypervigilant guards, but the frontier watched is their own subconscious.
- Slightest frustration brings coarse, insulting, ill-considered rage; spoiled-child tantrums and "scenes"; no capacity to inhibit impulses (throw things when angry, burst into tears when sad); acting out often gets their way (others embarrassed into silence) and discharges tension, but reinforces infantile impulsiveness; few can stand being around them, which infuriates them further.
- Defenses fail and grief emerges in significant periods: they get **depressed**, acutely, because they have never learned to cope with it (not necessarily as chronically as other types). They see nothing romantic in suffering and want to reconnect with the environment quickly, but may be too distraught to do so in balance and become manic. [s.19]

**Level 8: The Manic Compulsive.** [s.19]
- Fearing loss of the very capacity for pleasure, they deteriorate from impulsiveness to **mania**: willing to do anything to feel something; wild activity to keep functioning and avoid the full brunt of anxiety and shame; may seem somewhat functional for a while but are extremely unstable in behavior and mood ("as erratic as the path of a tornado"); moods swing from belligerence to tearful remorse to elation within minutes; trying to reason with them or limit their "high spirits" produces unpredictable, dangerous reactions.
- **Delusional elation:** unnaturally high, talking loudly and rapidly "as if on amphetamines," believing they can do anything, squandering money on grandiose plans, using dangerous doses of drugs and alcohol.
- **Compulsive activities** to ward off depression: shopping, gambling, binge drinking and drugs, eating, sex; accidents (drunk driving, overdoses, daredevil escapades); formal manias such as kleptomania.
- They do not see the delusion because their only defense is to act anxiety out before it registers; completing plans is not the point, "having avenues of escape is." Minds like "beads of water skittering on a hot iron"; slowing down would mean serious depression: this is the **manic-depressive defense**. Compulsiveness gets them into trouble; others and reality frustrate them and close the routes of escape. [s.19]

**Level 9: The Panic-Stricken "Hysteric."** [s.19]
- Having "consumed" everything, they have nothing to ground themselves; anxiety finally breaks into consciousness and they become hysterical in the popular sense: panicked, trembling, unable to act, "as if pursued by a raging beast." Hardened, worldly people are devastated; defenses crumble instantly; the unconscious (unknown territory) engulfs them; grief, guilt, trauma, desolation, confusion and anguish crash in, and having spent their energy avoiding them they cannot cope: paralyzing terror, afraid to move or think.
- A waking version of the nightmare; normal people wake and re-repress, but they are already awake: claustrophobic, terrified of annihilation, of going mad, of endless torment.
- Reckless activity has pushed bodies to breakdown (accidents, drug and alcohol abuse, sexually transmitted disease, overtaxing); escape by leaping into action is curtailed. The source of terror remains unnameable; as they deteriorated they were "buying time" and now see they were wrong. [s.19]

## The Dynamics of the Seven [s.19]

**Direction of Disintegration: the Seven goes to One** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Starting at Level 4, stressed Sevens act out average to unhealthy One traits: [s.19]

| Seven level | What going to One looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | They recognize they must focus energy to keep the means to enjoy themselves and begin to impose limits and work harder, but from the wrong motivation, so they feel frustrated and rebellious and often do more of what they tried to limit. Like average Ones, they educate others about experiences (zealous reports on a workshop, warnings about a poor restaurant) and enthusiasm can turn into debate; they force themselves to stay on track (exercise, homework) out of "should," feeling more frustrated and sure they are missing something better. |
| 5 | Scattered, restless; to rein themselves in they become self-controlled and rigid; swing from boisterous irreverence to grim seriousness; brusque, impersonal, impatient with others' "incompetence"; self-control does not work because it resists real feelings; the only feeling that emerges is anger and resentment that needs are not met. |
| 6 | Highly critical and sarcastic toward people who do not drop everything for them; perfectionistic about service and their own accomplishments (judging efforts inadequate before trying), and about the environment: nothing is satisfactory; they scold, nitpick, make condescending remarks. |
| 7 | Bitterness and resentment worsen; vitriol and rage when confronted about irresponsible lifestyle; inflexible about their activities; intolerant of others' quirks and weaknesses despite no self-control; a "nasty misanthrope." |
| 8 | A last-ditch effort to regain control: obsessive activity or fetishistic belief; fixation on an idea they believe will rescue them, or on a person or object believed to cause their distress; usually delusional and only buys time. The chosen rallying point lies outside themselves and becomes a lightning rod for their destructive impulses and for hatred of those who frustrated them. |
| 9 | Energy once invested in pursuing happiness implodes into a core of **hatred** of reality and of those who frustrated them; an interest in a person or thing becomes obsession, with a rationale to root out the source of their pain; punitive impulses and vile condemnation. Dangerous because impulsive, violent and thinking is disturbed: they may kill or injure the very persons they fix on, or abuse children or spouses; if they succeed, the manic defense may give way to severe depression and suicide. |

**Direction of Integration: the Seven goes to Five** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Integrating Sevens, already psychologically balanced, no longer fear deprivation; they become involved with things **in depth**, internalizing experiences, which creates the anchors for stability and security. Gratitude for life leads them to want to know more about what made them happy; attention moves from themselves (experience and immediate happiness) to the world; they respect the integrity of everything, as existing for purposes other than their gratification; no longer consumers but "contemplators"; gratitude blossoms into wonder and curiosity about creation. They concentrate on experience and gain vastly in satisfaction, becoming more expert, penetrating reality and letting reality penetrate them. They keep their enthusiasm and productivity and may become more productive by contributing something original. [s.19]

## The Major Subtypes of the Seven [s.19]

**The Seven with a Six-wing: "The Entertainer."** [s.19]
- Tension of types: Sixes are people-oriented, Sevens oriented to things and experiences and able to fulfill their own needs; both have dependencies (Sixes on approval and security from others, Sevens on the environment to make them happy). They seek satisfaction themselves and use others as extra sources of stimulation. Perhaps the most gregarious and outgoing subtype; more relationship-oriented than the Eight-wing, which is more experience-oriented. (Examples include Robin Williams, Spielberg, Rubinstein, Mozart, Bernstein, Elizabeth Taylor, Feynman, Leary, Midler, Elton John, Jim Carrey, Mel Brooks, Liberace, John Belushi, "Miss Piggy.")
- *Healthy:* highly productive with infectious joie de vivre; playful, childlike, engaging, silly; keep a certain innocence and belief in life's goodness; creative and entertaining; a more positive outlook (like Nines) than the Eight-wing; quick minds, inspired humor; can use the Six's discipline, cooperation and organizational ability; essentially assertive but want to be liked; generous with money (socializing, parties, travel); soft, sweet side; a kaleidoscope: ingratiating and sassy, vulnerable and resilient, spontaneous and dependable, adult and childlike.
- *Average:* the Six's fears feed the Seven's scatter; more insecurity and more awareness of anxiety; nervous, flighty, revved up, fidgety; weaker follow-through; defensive, impulsive; want approval, fear anxiety and being alone; high expectations of partners; fall in love easily and out of it once romance wears off; always in a relationship or seeking one; "cheerleaders" who become excessive and tiresome; when expectations of stimulation are unmet, Sixish sulking and pessimism emerge; lower average: caught between the Seven's wish to move on to "greener pastures" and the Six's fear of losing safe connections; gregarious but insecure about what people think, impulsive but anxious about decisions, extravagant yet anxious about money; increasingly insensitive without knowing it, demanding that others help them through anxiety. The Six-wing softens aggression but reinforces anxiety; strong propensity for substance abuse (as in the Six with a Seven-wing).
- *Unhealthy:* the Seven's erratic mania plus the Six's fearful clinging; a restless search for "playmates" as "coconspirators" to keep them distracted; acute inferiority and anxiety; tearfully but obnoxiously demand others solve their problems; stay on the phone or in nightspots night after night; if this fails, hysterical and helpless, thrashing and lashing out, driving others away and calling them back; highly prone to self-destruction and dramatic, masochistic episodes such as suicide attempts; eventually addictions or collapse.

**The Seven with an Eight-wing: "The Realist."** [s.19]
- Very aggressive combination, in two ways: the demands made on the environment and the ego strength to enforce them; "No one frustrates people of this subtype without hearing about it." Extremely assertive, industrious, goal-oriented, pragmatic, ambitious; intense active lifestyle; less worried about having others along. (Examples include JFK, Malcolm Forbes, Cary Grant, Fellini, Joan Collins, Joan Rivers, Noel Coward, Cole Porter, Larry King, Howard Stern, "Martha.")
- *Healthy:* exuberant, materialists "in the broadest sense"; the Seven's intelligence with the Eight's daring and drive often yields material success; self-confidence, willpower, self-assertion to overcome obstacles; capacity for leadership (quick minds, brilliant personal style); adult, earthy, businesslike, persistent, tenacious, "bemused worldliness"; strategic thinkers who can rapidly reorganize internal and external resources toward a goal.
- *Average:* more practical, worldly, cosmopolitan than the Six-wing; multitasking or "multi-careering"; the Eight-wing adds workaholism; seek intense experiences for the adrenaline; stronger desire for possessions or "toys" (subtext: "I'm worth it!"); focus on activities more than socializing; hard-nosed realists about relationships, not afraid to be alone, clear about needs; directness verging on crude bluntness in lower levels, "pushing to the front of the line," sometimes without regard for law or morality; not conflict-avoidant, stimulated by confrontation; jaded, world-weary rather than childishly hyperenthusiastic; stronger-willed, resisting control, so somewhat less prone to substance abuse; more hardened and willful.
- *Unhealthy:* compulsive mania plus the Eight's destructive antisocial tendencies; dangerous scenes (gambling, underworld involvement, extreme sexual practices, "living on the edge") deplete physical, emotional and financial resources; ruthless toward whoever has what they want; unhealthy Sevens fly out of control and unhealthy Eights overestimate power, so extremely reckless and dangerous; physically destructive acting out; erratic behavior leads to death or mental breakdown.

## Some Final Thoughts (Seven) [s.19]
Unhealthy Sevens bring about what they most fear: they are deprived of happiness not because the world frustrates them but because they have not trusted life enough and consumed experience superficially. It is "exceedingly rare" to find Sevens actually deprived, at least for long, since they are aggressive and usually get what they want; the same aggression drives them to excess and ruin. If Sevens do not "experience their experiences," what they do is "worthless and wasteful"; unless they internalize experience in the depths of their souls they bar themselves from life's most sublime experiences: "trade true gold for lead." [s.19]

# CHAPTER 10 — TYPE EIGHT: THE LEADER [s.20]

[note: in this edition's chapter heading and text, Type Eight is titled "The Leader"; the repository's other Enneagram files use "The Challenger." The title is reproduced here as the book gives it.]

## The Eight in profile (the book's summary page) [s.20]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Self-assertive, self-confident, strong: able to stand up for what they need and want; resourceful "can do" attitude and passionate inner drive. Decisive, authoritative, commanding: the natural leader others look up to; take initiative, make things happen, champion people; providing, protective, honorable; carry others with their strength. **At their best:** self-restrained and magnanimous, merciful and forbearing, mastering self through self-surrender to a higher authority; courageous, willing to put self in serious jeopardy for their vision and lasting influence; may achieve true heroism and historical greatness. |
| Average | Self-sufficiency, financial independence and having enough resources are central: enterprising, pragmatic "rugged individualists," wheeler-dealers; risk-taking, hardworking, denying their own emotional needs. Begin to dominate the environment and others, wanting to feel others are behind them; the "boss" whose word is law: swaggering, boastful, forceful, expansive; proud, egocentric, imposing will and vision, not treating others as equals. Highly combative and intimidating: confrontational, belligerent, adversarial; everything a test of wills; threats and reprisals to get obedience and keep others off-balance and insecure. |
| Unhealthy | Defying all control: ruthless, dictatorial, "might makes right"; the criminal and outlaw, renegade and con artist; hardhearted, immoral, potentially violent. Delusional ideas of power, invincibility and ability to prevail: megalomania; feel omnipotent, invulnerable; recklessly overextend. If in danger they may brutally destroy everything that has not conformed to their will rather than surrender; vengeful, barbaric, murderous; sociopathic tendencies. |

**Key Motivations:** want to be self-reliant, to resist their weakness, to have an impact on the environment, to assert themselves, to stay in control, to prevail over others, to be invincible. [s.20]

**Examples** (abridged): Martin Luther King Jr., Franklin D. Roosevelt, Lyndon Johnson, Gorbachev, Gurdjieff, Picasso, Wagner, Indira Gandhi, Brando, John Wayne, Hemingway, Lee Iacocca, Donald Trump, Frank Sinatra, Bette Davis, Fidel Castro, Saddam Hussein, Napoleon, Jim Jones; fictional "Don Vito Corleone." [s.20]

## Overview of the Eight [s.20]

- **Opening epigraph (Galbraith, *The Anatomy of Power*):** power is pursued for its own sake as well as for interests; the exercise of power is profoundly enjoyed in all societies (rituals of obeisance, precedence, commands, "this is what we'll do"); a sense of self-actuated worth derives from the context and exercise of power; Hazlitt: "The love of power is the love of ourselves." The authors add that power cannot be described without wandering into leadership, authority, will, courage, self-reliance and destructiveness, and ask whether it is proper to call the use of power one agrees with healthy; they say Eight is "the personality type which most exemplifies power": courage, will, self-reliance, leadership, authority, self-assertion, "and the dark side of power, the ability to destroy what power has created." [s.20]
- **In the Instinctive Triad** (Eight, Nine, One). All three keep the environment from affecting them: **Eights by dominating it, Nines by ignoring it, Ones by striving to perfect it.** Eights are the most openly aggressive type, with a powerful connection to instinctive drives (energy, confidence, desire to impact the world); among the easiest to identify and the most difficult to deal with. Healthy: use will to remake the environment constructively. Unhealthy: power deteriorates into prevailing over others at any cost. [s.20]
- **Theme of the Triad: resistance.** Eights' form is conflict with the environment: the world is struggle and endurance, and mettle must be continually tested to survive; this yields powerful assertiveness and a sturdy confidence; "more steely determination and single-mindedness than any other type." Unseen, their willfulness can become immensely destructive, galvanize others against them, and make their fear of being rendered helpless in a harsh world come true. [s.20]
- **Jungian correspondence: extroverted intuitive type.** The quoted Jung passage: a keen nose for the new and in the making; stable conditions suffocate him; little regard for others' welfare; often seen as an unscrupulous adventurer; tycoons, entrepreneurs, speculators, politicians; with good intentions "he can render exceptional service as the initiator or promoter of new enterprises," "natural champion of all minorities with a future," can "make" men by intuitive diagnosis of their potentials, unrivaled at inspiring courage though he may drop the idea tomorrow. The authors read this as: if not too egocentric, Eights' goals benefit others (they may build skyscrapers, cities or nations); natural leaders; historical greatness if goals reach beyond themselves; but Eights tend to become egocentric and, even at average levels, pit themselves against others as if others' welfare means their own must suffer ("only one person in charge"). Both ends of the spectrum are extreme: no type can exert as much constructive influence, and none can so completely misuse power as unhealthy Eights. [s.20]
- **Aggression and repression (Triad's shared problem).** Aggressive impulses are totally repressed (Nine), sublimated into idealistic work (One), or forcefully expressed (Eight). All three think nothing is wrong with them; problems lie in the environment (dominate / ignore / improve); repression protects them from anxiety over consequences and self-doubt, simplifying life for them but not for others. [s.20]
- **What Eights repress: vulnerability and fear.** Driven by fear of being dominated, harmed or controlled; the world is "dog-eat-dog" and they intend not to be eaten, so they toughen themselves and repress tenderness and vulnerability. As children they are usually very sensitive, but their exuberance is resisted or feared by adults; peers and adults attack them; they learn to armor themselves at the cost of sensitivity and gentleness. Most recall a **pivotal childhood crisis** in which they realized they could no longer afford to be open, and feel it as one of the greatest tragedies of their lives. They repress softness, need for affection and the ability to ask for help, above all their fear; they confront fears directly through challenges (heights: mountain climbing and skydiving; animals: hunting and wilderness trekking): "the counterphobic approach to life par excellence"; irony: fearing control, they end up controlled by the fear. [s.20]
- **Cost to relationships.** Healthy Eights are big-hearted and jovial; fearful ones believe they cannot let others close, tell themselves they do not need people and that others stay only because the Eight has something they need; they may know people need them but not believe people love them, and fear admitting how much they want and love others. [s.20]
- **Parental orientation: ambivalent to the nurturing-figure.** Eights neither bonded and identified strongly (like Threes) nor separated entirely (like Sevens). They kept a connection by functioning in a role **complementary** to the nurturing-figure, who "owned" motherhood qualities (warmth, caring, nurturance, approval, gentleness, sensitivity); they took the complementary "patriarchal" role: "the strong one," the little protector others turn to in a crisis, especially. They identified completely with it, feeling that giving it up would lose identity and any hope of love. Like Twos and Fives (the other "ambivalent" types), Eights feel survival depends on fulfilling the role: Twos must selflessly nurture, Fives have no role and must find one, Eights must be the decisive, strong one, indifferent to hardship and suffering; as fear grows, roles become prisons. [s.20]
- **Family conditions.** Dysfunctional or dangerous childhoods demand toughness: a tough shell of aggressive defenses so no one can reach the soft person inside. Serious abuse produces constant anticipation of rejection and betrayal; unlike Sixes (who also have trust issues), Eights do not believe they can rely on anyone or anything outside themselves; either no one to turn to, or they pitted themselves against authority figures (parents, teachers, older kids, police); "The buck stops here." If there was some warmth and mutual support, the adult Eight takes a strongly protective role toward the few trusted; if little, an "every man for himself" attitude (looking out for number one is a full-time job; caring too much is a survival risk). The type shows how natural qualities (high energy, endurance, willpower) plus a family constellation crystallize identity; encouraged, they yield constructive, empowering people with a legacy; twisted by abuse, they yield vengeance and destructiveness. [s.20]

## Analyzing the Healthy Eight [s.20]

**Level 1: The Magnanimous Heart.** [s.20]
- Big-hearted, compassionate; surrender their willfulness to serve something greater than their ambition; truly selfless without being passive (retaining full energy), acting without self-interest; see what the greater good requires and do it.
- Learn not to act automatically on the impulse to self-assert; become internally quiet and wait for a deeper, truer impulse not based on fear and reaction; master themselves and passions; strength proved by not acting when they could act forcefully. "Paradoxically, Eights never seem as strong as when they act with self-restraint": forbearing, "magnanimous dominion rather than domination."
- Not pushovers; know they are free at the deepest level; realistic about asking for help, generous in giving it, which lifts burdens and releases gusto and love of life.
- Self-mastery shows as courage, physical and moral, possibly at risk to their lives: heroes others respect. Charismatic, with an aura of self-mastery and compassion that draws people for guidance, safety, protection; their largeness of spirit "uplifts and ennobles everyone." Past their egos they actualize an objective value (achieving peace, building schools, helping concretely). Their greatness lies in alleviating others' burdens; benefactors to circles, countries or the world; people give them near-worshipful loyalty, though the Eights remain unaffected, with innocence, simplicity, purity of spirit, forgiveness, gentleness, deep faith in life, and contact with profound truth.
- They may be great even if they do not succeed: their example inspires millions to carry on; if they die early people feel their protectors are taken away; no type has so elemental an effect on others. Heroic Eights achieve immortality in hearts and minds. [s.20]

**Level 2: The Self-Confident Person.** [s.20]
- If they succumb to fear of being harmed or controlled, they protect themselves and become self-reliant, asserting themselves against the environment and reinforcing willpower; want to be "master of their fates" with freedom and space; almost never doubt their ability to overcome obstacles.
- Sense of self comes from the full weight of their will meeting the world's challenges; solid; comfortable struggling with adversity and coming back stronger ("That which does not destroy me makes me stronger."—Nietzsche, quoted); stand up for rights and needs; resist pressure; turn setbacks into opportunities; rather than ask why something cannot be done, they feel confident they can do it. Seldom suffer self-doubt, anxiety, insecurity or excessive introspection; nothing subtle or indirect; they assert at every opportunity.
- Highly developed **intuition** focused externally: "hunches"; see possibilities in people and situations (junk garage into something useful; burned-out tenement into a home or office building; a struggling person into a leader); excellent judges of character, sensing dishonesty "a mile away." Resourceful, self-motivated, take the initiative; they are "the only personality type which has such inner solidity." [s.20]

**Level 3: The Constructive Leader.** [s.20]
- Having identified as strong and independent they fear ever being weak, timid or dependent, so they take on challenges to prove it; people naturally turn to them for leadership: masterliness, authority, decisiveness, a sense of honor; trusted protectors and providers, "empowering coaches"; their egos serve something outside themselves; when they are in charge others do not mind because Eights are honorable, resolute, fair and persuasive (Truman quoted: leadership is getting people to do what they don't want to do, and like it).
- Decision-making: direct and impassioned; not afraid to take an unpopular stand; take full responsibility and "take the heat." Respect matters to them; honorable and trustworthy (a handshake or word is sacred; deals closed on a handshake); they quickly end dealings with the dishonorable. **Injustice affects them viscerally** (compare Ones, who evaluate by an internalized code; Eights respond from the gut and swiftly, e.g., stopping a mugging).
- Vision: use intuition constructively (buildings, public works, parks, social programs) or to build up individuals through challenges and incentives ("If you learn how to play the guitar, I'll buy you a good one"; "If you can take care of this arm of the business, I'll promote you to vice president"). Not threatened by others' strength; value teamwork and delegation; build strength in their children "like a lioness nudging her cubs"; authoritative rather than authoritarian.
- They want to leave a legacy, a positive impact on people, community or the world; they feel they have a destiny; the trust placed in them is both satisfaction and burden.
- The authors summarize good leadership, the traits of the healthy Eight: give a clear, worthwhile direction and the means; create and maintain a stable social order at any scale (family, corporation, nation); inspire work for something larger (winning a war, launching a space station, building a city); know how to create a community or a people and sometimes become its symbol; build others' self-esteem and courage; accept ultimate responsibility and the consequences of failure. [s.20]

## Analyzing the Average Eight [s.20]

**Level 4: The Enterprising Adventurer.** [s.20]
- Hidden doubts about "pulling off" their undertakings begin to nag; confidence slips, so they focus on building up resources needed to carry out plans and keep their "charge" safe. Priorities shift from enthusiasm, love of truth and justice, and shared vision to pragmatism and keeping enterprises going and growing; the world becomes harsh, competitive "dog-eat-dog" with "profit and loss, winning and losing"; they want the best for themselves and "their people" and, unlike Threes, are up-front about it.
- Warmth and levity continue in moments but a driven, "no nonsense," hardworking, rugged, businesslike quality enters; they run families like businesses; if short of means they take multiple jobs; "construct fortifications and stockpile resources." Protective of the few they care about; the circle of concern narrows though the circle of dealings may be wide; friendly but respect and affection are not given easily.
- More impulsive self-assertion; shrewd about others; enter conflict if they suspect others are "up to something"; demand absolute honesty and straight talk but increasingly refuse to discuss feelings and vulnerability.
- "Rugged individualists," believers in free enterprise; less cooperative, poor team players, concerned for others only if they contribute to the effort or are among those the Eight feels responsible for. Movers and shakers in business, real estate, industry and finance, anywhere entrepreneurial skill is valued and businesses seen as indispensable; found in all careers (health professionals, homemakers, chefs, athletes, artists) but always wanting to direct their own activities and frustrated with no say.
- **Eight versus Three:** Threes want visibility, prestige or glamour, to build self-esteem; Eights want control and independence and would happily run a lucrative garbage dump or a light-switch factory because people will always need them, a "reliable base of power."
- Money means self-sufficiency, not depending on or submitting to anyone; funds allow them to "buy what, and whom, they need" (Rhett Butler quote). Persuasive salesmen; unscrupulous ones, outright con artists. Quick improvement in finances; effective negotiators who can withstand pressure, say no, and compromise when it serves; careful consumers; *Homo economicus*. Line of work matters less than the bottom line.
- Enterprising rather than competitive: they prefer others concede at once. Risk-takers who love danger and excitement (airplanes, sailing, scuba, racing); they want to do the impossible; they calculate odds, not foolhardy: interested in surviving and legacy. They succeed because they work constantly and enjoy it; deals and risks are "a way of literally making more of themselves." [s.20]

**Level 5: The Dominating Power Broker.** [s.20]
- If projects falter or respect is lacking, they assert more aggressively, not to win resources but to show power and importance; they want to be the "big shot" and to convince themselves they are up to the job; focus shifts from projects to themselves; the environment must reflect their will; (like Fours) they may have had erratic nurturing and build an environment supporting their self-image; the fear of being harmed or controlled now cannot tolerate competition: others' strength is a threat.
- The **"edifice" complex:** epic scale, building a house or financial empire reflecting themselves; they would like to be monarchs of a family dynasty perpetuating influence for generations; seeking immortality.
- Still use persuasion more than intimidation: dole out favors with hints of more to come; "big talk," bluffing, big promises (e.g., "over a hundred grand in the first year"); integrity erodes and they will con people. Ostentation: throwing money around, paying everyone's dinner, lavish tips; provoking or teasing to show they can get away with it; coarse language to seem "real" and "street-smart"; swagger betrays growing insecurity and an expectation of rejection. They expect perks, privileges, signs of respect and obedience, family members following dictates without question; heavy-handed if not.
- An innate sense that power is "not a thing but the ability to get things done, to shape events"; it must be continually used to be maintained. They are often unaware of the force they use (a conversation feels to others like a heated argument; blunt expression feels insulting or threatening); they assume others enjoy confrontation and can defend themselves as they can; as they deteriorate they ignore others' feelings and handicaps. Every interaction is a potential challenge; "best defense is a good offense"; territoriality ("This is my area. Stay out.").
- **Expansive** power: fill any void; widening the sphere raises their sense of greatness; expansive in every area including sexual life. *Male Eights:* macho "man's man," "phallic exhibitionists" ("I am the biggest and the greatest!"), lord and master at home; aggression and sexuality intertwined, stormy relationships, womanizers who treat women as "whores or madonnas" and possessions. *Female Eights:* may dominate spouses; outside the home culture discourages aggression, so they suffer stress and frustration and confine aggression to the domestic front (controlling finances, demanding gratification); they are called "dragon ladies," "pushy broads," condemned for traits praised in men (the authors compare Two males); society's repression of Eight energy in women has not brought out their healthier qualities; "the whole issue of gender and the Enneagram types is one which is bound to uncover many imbalances in cultures."
- Temptation to believe their "larger-than-life" image (Mafia dons, four-star generals); stop empowering others and begin to suppress them; do not delegate, trust no one, want total obedience; threatened by anyone else exercising will; yet need others to carry out their orders, offering **patronage** (money or protection). Turning point: dominating dehumanizes everyone, making aggression and destruction more likely. [s.20]

**Level 6: The Confrontational Adversary.** [s.20]
- Complaints from those "under their wings" cause a crisis: fear of losing control and that the protected may challenge their authority ("marching into battle, but the troops are not behind them"). Anger boils; everything is a struggle; they cannot trust anyone and push and force things (sometimes literally breaking a clock or knob trying to force an object to "cooperate").
- Everything becomes adversarial; they create conflicts even over trivia because **pride is at stake**; everyone from associate to greengrocer is an opponent; threatening, intimidating, strong-arming, bullying without apology, exploding in rage if orders are not carried out ("What I say goes!").
- **Power base:** they make it their business to have what others need and try to control necessities such as food, shelter and security; money becomes the yardstick of success and the one dependable source of security and independence (spouse, children and friends being undependable).
- Headstrong, cannot compromise; winning every contest of wills defends the self; proud of getting concessions "without having to fire a shot": they apply more pressure than opponents can, shouting longer and louder; "no personality type is better at waging psychological warfare" or at bluffing; an implicit threat of violence ("You don't want to make me angry."). Fair play degenerates into "an eye for an eye."
- Opposite of the healthy Eight's empowering effect: egos too fragile to share glory, afraid to let anyone close be powerful; they undermine others' confidence, create dependencies, and oppress so that others cannot even voice frustrations. They hate softness in themselves and others; use the "carrot and the stick"; "Take it or leave it!"; forbid questioning; their word is law.
- Irony: they intimidate mostly those they are sure they can beat, finding a weak point; they confront equals only with backs to the wall; a defeat would be catastrophic to pride. By bullying they make enemies of those they depend on and create a pool of resentment; they can never relax, bracing to be pushed back. Not psychopaths: they recognize limits of self-interest and push until self-interest is no longer served, but can misjudge and violence may erupt. [s.20]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Eight [s.20]

**Level 7: The Ruthless Outlaw.** [s.20]
- A war of intimidation has pushed relationships past breaking; others may truly band against them. Pragmatic Eights may reconsider; if overwhelmed by crises or exceptionally abusive childhoods, they escalate: doing almost anything to survive and prevail, "life or death" literally or figuratively.
- Pragmatism becomes ruthlessness and brutality: **might makes right**, law of the jungle, survival of the fittest, realpolitik: "Expedience is all." Utterly betrayed and rejected (from childhood abuse or recent losses), they trust no one and see a cruel, pitiless world; outcasts, society's "rejects," who believe they have crossed a line with no return, so there is nothing to lose (once one crime is committed, less anxiety about another).
- Capable of crime: any restriction is an invitation to defy; "any line drawn in the sand is sure to be crossed"; if in a position to rule, no limit to their control: despotic, tyrannical, taking away rights, freedom and dignity.
- Impossible and dangerous to be intimate with: they read friendliness as weakness to exploit; lying, cheating, stealing, reneging; "the most frequent perpetrators of the Big Lie" (a falsehood repeated until accepted).
- **Violence:** used almost reflexively, with little provocation, without guilt (they defy guilt as a weakness that would leave them prey); other types resort to violence only when no alternative and feel guilt. Quoted Fenichel: power as a means of combating guilt ("I alone decide what is good and what is evil") can fail because the superego is part of one's own personality, setting up a vicious circle requiring more power and more crimes. The authors' restatement: the more terrible they are, the more terrible things they must do so as not to feel guilty.
- Worst abuses fall on those closest and least able to defend themselves (verbal and physical abuse; the text names rape, child molestation, wife beating as common expressions); they play for control of families, fortunes, businesses or nations. They cannot stop: they fear retaliation from those wronged. The authors caution that such Eights did not become so by accident or desire to be "evil," and that perpetrators of violence were often victims as children; they resolve that no one will ever harm them or get close again. [s.20]

**Level 8: The Omnipotent Megalomaniac.** [s.20]
- Powerful adversaries (the state, creditors, opponents) arise; they become obsessed with invulnerability (changing phone numbers, private eyes, disappearing), but the stress plus never having been defeated yet produces **delusion of invincibility**: omnipotent, godlike.
- They consolidate power by terror; may punish or harm innocent people to show they mean business and signal escalation to horrific levels; convinced fate has given them privileges others lack, "supermen beyond morality."
- With no self-restraint they "play God" in ever more outrageous ways; **obscene fascination with death**: fear of death (a reflection of fear of being harmed) impels them to defy it by killing, as a magical way of warding off their own death. (The chapter quotes a review of a film portrait of Idi Amin: all are potential victims; he "sweats like a cornered beast while proclaiming his invincibility.")
- Out of touch with reality; the ego "like a cancer"; judgment needed for survival undermined; the more delusional, the more overextended and reckless: sowing seeds of their own destruction. Conflict: isolating from a hostile environment while convincing themselves and their entourage they are masters; they degrade others and raise the violence; they "must and will eventually be stopped." [s.20]

**Level 9: The Violent Destroyer.** [s.20]
- Knowing they cannot hold out forever, they destroy before being destroyed: "the most widely destructive and antisocial of all the personality types, just as when they are healthy, they are the most constructive." They sacrifice anyone and anything (wife, children, friends, associates, all they built) for survival; if the world will not conform to their will they destroy it, "barbarically destructive on as great a scale as is within their power."
- A "death trip": absurd, since the deaths of others do not assure survival; the horror brings destruction on themselves and a bid for greatness becomes infamy. Creation and destruction are the two ends of the Eight's spectrum, originating from the same impulse; when the life impulse becomes saving one's own life at any cost it turns corrupt and destructive.
- They can destroy because they never identified with anyone else; they see only themselves in the world and hate it if it does not reflect them. (Paraphrasing Hillel: "If you are not for yourself, who will be? But if you are for yourself alone, what are you?") [s.20]

## The Dynamics of the Eight [s.20]

**Direction of Disintegration: the Eight goes to Five** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Beginning at Level 4 stressed Eights act out average to unhealthy Five traits. The enticement: a tactical retrenchment from assertive action into safety of thought, "the union of power and safety": more premeditated, secretive, wily, striking without warning. [s.20]

| Eight level | What going to Five looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | Overwhelmed by projects or led off course by impulse, they retreat to gather information and resources like average Fives, doing research themselves (long nights acquiring skills) or hiring analysts but reviewing findings themselves. |
| 5 | Blunt, rough behavior masks expectation of rejection and social unsureness; they withdraw into projects, emotionally detached, uncommunicative, secretive, high-strung; compartmentalize friendships and activities so no one knows too much and gains advantage. |
| 6 | Intimidate and see relationships as adversarial; trust almost no one; want only people they can dominate; the Five adds provocativeness and dark, extreme views; the Five's cynicism and rejection of prevailing values reinforce feeling an angry, misunderstood outsider; contempt for human weakness is worsened by the Five's intellectual arrogance. |
| 7 | Profoundly antisocial; trust only own wits and will to survive; turn on those they feel betrayed them or provoke aggression; or hide out, minimize needs, cut off remaining ties, burn bridges, live as a recluse; the unhealthy Five's nihilism may justify suppressing guilt. |
| 8 | Delusions of omnipotence; overextension leaves them open to predators and enemies; cracks in the repression of fear begin at Level 7, and at Level 8 energies are taxed so that terrors erupt, throwing the mind into chaos and horror and more irrational, devastating behavior. |
| 9 | Phobic about survival and detached from feelings; isolating, they can neither defend nor assert, so what power remains crumbles, giving real reasons for some fears; terror and isolation feed each other; counterphobia and denial fail; terrified of punishment for crimes; paranoia and phobia may break with reality (the authors say a genuine schizophrenic illness may result if long-lasting but not necessarily for all); enemies have their chance: "no omnipotent god but a pathetic devil in torment." |

**Direction of Integration: the Eight goes to Two** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Growth lies in opening to others rather than dominating. Healthy Eights are heroic and magnanimous mainly toward groups from whom they stand apart; at Two they put aside the lofty position and relate to others as individuals and equals: identify *with* others, with empathy and compassion, nurturing, generous, helpful, concerned for others' needs as their own. "They learn the power of love rather than being obsessed with the love of power." They discover it is in their deepest self-interest to love others: power not used for others turns destructive, dominating never brings what they most need (to be loved for themselves), and using power for others does not diminish them. The greatest learn the higher lessons of love at Two, seeing themselves as servants of others, "an extraordinary act of heroism" that makes them immortal. [s.20]

## The Major Subtypes of the Eight [s.20]

**The Eight with a Seven-Wing: "The Maverick."** [s.20]
- Most openly assertive subtype (both components aggressive: Eights for power and autonomy, Sevens for experiences and possessions); blunter, more realistic and extroverted than the Nine-wing, more consistent (same at work and home, treating all alike); "cut to the chase"; people of action. (Examples include Gorbachev, FDR, Lyndon Johnson, Gurdjieff, Iacocca, Trump, Kissinger, Sean Connery, Barbara Walters, Hemingway, Muhammad Ali, Wagner, Al Capone, Marcos, Stalin, Mao, Qaddafi, Idi Amin, Jim Jones.)
- *Healthy:* highly extroverted, action-oriented, energetic; the Seven's quick mind with the Eight's vision for practical possibilities; initiative in everything from deals to romance; entrepreneurial; perhaps the most independent subtype, uninterested in working for others unless it leads to greater independence; charisma plus enjoyment of life attract many people into their plans; create opportunities and challenge people; may have a public or historical impact; practical magnanimity (concern for others' material well-being).
- *Average:* power and experience reinforce each other; strong business sense and drive; robust, earthy, materialistic; attention-seeking storytelling and "straight talk"; throw money around even with limited means; the Seven-wing adds hyperbole, big promises and exaggeration to recruit others; display feelings openly and ignore reactions; impatient, impulsive, passion-led; lower levels: bullying, chip on the shoulder, manipulating with resources; no compassion or guilt; still insecure about money and power, never feeling they have enough to be fully independent.
- *Unhealthy:* ruthless, impulsive (acts later judged "a stroke of genius or a fatal mistake"); offensive, tyrannical, brutish; explosive tempers; easily feel betrayed; resist restraint; manic tendencies reinforce delusions of omnipotence (vast spending); out of control when anxious or threatened; defend against anxiety by acting out, striking first, "destroy before they are destroyed."

**The Eight with a Nine-Wing: "The Bear."** [s.20]
- Some conflict: Eight asserts and takes on conflict; Nine suppresses aggression and avoids conflict. They enjoy comfort and peace, more domestic, more oriented to people and less to possessions and experiences; softer, more accepting, less confrontational in relating; get their way with a "soft, firm voice" and casual demeanor; "quiet strength and … power held in reserve"; a "poker face," stolid, but passionate and angry when aroused; like Nines, often unaware of their strength and the force of their anger. (Examples include Martin Luther King Jr., Golda Meir, de Gaulle, Indira Gandhi, Picasso, Brando, Paul Newman, John Wayne, Johnny Cash, Fidel Castro, "Darth Vader," "King Lear," "Don Vito Corleone.")
- *Healthy:* quiet power, understated wisdom; strategic planners, steady and inexorable; not easily perturbed, at ease, no need to assert at every moment; less wheeler-dealer but still prefer running their own enterprises; open to concerns beyond self-interest, particularly family; kinder, benign patriarchs or matriarchs leading through support and protectiveness; a personal, almost mystical bond with others; interest in arts, nature, animals, children.
- *Average:* a definite split between the public aggressive side and the passive, accommodating side shown to very few (mostly family): warm at home, hard-nosed at work; avoid publicity, control affairs from behind the scenes; expansive forcefulness grounded on an "inner fortress of imperturbable strength"; others experience it as stubbornness and an ability to turn out anxieties; "velvet glove over an iron fist"; sly, watchful, "daring others to underestimate them"; speak slowly but read body language; look agreeable while sizing people up; lower levels: stubbornness becomes a quietly menacing quality; unpredictable moods (intimidating and belligerent, then accommodating and kindhearted); no one knows when the explosive temper will erupt.
- *Unhealthy:* reclusive, depressed, dissociated, but vengeful if they have access to power; almost immune from anxiety, so destructive without remorse, "ruthlessness with indifference"; depersonalized, "like some sort of cosmic force which swatted people aside"; generally less violent and destructive than the Seven-wing; may regret suffering they cause yet feel no empathy; may do more long-run damage by living longer.

## Some Final Thoughts (Eight) [s.20]
From the average levels down, Eights' self-esteem came from being destructive rather than constructive; the authors ask who is more truly powerful, "the person who destroys a city or the person who builds one." Unhealthy Eights bring about what they most feared (depleted, vulnerable to just retaliation: a self-fulfilling prophecy). The age-old problem of stopping a tyrant without becoming one; the Eight forces others to ask what they believe the ultimate values of life are (self-interest, or something more). "If there is no God, then Eights are the most expedient and shrewd of people"; but all are mortal and subject to others' judgment; and the last irony: if destructive, they will not have remade the world in their image, frustrating one of their deepest desires. [s.20]

# CHAPTER 11 — TYPE NINE: THE PEACEMAKER [s.21]

## The Nine in profile (the book's summary page) [s.21]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Deeply receptive, accepting, unselfconscious, emotionally stable and serene; trusting of self and others, at ease with self and life, innocent and simple; patient, unpretentious, good-natured, genuinely nice. Imaginative and creative, attuned to nonverbal communication; optimistic, reassuring, supportive; a healing and calming influence, harmonizing groups, bringing people together; good mediator, synthesizer, communicator. **At their best:** self-possessed, autonomous and fulfilled; great equanimity and contentment because present to themselves; paradoxically at one with self and thus able to form more profound relationships; more alive, awake, alert to self and others. |
| Average | Self-effacing and agreeable, accommodating themselves, idealizing others and "going along" to avoid conflict; a "philosophy of life" that quickly quiets anxiety; submerge themselves in functions for others. Unresponsive, complacent, walking away from problems and "sweeping them under the rug"; passive, disengaged, unreflective, inattentive; thinking hazy and ruminative, mostly about fantasies; "tune out" reality, becoming oblivious; emotional indolence, unwillingness to exert themselves on problems: passive-aggressive and indifferent. Minimize problems to appease others, "peace at any price"; fatalistic and resigned yet stubborn and resistant to influence; wishful thinking, waiting for magical solutions; inadvertently create conflicts by denial and obstinacy. |
| Unhealthy | Repressed, undeveloped, ineffectual; do not want to deal with problems: depressed, listless, dissociating from all conflict; neglectful and dangerously irresponsible. Blocking from awareness anything that could affect them, they dissociate until they cannot function: numb, depersonalized. Severely disoriented and catatonic, they abandon themselves, becoming shattered shells; multiple personalities possible. |

**Key Motivations:** want serenity and peace of mind, to create harmony in their environment, to preserve things as they are, to avoid conflicts and tension, to escape upsetting problems and demands. [s.21]

**Examples** (abridged): Abraham Lincoln, Joseph Campbell, Carl Jung, Ronald Reagan, Gerald Ford, Queen Elizabeth II, Princess Grace of Monaco, Walter Cronkite, Walt Disney, George Lucas, Garrison Keillor, Sophia Loren, Kevin Costner, Keanu Reeves, Ringo Starr, Whoopi Goldberg, Jim Henson, Marc Chagall, Norman Rockwell; fictional "Edith Bunker," "Marge Simpson." [s.21]

## Overview of the Nine [s.21]

- **Image of the inner landscape:** someone riding a bicycle on a beautiful day, enjoying the flow; the whole situation, not any part, is what is pleasant and identified with; the Nine's sense of self comes from being at one with their experience, an "effortless oneness" they want to preserve. Their receptive orientation gives such deep satisfaction that they see no reason to question or change anything essential. The fault arises when they refuse to see that life has difficulties: "their refusal to fix the tire when it goes flat" is the symbol. The type shows the personal cost of "peace at any price": refusing problems does not remove them, the peace is bought at others' expense and at the cost of relating to reality; with all good will Nines can do terrible harm while coasting along, turning a blind eye. [s.21]
- **In the Instinctive Triad (the primary type of it).** Nine is "the type most out of touch with their instinctual drives and their ability to relate to the environment," because Nines do not want to be affected by the environment or by powerful feelings their instincts would stir; they dissociate from the intensity of passions, drives and anger to stay tranquil. *Healthy:* they create peace directly (soothing, healing conflicts) or indirectly (creativity and communication appealing to innocence and gentleness), which also supports their own peacefulness. *Less healthy:* they ignore what disturbs them, finally relating to an inner idealized image of others rather than the real people, and tune out aspects of themselves too, so (unless very healthy) they develop neither a clear self-awareness nor a well-defined awareness of the world. [s.21]
- **Search for autonomy.** Like Eights and Ones, Nines want freedom and space, but they are blocked in asserting it for fear of ruining harmony; they repress the desire and find freedom by **dissociating**: breaking contact and inhabiting imagination and dreams; others are seen as idealized impressions and their own self-image is kept in "soft focus"; risk: never becoming independent, fully functioning people with clear identities. [s.21]
- **Idealizing others, devaluing themselves.** They project strength, self-assertion, poise, self-confidence onto an idealized other; they are not anxious about the lacks (not much focused on self) but are repeatedly drawn to assertive, energetic people; subconsciously they want to merge to incorporate the repressed qualities; the result is an ill-defined self and undeveloped potentials, with inner peace the all-important motive. [s.21]
- **Healthy versus average orientation.** Only healthy Nines know themselves as distinct persons who actively choose what they want and act directly. Average Nines are passive: willpower is used to deflect, resist, fend off reality, maintaining two boundaries, one against the **outer environment** (others influencing them) and one against **inner** feelings, memories, thoughts, sensations that would jar them. The price: they cannot contribute to or love others without developing as persons, which requires risking discomfort and unwelcome truths. Irony: many are drawn to personal-growth books and seminars but gravitate to comforting disciplines or "edit" teachings to be palatable. [s.21]
- **Repression and aggression.** Like Eights and Ones, Nines overcompensate in one area for underdevelopment in another: they repress self-assertion to be more receptive; at the extreme they barely function as individuals (living through someone else or in a "world of hazy illusions"); their awareness is leveled so nothing bothers them. They equate self-assertion with aggression and fear the powerful feelings it brings, so they repress aggression until unaware of it, "solving" it by ignoring it out of existence and denying aggressive acts. Their peace is partly "willful blindness," a self-deception; their passivity, denials, inattention and disengagement are themselves negative forms of aggression, **passive resistance**: "Nines are far more aggressive than they think they are." [s.21]
- **Parental orientation: connected with both parents.** Nines identified with and incorporated the agendas of both the nurturing-figure and the protective-figure, so much energy goes into keeping these identifications in inner harmony: "a balancing act." From a peaceful household the incorporated messages were manageable; from a strife-torn one, holding all the conflicted feelings was nearly intolerable, so average to unhealthy Nines learned to **dissociate** and tune out the conflict (like a child singing to herself to block out parents fighting). Identity is more or less "given," but they may find it is not who they really are (like Threes) and that they depend on something outside (like Sixes); little space remains for them ("crowded out of their own selves"), so claiming something just for themselves matters (what seems trivial to others is defended fiercely; once they understand their accommodations they can let go of some of those habits). Discord or separation between the parents is terrifying (same as inner discord): they feel whole as long as those they identify with are whole; when less healthy they merely *imagine* others are well, thereby beginning to lose the people they want to stay connected with. [s.21]
- **Jungian correspondence: introverted sensation type.** The authors say Jung describes average to unhealthy Nines. The quoted passage: calm, passive or rationally self-controlled; "unrelatedness to objects"; the object's stimulus is removed and replaced by a subjective reaction, which feels like devaluation to the other person; "well-intentioned neutrality" that soothes and adjusts (too low raised, too high lowered, enthusiasm damped); a "menace to his environment" through "total innocuousness"; easily a victim of others' aggressiveness, then taking revenge "on the most unsuitable occasions with redoubled obtuseness and stubbornness." [s.21]
- **Nine's characteristic selfishness:** willingness to sacrifice a great many values, "all of reality," to keep inner serenity; any emotional reaction disrupts repression, so they seek peace at any price and lose contact with everyone and everything, including themselves; "burying their heads in the sand," problems never solved, sleepwalking, poor judgment sometimes with tragic results; they would rather turn their backs on reality than make "the impossible effort of putting their world right again." *Healthy* Nines are "the most contented and pleasant people imaginable," receptive, with a mature peace that admits conflict, separation, growth and individuality; "their own persons, yet they delight in giving themselves away." [s.21]

## Analyzing the Healthy Nine [s.21]

**Level 1: The Self-Possessed Guide.** [s.21]
- Having overcome fear of loss and separation, they become fully empowered, independent persons: contentment and unshakable equanimity because they are "in union with someone from whom they can never be separated: themselves." Rare wholeness and integrity, a source of beauty, creativity and joy.
- Serene yet vital and alive, in touch with thoughts, feelings and desires; aware of even aggressive feelings without alarm, understanding that having aggression is not acting aggressively; so they bring more of themselves to relationships, which deepen. Self-respecting, with enormous dignity and no egocentricity; they see themselves as they are and therefore see others as they are: no longer idealized, so they are "truly other" and loved realistically.
- Firmly centered, dynamic, powerful, able to deal with problems; they actively inhabit their own consciousness, an "extremely private, almost spiritual event": being born psychologically as mature centers of awareness ("a new being, an ancient child, an indomitable spirit").
- An example to all types of being at one with self and with the world; self-possession and self-surrender "so profound as to have mystical overtones"; they must reflect what humans were like "before the Fall into self-consciousness and alienation"; "we are each a gift to the other." [s.21]

**Level 2: The Receptive Person.** [s.21]
- Fear of losing balance and harmony remains in the shadows; if they succumb, they seek peace by becoming unselfconscious: they begin to lose themselves in their impressions and diffuse their own awareness, losing grounding in sensation and instinct, merging with the other and losing their center.
- Identifying with both parents lets them identify with others and give full, positive attention; capacity for loving and sustaining others. Few conflicts; peaceful and truly at peace; high tolerance for stress and irritation (patient, imperturbable, relaxed); innocence and simplicity, guileless (lying or exploiting would not occur to them).
- "The most trusting of the personality types" (trust others, self, life); nonjudgmental acceptance makes them emotional anchors and sanctuaries; modest, gentle, approachable, uncritical, easy to please; undemanding; they are more gracious to those they dislike than any other type.
- They commune with nature (sailing, hiking, camping, gardening, animal care), especially its mystical and mythological side; they personalize everything (country as a living thing, pets as people, mountains and rivers as archetypal forces, elves and leprechauns in the living room). Their **archetypal imagination** gives the others a vision of the magic of the world through "innocent eyes"; they keep contemplation and wonder.
- Affinity for nonverbal communication (pictures, symbols, thinking in colors and impressions; music, singing, playing, where self is lost and harmony and vibration feed oneness). They see sex, birth, aging and death as natural; not defiant of the natural order, yielding to it. [s.21]

**Level 3: The Supportive Peacemaker.** [s.21]
- Because tension threatens their peace they become **peacemakers**, mediating disputes among those close to them; good mediators because they take complaints seriously, understand real differences and see common ground, believing cooperation gains more than divisiveness.
- Healing touch; "pour oil on troubled waters"; calming presence; optimistic and reassuring, stressing the positive when responsible; forgive and forget; affable, called "nice" or "salt of the earth"; sunny, unaffected humor and warm laughter; unpretentious (same directness with royalty or a cab driver); "as comfortable as an old shoe."
- Unstinting love and generosity toward those significant to them; gut instinct about others means what they do is appropriate and valuable. When something important must be said they can be extremely candid without desiring to hurt; "uncommon common sense"; no ulterior motives, ego to protect, concern for status; "the honesty of children and the wisdom of adults."
- Levelheaded in crisis; often in medical, health and nutrition fields; interest in healing and holistic approaches; students of metaphysics and human development (meditation, energy and body work, yoga, relaxation); explore dreams, symbols and images; attracted to indigenous ways living in harmony with nature; adept at synthesizing traditions to find common threads and new insights.
- Creative in traditional ways too, sharing visions of an enchanted, Utopian world; unselfconsciousness gives easy access to the creative process (this changes at average levels); their art is positive, reassuring, full of wonder. Can be very successful by bringing out the best in others through a nurturing environment; non-competitive and rarely calling attention to themselves, so underestimated and taken for granted until others realize how much they contributed. [s.21]

## Analyzing the Average Nine [s.21]

**Level 4: The Accommodating Role-Player.** [s.21]
- Outwardly similar to healthy Nines, but the shift is in attitude: they subordinate themselves to roles and social conventions, not wanting to stand out. Fear that asserting themselves will create conflict sweeps them into the average levels; ironically this breeds conflict because people want responses ("Where would you like to go for dinner tonight?" "I don't care. Wherever you want to go.").
- They lower expectations of life and themselves and go along with others' wishes; social expectations create a role (made by others for others' needs) into which they disappear; expressing or asserting feels like "making a scene."
- **Self-effacement**: in contrast to Threes (who want to be outstanding in their roles), they do not want to draw fire; they become "generic" versions of a role (boy or girl next door, nine-to-five worker, journeyman musician, pleasant doctor), hard to distinguish from the role.
- Their sense of self depends on accommodation; they idealize those they identify with (the more wonderful the other, the better they feel) but in doing so forget themselves and neglect their development; the mother who lives for her children, the wife for her husband (appropriate for infants, a problem if continued). The wishes and thoughts of others become theirs. The reciprocal motion: accommodate, idealize; the idealized person can do no wrong and a value or belief is never questioned.
- They fall into conventional roles (husband, wife, breadwinner, parent, citizen) and take on the culture's lifestyle, religious and political beliefs, and expectations for children, even in an "alternative culture" (dress, behave "normally" for it; conscientious in a spiritual community's protocols). **Respectability** matters (not keeping up with the Joneses but being proper). "The archetypal common man," "the glue of society"; **conservative** in the sense of resisting significant change; **past-oriented** (the past is known, already lived through; nostalgia and sentimentality as sources of good feeling and a reliable resource when conflicts erupt). The problem is not their values but not thinking them through, adopting a way of life wholesale at face value. [s.21]

**Level 5: The Disengaged Participant.** [s.21]
- Emotional stability depends on their inner world of beliefs and idealizations, so they fear change and maintain the status quo, letting things "work out on their own." Irony: they must *do* something to do nothing: disengage from perceived threats. Healthy unselfconsciousness becomes unreflective disconnectedness; sluggish complacency, intellectual laziness, emotional indolence ("Oh, well, we don't have to worry about that..."); life happens to them.
- Vagueness and impassiveness; "I can take it or leave it"; matter-of-fact even where passion is called for; mellow and "laid-back" to a fault, the classic phlegmatic temperament ("on cloud nine"). Their highs are not high nor lows low (as Jung noted); not aware their feelings are dampened; others sense something missing, "not all there," spacy, "a million miles away."
- Nothing seems urgent; they forget things, ignore details, drift off from work within minutes, ramble or change subject abruptly; dreamers contemplating an idealized vision; intelligent ones may talk about philosophy, theology, arts or science but it is "vague woolgathering" to pass time. **"Busy work"** to feel constructive (errands, projects that maintain their world but do little); inner resistance to leaving routines "like swimming through molasses"; "autopilot."
- Healthy simplicity decays into obliviousness, like looking at a clock without seeing the time; they disconnect from what they do not want to see, as people tune out television commercials; sleepwalkers; semiawareness like long-term tranquilizer users; physical and emotional comfort valued; undemanding pastimes (puttering, errands, collecting knickknacks, mindless TV).
- Psychological passivity is not the same as complete inactivity: an average Nine may head a multimillion-dollar corporation while inwardly disconnected, thanks to **compartmentalization (isolation)** as a defense; roles are used to keep others from affecting them (relating through roles without investment), withdrawing into a safe inner sanctum.
- No cause-and-effect connections: they do not think of consequences, including those of omissions, trusting everything will work out. Root: lack of self-awareness; they perceive all reality vaguely, so when practical problems arise they become part of the problem.
- Underneath, **anger** at others for not seeing their needs and at themselves for inability to assert; they repress it, not realizing it is the very energy that would let them assert themselves, and so disconnect from all feelings. They also **compartmentalize relationships**: those with whom they identify versus everyone else, who are "essentially unreal," an abstraction toward whom they can be callous and indifferent. Even the first group gets little energy: attention shifts from real people to the idealization, so others sense neglect and lose interest ("As Nines drift off, others drift away"). [s.21]

**Level 6: The Resigned Fatalist.** [s.21]
- When doing nothing fails, they **minimize** importance: underestimate consequences of passivity, how hard it will be for others to correct problems, and the need to do anything. They take pride in enduring by tuning out; fatalism ("Well, it doesn't really matter anyway"); receptivity has become resignation, "a giving up rather than a mature letting go. This is not optimism but selfishness. ('I don't want to hear it—I just don't want to be upset.')"
- Life is something to "get through"; staying in a bad marriage or workplace rather than risk disruption; apathy replaces compliance, even going along; profound indifference to themselves, life and events.
- "Nines refuse to see the problem": they appease angry others with concessions to "get their problems behind them," feel the crisis has passed, forget how it was settled; next week the problem persists. They water down conflicting positions, give false hope, tell people prematurely to "calm down—everything will be all right"; others cannot connect with them; irony: those who feared losing connection have withdrawn attention; when pressed they are stubborn and angry ("Why can't you accept me as I am?").
- Poor judgment, "penny wise and pound foolish"; do only as much as they must, lack staying power, think a little effort is enough and undo the good they began.
- **Wishful thinking, belief in magic:** someone will come and fix it, or patience will remove it ("Someday my ship will come in"; "Someday my prince will come"); dreams fade while days pass. Others suffer the consequences but rarely press them because they are "still so nice."
- Selfishness named: they put peace above others' serious needs and above reality, appeasing as a defense against changing anything about themselves or the idealization of their relationships, sacrificing "their spouses, their children—and themselves—to the god of peace." The aggression is so subtle it is rarely noticed; others are no longer real: "a relationship with a fantasy." Anxieties become too great to ignore, and everyone now seems to have a grievance with them. [s.21]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Nine [s.21]

**Level 7: The Denying Doormat.** [s.21]
- Adamant about not facing problems; active **denial** to avoid pain and keep illusions; obstinate, neglectful, impervious to pressure; even an obvious easy solution goes unused. Their defense, **repression resistance**, is extraordinarily frustrating, like a bolted inner door; once open and receptive, now impenetrable; furious at being forced, yet their only way to express anger is to resist and block out more (**passive resistance**), apart from occasional inadvertent eruptions of rage when repression fails.
- More typically **victims and "doormats":** self-effacement degenerates into allowing exploitation and abuse; fear of conflict and loss and low self-esteem prevent self-defense from psychological or physical abuse; they are profoundly outraged but repress rage and even self-preservation instincts since powerful feelings would overwhelm their equilibrium; repression is tiring, leaving them depressed, confused, nonfunctional, more dependent on their oppressors.
- Seriously neglectful of responsibilities to others and themselves (not going to a doctor when ill, ignoring spouses' or children's medical or emotional problems, unable to do work that upsets them); totally unreliable; "like running into a stone wall." Inadequate, undeveloped, helpless; fatigued because energy goes into warding off reality, hence depression, listlessness, dependence; cannot cope with any tension; others, sometimes those they harmed, must step in.
- Serious interpersonal conflict certain; they are mystified by others' negative feelings and do not realize what their inattention cost. Facing the harm they did to someone they identify with would bring extreme anxiety, guilt, despair, possibly suicide; repression spares them but moments of insight break in (consequences that cannot be undone): "like a beast pounding at the door of their unconscious. How to keep it out?" [s.21]

**Level 8: The Dissociating Automaton.** [s.21]
- To avoid awareness of what they have done they cut themselves off from everything: blanking out subjectively, **depersonalized**, regressing to an infantile state "as if they desired to return to the womb," numb and affectless as if in amnesia.
- Automatons that do not feel or react: as if the self had been removed from the body, which functions on its own. Extent of denial can be astounding (denying a lost limb or expecting it to regrow; not really fired; divorce or death did not happen). Dissociation is now habitual, "a way of not living"; life seems a bad dream; unlike other types in trauma, they dissociate because they feel they can never deal with reality again.
- Resemble trauma victims in shock: lost, confused about identity and sometimes whereabouts; depression from Level 7 chronic; desolate and numb yet anguish and anger break through (blank one moment, sobbing hysterically the next); regress to earlier and earlier memories; tremendous unconscious rage that they fear would destroy inner refuges; hysterical outbursts and temper tantrums possible. A repressed hysterical element: nowhere to go outward or inward; the only way out is to push dissociation one step further into self-abandonment. [s.21]

**Level 9: The Self-Abandoning Ghost.** [s.21]
- If pushed over the edge, a psychotic break with reality and schizoid breaks within the psyche: the most extreme dissociation from who they are, resembling **autism**. Those with chronic, extreme childhood abuse may be prone to multiple personality disorder (not all cases are Nines, not all unhealthy Nines develop it, but "some overlap"): attempting to accommodate highly conflicting emotional material and build an identity from it.
- Usually, they abandon themselves as whole persons, reinvesting consciousness in fragments, each an aspect of the repressed, denied, undeveloped self; memories, dreamlike trances and emotional reactions come and go at random; the personality comes "unglued." This is a "solution" of sorts: someone else lives (the "other-self," the disconnected identifications with significant others from the past); "a dream without a dreamer"; and a subpersonality can harm others or itself. Fitting outcome for those who never took interest in themselves as individuals: now "many different 'people'—and no one"; having feared losing others, they lose themselves. [s.21]

## The Dynamics of the Nine [s.21]

**Direction of Disintegration: the Nine goes to Six** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Starting at Level 4, under stress Nines act out average to unhealthy Six traits: when events overwhelm the defense of disengagement, they feel the full intensity of anxiety and become reactive and insecure; they try both constructive action and contact with feelings, but in erratic, imbalanced ways. [s.21]

| Nine level | What going to Six looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | Accommodating others' wishes, with their own agendas on the "back burner"; under rising anxiety, lots of "organizational activity" to stabilize environment and relationships; intense work periods for security (driven by anxiety, not positive intention); identify more strongly with protectors, supporters, groups or ideas that give confidence, purpose and direction. |
| 5 | Staying in their "comfort zone"; when demands threaten it they go negative and defensive; anger and anxiety escalate; passive-aggressive tactics (keep saying yes to demands, then do what they want); feel pressured, complain, are evasive like average Sixes. |
| 6 | Digging in heels with resigned, fatalistic attitude and entrenched comforting routines; when others disturb their "slumbers," a siege mentality; aggressive reactions like Sixes; blame others or react defiantly; temper outbursts often surprising to the Nine as much as to others; belligerence creates more conflict and escalates anxiety. |
| 7 | Maintaining peace takes huge energy and willful turning from reality; feel they cannot cope and repress to numbness; the move to Six shows growing dependency, helplessness and a desire to hand their life to someone who will "fix everything"; but too frightened and too enraged about their accommodations to confront problems, which get more unmanageable. |
| 8 | Shutting down, highly dissociated; depression chronic; underneath, terror and rage; when numbness fails, anxiety and rage explode hysterically as in unhealthy Sixes: irrational rants, random violence, paranoid delusions about people around them; throwing dishes, wrecking furniture, attacking people. |
| 9 | Anxiety breaks through the repression completely; the easygoing person becomes an overreacting hysteric: fearful, agitated, tearful, panicked; needing care; may become abjectly self-abasing ("morbidly dependent," in Horney's phrase as the text spells it) and masochistically self-destructive so others must care for them; self-defeating, humiliating acts for self-punishment (guilt for letting others down) and self-abasement (to draw people back); this fails because aggression toward self and others has also been let loose: self-hatred and hostility toward anyone who increases rather than relieves their anxiety; alcohol, drugs or suicide if peace cannot be found. |

**Direction of Integration: the Nine goes to Three** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material). Integrating Nines become self-assured and interested in developing themselves and talents fully: from self-possession to "making something more of themselves," from a just-born presence to an active inner-directed force; no longer living through someone else or needing conventional roles for identity; they create themselves by asserting themselves properly; no longer fear change (flexible, adaptable). They connect with vitality ("in Freudian terms … their id, the aggressive and instinctual side") and learn their impulses are not necessarily destructive but can lead to self-development. Their peace becomes less fragile because they can assert without aggression and so without jeopardizing relationships; as self-esteem rises relationships mature; they no longer need to be self-effacing to have a relationship and, by being themselves, attract others who may even begin to identify with and accommodate to them (which they will discourage as dependency but nonetheless enjoy). [s.21]

## The Major Subtypes of the Nine [s.21]

**The Nine with an Eight-Wing: "The Comfort Seeker."** [s.21]
- Conflicting components (passive, harmony-seeking Nine versus aggressive, self-interested Eight); since Nine is basic, they are fundamentally other-oriented, receptive, unselfconscious, agreeable, but some part asserts strongly at times; "mellow," outgoing, sociable (jokes, stories, friends); more sensual, instinctive, "physical," grounded than the One-wing; operate on feelings and hunches; "one of the most difficult subtypes to understand." (Examples include Reagan, Eisenhower, Gerald Ford, Kevin Costner, Keanu Reeves, Ingrid Bergman, Sophia Loren, Ringo Starr, Whoopi Goldberg, Bing Crosby, Walter Cronkite, Lady Bird Johnson, Marc Chagall.)
- *Healthy:* the Eight-wing adds inner strength, willpower and an expansive, passionate quality: "at once powerful and gentle"; assert effectively despite unselfconsciousness; forceful despite graciousness; courageously independent despite subordinating to common goals; formidable but rarely used tempers; dry sense of humor; skilled but not self-promoting; more engaged with people and things; concerned with immediate needs; accepting of people as they are; often helping professions, consulting, sales, services, human resources and negotiation.
- *Average:* compartmentalize emotions completely; self-image of peacefulness yet occasionally quite aggressive without realizing; more likely to fall into **sensual indolence** (complacent, even lazy in some areas, extremely competitive in others); if not intellectually gifted may seem good-natured but thickheaded; strong elemental drives for psychological and sexual union; self-interest tied to material comfort; more stubborn and defensive than the One-wing; bad tempers triggered by interference with their sense of well-being (others can see anger building; blunt, explosive, then suddenly placid again); protective instincts aim to protect themselves and property, not to hurt; belligerent but with little lasting animosity; greatest ire against attacks on their families, beliefs or way of life; afterward they sue for peace and make allies of former enemies; as they deteriorate they dig in and refuse to cooperate with anyone threatening safe routines.
- *Unhealthy:* often resemble unhealthy Fours: depressed, low energy, but flat, without affect, with tremors of tearfulness and anxiety; Eight's fear of control adds to resistance to help; capable of violence with little concern for consequences; strong aggressions and id impulses with little ego strength to regulate them; aroused by **sexual jealousy**; separation through alienation of affections devastates the Nine's self and inflames the Eight's wounded pride; may retaliate while dissociating emotionally from harm done; chronic depression, extreme dissociation, addiction possible.

**The Nine with a One-Wing: "The Dreamer."** [s.21]
- The traits reinforce each other (Nines repress emotions for peace, Ones for self-control): more cerebral, interested in ideas, symbols, concepts; emotionally controlled and cooler; moments of anger and moral indignation (the complacent Nine suddenly critical and sarcastic); both subtypes are drawn to philosophy and spirituality but this one has a distinctive idealistic quality. (Examples include Lincoln, Queen Elizabeth II, Rosalynn Carter, Henry Fonda, Jimmy Stewart, Garrison Keillor, Princess Grace, Joseph Campbell, Carl Jung, George Lucas, Jim Henson, Walt Disney, Norman Rockwell, Emerson, "Edith Bunker," "Marge Simpson.")
- *Healthy:* enormous integrity, principled; common sense and wise judgments about others; alert to fairness and objectivity; synthesizing schools of thought and finding common threads; imaginative, creative, sharing visions of an ideal world; share what they know and appreciate others' discoveries; sunny, friendly, reassuring but serious about ideals; enjoy teaching, may be moral leaders teaching by example; the Nine's openness with the One's objectivity gives simplicity and guilelessness toward others, peacefulness and moderation toward themselves; a good friend or therapist (nonjudgmental listening plus wisdom and advice).
- *Average:* may be crusaders (idealistic wish to improve the world), sure of their opinions and fixed on beliefs; orderly, self-controlled, less openly passionate than the Eight-wing; caught up in nonessential activities, busy within their sphere (often maintaining order and the status quo) yet detached, which derails long-range goals; less adventurous, more thoughtful and reserved; complacent, disengaged and conflict-avoidant yet easily moved to anger (testiness, edginess); restrain anger more, becoming indignant and smoldering through clenched teeth, indirect, sarcastic, scolding rather than making a scene; concerned with propriety and respectability, often feeling morally superior (other classes, cultures, lifestyles); puritanical, "prim and proper"; rationalize, moralize, appeal to ideologies; perfectionistic in some areas while neglecting more important aspects of life; surprisingly impersonal and callous because they abstract from the real world in favor of idealistic notions.
- *Unhealthy:* extremely withdrawn, may resemble unhealthy Fives: detached, schizoid, little affect; extremely angry in a compartmentalized way, impulsively "out of the blue"; more resentful than the Eight-wing, stewing over wrongs, feeling a duty to punish or condemn; arbitrary action contradicting their usual behavior; obsessive-compulsive traits (obsessing about apparent troubles while dissociating from the compulsive actions or real problems, or "forgetting" what had just been the obsession); defenses less global, so they feel conflicts more and risk more severe emotional problems or breakdowns; if untreated, retreat into highly dissociated autism-like states; helpless and despairing with sudden bursts of frustration and rage.

## Some Final Thoughts (Nine) [s.21]
Average to unhealthy Nines bring about what they most fear, loss and separation: fragmented, they are separated from others and from themselves, alienated from and terrified of their own lives; the remaining core can rebuild only with great difficulty. Relatively few Nines reach this neurosis; most deteriorate into denial and dissociation after a crisis and bounce back to some normal functioning, since their all-encompassing defenses repress most traumas, but endurance is bought at the price of an emotionally and personally impoverished life. The central problem is how to awaken to themselves and sustain self-possession; the answer given: Nines must learn to **accept suffering, especially the suffering of anxiety**. Suffering consciously accepted can catalyze and shock people into awareness and compels one to choose what it means; choosing a meaning creates oneself; one who gives meaning to suffering is "both the self who suffers and the self who transcends suffering. In that moment, the self is aware and unified." [s.21]
