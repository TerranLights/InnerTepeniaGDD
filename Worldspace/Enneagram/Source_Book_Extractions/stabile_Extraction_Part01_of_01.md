## Overview

The book is a relationship-focused companion to the same author's *The Road Back to You*. It treats the Enneagram as a "translation tool" for the "path between us": the nine types see the world differently, so every relationship requires translation. Chapters are ordered 8, 9, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 (following the author's teaching order, not numerical order). Each chapter opens with a story and "What's Going on Here?" questions, then explores how that type approaches relationships (strengths, blind spots, passion, stress and security movement), gives a compact "[type]s and Others" table (how that type does with each of the nine numbers, including itself), notes the type's limitations in relationships, closes with "The Path Together", and ends with two advice lists: one for people of that type ("Relationships for [type]s": You can / You can't / You'll need to accept) and one for everyone else ("Relationships with [type]s"). The author's recurring points: every number "avoids something"; we cannot change how we see, only adjust what we do with how we see; do not use your number as an excuse; do not use others' numbers to mock, criticize or stereotype them; work on yourself rather than on others.

## Introduction: The Enneagram Is a Journey [pp.6-16]

**Opening story [pp.6-7].** Author (a Two) and husband Joe (a Nine, bilingual) on a flight: an older couple who speak no English cannot place their luggage or sit together. The author nudged Joe to help; he did not. Later she asked why. His answer: "it never occurs to me that I should help"; he sees they struggle but does not think of getting involved. Her reply as a Two: she always knows who needs help and usually what they need, but is not always equipped to offer it. Point: "All relationships ... require translation," and the Enneagram is one of the most helpful translation tools. A closing bookend story [pp.15-16]: on another flight, remembering what Joe taught her about Nines, she stayed silent; Joe himself got up and, in Spanish, solved the problem of a passenger who could not sit in an exit row. Lesson: "When we are able to see ourselves as we are, and as we can be, it's a beautiful thing."

**Premises [pp.7-8].**
- Two things all people share: we want to belong and we want our lives to have meaning; both depend on building and keeping relationships, with people like us and often with those who are not.
- "None of us can change how we see," so we adjust what we do with how we see.
- The Enneagram teaches nine ways of experiencing the world and nine answers to: Who am I? Why am I here? Why do I do the things I do? How we build and maintain relationships varies significantly by number.
- The book explains how each number sees, makes sense of what it sees, decides what to do, and relates to others. Human interaction is "unpredictable and messy."

**Quick type refresher [pp.9-11]** (the author's own capsule descriptions):

| Number | Titles | Capsule (author's wording, condensed) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Perfectionists (dislike the title) | Struggle with anger, turned inward as resentment; constant inner critic; settle for being right or correct; judging/comparing mind; notice errors others miss and feel responsible to correct them; give their best and expect it from others. |
| 2 | Helpers or Givers | Need to be needed; give sometimes altruistically, sometimes (subconsciously) to receive; on entering a room attention goes to others ("How are you doing? What do you need? How can I be helpful?"); build relationships by sensing and meeting needs. |
| 3 | Performers | Need to be, and be seen as, successful, efficient, effective; trouble reading feelings (own and others'); hide anger, fear, sadness, disappointment and embarrassment until alone; set and reach short- and long-term goals; motivate others ("when we win, they win"). |
| 4 | Romantics | Most complex number; need to be both unique and authentic; believe something is missing; comfortable with melancholy, energized by the tragic; the only number that can bear witness to pain without fixing it; value authenticity, abhor disingenuousness, so elicit depth. |
| 5 | Observers or Investigators | Want adequate resources so they never depend on others; most emotionally detached (can have a feeling and let it go); manage fear by gathering information; limited daily energy so careful what they offer; showing up for relationships costs them more than any other number. |
| 6 | Loyalists | Need security and certainty, anxious about future events, threats, hidden agendas; manage anxiety with worst-case planning, order, rules, plans, the law; do not need to be the star; "the glue" of organizations; most concerned with the common good. |
| 7 | Epicures or Enthusiasts | Delight in the best possibilities; need to avoid pain, reframe negatives into positives; believe they have a full emotional range but live mostly on the happy side; repetition and routine are turnoffs; masters of denial, manage fear by diffusing it; uplift others. |
| 8 | Boss or Challenger | Independent thinkers; see extremes (good/bad, right/wrong, friend/foe); anger is the emotion of choice but short-lived; want and respect forthrightness; focused outward; for the underdog; most energy of any number; give everything to what they believe in. |
| 9 | Peacemakers or Mediators | Least complex; least energy because they keep in anything that causes conflict... [note: text says they "try to keep in anything that would cause conflict and keep out anything that would steal their peace"]; most stubborn; passive-aggressive anger; see two sides to everything so prone to procrastination and indecision; loyal, like closeness; self-forgetting, merge with others. |

**System overview [pp.11-14].**
- **Triads** (how you encounter information): Heart Triad (2, 3, 4) feelings dominate; Head Triad (5, 6, 7) thinking; Gut Triad (8, 9, 1) doing.
- **Four dynamic numbers** for each type: two **wings** (neighbors; affect behavior when dominant, not core motivation; e.g., a Four with a Three wing is more outgoing than a Four with a Five wing), the **stress number** (arrow pointing away; e.g., Sevens in stress draw on One: less easygoing, more black-and-white), and the **security number** (arrow pointing toward; e.g., Sevens in security draw on Five: less-is-more). Stress is not necessarily a negative move; all numbers need the security behavior "to experience holistic healing." Core motivation and number never change; mature students learn to move around the circle.
- **Stances** (habitual posture/attitude; default mode):

| Stance | Numbers | Description | Time orientation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aggressive | 3, 7, 8 | Happy to be in charge; agendas first; stand independently, at times move against others | Future |
| Dependent | 1, 2, 6 | Concerned with others' expectations; loyal, dependable; move toward others | Present |
| Withdrawing | 4, 5, 9 | Slow to act, shy or introverted; move away from others | Past |

**Advice and cautions [pp.14-15].** Read the whole book (each chapter contains info on that number in relation to the others). Use *The Path Between Us Study Guide* alone or in a group. The Enneagram is not static: we move from healthy through average to unhealthy and back; most people live in the "high-average range"; the book depicts average-to-healthy responses. Variations within a number depend on introversion/extroversion, current health level, instinct (social, one-to-one, self-preserving), time orientation, and familiarity with the Enneagram and other growth tools. Three interpersonal principles: (1) do not use your number as an excuse; (2) do not use knowledge of other numbers to make fun of, criticize, stereotype or disrespect them, "Ever"; (3) spend energy observing and working on yourself, not on others. Hope: grow in "accept, love, and walk beside one another ... with loads of compassion and respect."

## Chapter: 8s, "Vulnerability Is Not Weakness" [pp.17-36]

**Opening story [pp.17-19].** Melissa, an Eight and head of recruiting at a startup, inherited a staff including Emily, whom she would not have chosen (Eights function best choosing who they work with). Melissa works about 55 hours; Emily struggles to manage 40 and misses work events for family reasons. At a six-month review Melissa opened with "do you have anything you want to talk about?"; Emily, shaky, said she felt disrespected and bullied (others had too). Melissa asked for "objective proof" and explained business stakes. She asked the author, sincerely: "why can't people just do their jobs?" Emily asked for a transfer.
- Questions: which person do you identify with? Is Melissa a bully? What does Emily really want from Melissa? How can the Enneagram explain this?
- Author's reading: two people who see the job, and the world, from completely different perspectives. Most numbers "broker peace" through quick exchanges after friction; Eights do not feel that obligation and move to the next thing. It can look like not caring; "they simply aren't thinking about us" but about what needs doing.

**The World of Eights [pp.19-20].** First response: "What am I going to do?" (others ask "What do I think?" or "What do I feel?"). Eights get along with Threes and Sevens (also all about doing); they struggle with those burdened by feelings or who think too long before acting. Doing-first often seems aggressive to thinking and feeling types. For Fives and Sixes, quick action seems outrageous and irresponsible because options must be evaluated first. Pausing is hard because Eights want control of their inner state and the world. Others look to Eights to decide and lead and expect them to slow down, explain the plan and ask for suggestions; this does not occur to an Eight. Eight executive director Todd Dugas: realized he never trained or told staff what he really wanted, expected them to expand on basics, struggled to converse, and used to fire the barely adequate. When Eights move faster than others can follow, others go along because they have no choice, producing resentment. An Eight at home: unarticulated expectations cause misunderstandings; inclusion "requires very little time and the payoff is significant."

**Vulnerability and self-protection [pp.21-24].**
- Eights avoid vulnerability to protect themselves. As children called bossy; as adults labeled aggressive, so others adopt a defensive stance. Eights protect themselves by avoiding helplessness, weakness, subordination.
- Quotes Brené Brown: connection requires allowing ourselves "to be seen." One of the few things Eights fear is being exposed in weakness, limitation or indecision.
- An Eight mother's statement of her vulnerability: she will trust, be in your corner, "fight for you more than I will fight with you," surprise with tears, find feelings under anger, love "beyond reason."
- Example: the author's Eight daughter Joey, as a child, secretly opened and rewrapped her Christmas presents because she did not want to cry, laugh or say the wrong thing: not knowing felt vulnerable; she still wants to know everything beforehand.
- Eights abhor weakness in themselves and those close to them; if they cannot distinguish vulnerability from weakness, they avoid both. Effect on others: the other feels their presence does not matter, they have nothing to offer, they could never be trustworthy enough. Remedy: stay connected to others when they are down or things do not line up; share what means most, frightens most, matters most.
- Eights are "old souls" with unexpected wisdom; as small children uncomfortable around weak, halfhearted people, they decided to be strong, often took responsibility early; the chosen strength "costs them their innocence." Part of adult life is regaining the ability to meet the world without protecting from it; possible only "in the context of safe relationships."
- Eights have good boundaries for protecting their space but may be unaware they are entering others' business.

**Standing up for the underdog [p.24].** Preoccupation with self-protection makes them best equipped to challenge oppressors; both powerful and gentle. But they often miss "what's in it for them"; giving with marginalized people can be mutual and relational. **Social awareness** defined: the ability to pick up others' feelings and understand life from their view; requires listening and observing. Busy Eights may solve problems without it. All relationships need give-and-take (Twos err the other way, paying too much attention to social awareness).

**Intensity and anger [pp.24-26].** The Eight's passion is **lust**, defined as **intensity**: all-in, high energy, impatient drive toward action. Obstacles trigger anger. Eights believe anger has served them; the author suspects it covers softer, tender feelings, and over time they can access little else. Sadness, fear and vulnerability are masked by anger. Eight anger is often perceived as "a barrier, not a boundary"; the less aggressive types (2, 4, 6, 9) guard themselves, so the truth and authenticity Eights seek are less likely. Example: Wendi, an Eight teacher, stormed at a colleague who broke a placement agreement; the rift taught her to stop and think before confronting when angry. Eights rarely regret confrontation but miss that aggression overshadows intent. Lust/intensity/anger masquerade as deep emotion, so Eights believe they are in touch with feelings when often not; awareness of intention is needed to recognize, feel and name feelings.

**Disconnection from feelings [p.26].** Abundant passion "for everything and nothing in particular"; intensity replaces joy, sadness, vulnerability, embarrassment. Tender feelings (hurt, fear) trigger fast decisive action to feel strong again. Relationships thrive on **interdependence**, defined as "frequent but unplanned mutuality." The illusion of control is shattered by feelings Eights cannot repress (tenderness for someone fragile and strong, etc.). "Eights aren't afraid to feel—they're afraid their feelings will betray them."

**Fear of betrayal [pp.26-27].** Eights in workshops tell many betrayal stories; the author reframes ("a mistake? a poor choice, not betrayal?") and they are surprised that it may look different from the other's view. Joey: she experiences betrayal daily when people who do not know her judge her and feed the judgment with their own feelings without trying to know her. Eights do not want to hurt loved ones; discovering they have is devastating even if composed.

**Stress and security [pp.27-31].**
- Healthy Eights in relationship: positive, playful, generous, accepting, committed, truthful, supportive provided others are committed to success. Unhealthy: combative, possessive, arrogant, uncompromising, quick to find fault (an Eight mother: "I'm raising men, not weak little boys"). Neediness in others is usually an attempt at connection; there is something to gain by reaching back.
- Initial response to stress for every number: exaggerate the number's behavior. Eights in stress: aggression, intensity, self-assurance. Primary defense mechanism: **denial of all feelings** that arise; it pushes them to work harder when they need to stop.
- Eights find comfortable what others find stressful (deadlines, confrontations, crises). One Eight: "I walk in like the Terminator, looking to identify friend or foe."
- Most Eights believe they can change reality to conform to their view. They deny their own limits (so are "all in or not in at all") and, without Enneagram knowledge or life experience, deny others' limits.
- Male Eights in stress refuse to stop, expect more of everyone, view those who cannot keep up as suspect; anger fills a room; failure may lead to retreat but more often continuing; stress illnesses (heart attacks, strokes). Female Eights in high stress are bossy and strident, refuse to be appeased; when they hit the wall they slide down it, cry, and go to bed; healthy ones announce a return, unhealthy ones leave no doubt they are angry and unavailable.
- **Stress move to Five:** intuitively pull back from the world; good, gives space to think and re-engage. **Security move to Two:** connect emotionally, exchanging tenderness usually covered by aggression.

**8s and Others (pairing table) [p.29]:**

| With | What the book says |
|---|---|
| 1 | Both committed and energetic, but focus differs: Ones on the problem, Eights on the solution. |
| 2 and 5 | Eights share a line with Twos and Fives (to Two in security, to Five in stress). From Twos Eights need tenderness, affection, awareness of others' feelings; from Fives, the ability to move slowly, gather information before acting, and appreciate the value of neutrality. |
| 3 and 7 | Do really well: all think fast, work hard, play hard, get a lot done, are not prone to tender feelings; all future-oriented and energetic; great coworkers and colleagues. |
| 4 | Hard for Eights to be present to a Four's mood changes. Once allowed, they find much in common: the most intense and passionate numbers, both committed to honesty regardless of cost. |
| 6 | Eights lack patience to wait for Sixes. Sixes are methodical and see in real time, Eights focus on the future. If Eights listen and wait, win/win. |
| 8 | Lots of passion; one will need to focus inward at times; responsibilities must be divided; an Eight with a Seven wing differs from an Eight with a Nine wing. |
| 9 | Fascinating when both are mature and healthy. Nines need their own energy, agenda, and sense of what is theirs to do; when Eights willingly and intentionally follow Nines, "that can be a beautiful thing." |

**Limitations in relationships [pp.31-33].** Eights value relationships but do not need or have time for many; usually not friends with coworkers (collegial, good team players; real social connection lives elsewhere). They prefer a few friends who value independence; friendship requires trustworthy, safe, reliable, expectation-free. An apprentice: impossible to have a meaningful relationship with someone who cannot stand up for themselves. Imbalance: overemphasis on doing and habitual ignoring of feelings. Example: Jeff, oldest of three boys, took over care for aging mother and all funeral tasks; his twelve-year-old daughter asked why he never cried; he believed that giving way to emotion would have left no one to do the work. "What we see and the way we see also determine what we miss": Eights do not know that lack of vulnerability tells others they are not trusted with who the Eight really is; long-term relationships are built "by walking beside someone, neither leading nor following." Eights are usually in charge, so must remember mutuality and collaboration. Quoted pastor Nadia Bolz-Weber: leadership with opt-in guilds requires letting go of control and predictability and checking in "with myself and ... the room," honestly and quickly, aware her voice carries weight. The author: Eights think they protect themselves by being in charge, but always leading can be isolating and keeps them from learning to handle surprises. Each number protects itself from something: Nines from conflict, Sevens from pain, Fours from abandonment, Eights from unexpected emotions.

**The Path Together [pp.33-34].** Eights are clear about who has access to their tender side. Joey's cupped-hands image: "I only have this much 'warm and fuzzy'" which goes to husband and sons, the remainder to prospective clients; she does not inquire into colleagues' lives or share hers; at work: do the job well and share camaraderie of that effort; "I have friends. I do not come to work to explore friendships." Counsel: bring feelings into balance; because they rarely appear in an Eight's arsenal "they are one of the purest parts of your being"; stop and consider how you feel before acting.

**Relationships for Eights [pp.34-35]:**
- *You can:* lead if people are willing to follow (requires respecting how they see); lead and plan though you cannot control outcomes; hire aggressive people but always work with non-aggressive ones; learn moderation, collaboration, patience with self-awareness to practice them; protect yourself emotionally while remembering you cannot avoid vulnerability.
- *You can't:* be fully heard without considering the other eight ways of receiving information; avoid or deny vulnerability and succeed in relationships; always lead (learn to follow another leader gracefully); affect the world without being affected; solve every problem with action and strength; share feelings you have not allowed yourself to experience.
- *Accept that:* you are not always right; you cannot guarantee outcome; you thrive on stress but others do not; there is something bigger than yourself to focus on.

**Relationships with Eights [pp.35-36]:**
- Their aggression is not personal; they mean no harm; do not be misled by strong opinions.
- Strong as they are, they still need care.
- If you do not stand up for yourself, are not forthcoming, honest, or are indirect, you are invisible to Eights.
- Do not beat around the bush: brief, straightforward, truthful.
- Eights control in relationships because they do not want to be controlled.
- Bring best effort; do what you say; show you are all in or not.
- Do not talk behind their back ("Tell me. I can handle it.").
- If unhappy with you, they tell you; if distant without telling, it likely is not about you.
- Acknowledge contributions but do not flatter (neither trust nor need flattery).
- Encourage regular exercise to use up excess energy.
- They sometimes mistake tenderness for manipulation.
- Intensity is always well received; be secure and strong in what you believe.
- They are often unaware of how they affect others.

## Chapter: 9s, "Risking Conflict for Connection" [pp.37-54]

**Opening story [pp.37-39].** Nine musician Andy Gullahorn, at sixteen or seventeen, played rhythm guitar in a cover band at a fire hall in Pedernales, Texas, when Willie Nelson (whose birthday it was, adjacent farm) joined for a couple of hours. Andy was content backing him; they never met beyond "Thanks"/"Thank you." Questions: how would you imagine the night; how would your response differ; how does the Enneagram explain the difference? Author's reading: Nines can emotionally detach even from a once-in-a-lifetime event; we learn how we relate to day-to-day people by how we relate to strangers; "Nines have the ability to disconnect in any relationship." **Nines tend to erase themselves from events.** Being relational is an experience of vulnerability; Nines manage it by assuring themselves their presence does not matter. But Andy's presence mattered to everyone but Andy (he knew all the songs and any key). When Nines entertain the idea that their presence matters, all encounters improve, especially with those they love.

**The World of Nines [pp.39-41].** Least energy of all numbers; easygoing, undemanding, "don't rock the boat" because intensity and desires have led to trouble; maintaining the easygoing approach costs much energy. Story: Joe promised to spoil the author for a weekend (breakfast in bed, coffee), then vanished into a chain of distractions (cat at door, newspaper, Home Depot ad for weed eater twine, mowing the lawn). Author felt "forgotten, unimportant, and overlooked." Lesson: Nines' lives are "made up of distractions"; they follow whatever passes in front of them; when they get distracted from you, it is not about you but about how they see; they constantly address their propensity for distraction.

**Conflict and self-protection [pp.41-42].** Nines avoid conflict; few things are worth arguing about unless integrity is involved ("the little stuff"). People are drawn to Nines but the Nine feels others want too much (who they are, what they stand for). Nines merge with others' ideas and agendas to avoid conflict, leaving others confused about whether the Nine can be trusted. Dr. Chris Gonzalez (Nine, family therapist): expression of personality changes radically depending on who he is with (emotional with the expressive, submissive with dominant or angry people, to appease and maximize peace). Nines can lean into difficult conversations by saying what they think and naming preferences "without losing anything"; otherwise the other person is left "to guess, suppose, and hope" (exhausting, unhealthy, unproductive). Mike McHargue ("Science Mike", Nine): healthy conflict produces intimacy and strengthens relationships; the Enneagram gave him that insight.

**Sloth [p.42].** Passion of Nines. Redefined (via a pastor in Abilene, Texas): "not lazy" but getting busy doing something that keeps you from what you ought to be doing. A Nine: very good at doing things and convincing everyone they are important; "energized by my neglect of that other thing." Nines get energy from avoiding what needs doing; some of their best, most creative thinking happens while avoiding. Those in relationship misread it as laziness and assume they must do it themselves.

**Right action [pp.43-44].** The "safety net" counterpart to merging in inconsequential matters: choosing to act despite the potential for conflict and negative consequences. Nines are not wishy-washy or without boundaries, but have limited energy and are mindful of its use; many things others take seriously Nines cannot get worked up about. When something is consequential for a lifetime, Nines are "forthcoming, courageous, committed, and determined." Science Mike: confident speaking of belief creates a methodology that makes peacemaking successful. Disagreement over vacation or dinner can unnerve a Nine, but for important, conflictual situations they proceed without hesitation; there is self-worth in standing up for something too important to compromise for "pseudopeace." Appearing distracted or disengaged does not necessarily mean lack of interest.

**Anger [pp.44-47].** Nines, with Eights and Ones, are in the **Anger Triad** (top of the Enneagram). Eights are clear about anger and wait for a response; Nines are passive-aggressive, quietly stubborn ("perhaps the most stubborn number"), refuse to be nagged, pushed or coerced. Ways of showing hurt or anger are all indirect (vague suggestions, nonverbal). They are not good tacticians when angry, buy time with passive expressions; fear fragmentation from direct exchanges and fear being unable to contain their anger once voiced. If you engage them angrily they sit it out, then methodically avoid you and the subject, believing time fixes it; leaving usually increases frustration. Example: Joe calls her home office an hour and a half after leaving for work; when something is wrong, he calls late morning, denies a problem and says he will call back. He wants her to acknowledge his unhappiness, figure out why, avoid it in the future, and go on. After years of work he communicates more directly.

**Stress and security [pp.47-48].** In stress: withdrawn, indecisive, noncommunicative, secretive, stubborn; absent emotionally and physically, or unable or unwilling to risk connecting; do not want to be controlled or affected by anything, so diminish perceived obstacles; risk missing opportunities. **Stress move to Six:** healthier Six behavior makes Nines more outspoken, especially for the common good; example: daughter Jenny, a Nine school administrator, became the voice for the faculty when the pay scale changed unfairly. Nines care about justice. **Security move to Three:** performing, doing, accomplishing; set attainable goals; partners and coworkers are pleased when Nines step up confident. "Some Enneagram numbers prefer predictability while others require spontaneity."

**9s and Others (pairing table) [p.46]:**

| With | What the book says |
|---|---|
| 1 | When Ones have thought things through and are ready to move, Nines often want to stay on the fence. Nines must remember there is also a time for action. |
| 2 | Common and usually successful. Nines can teach Twos that responding to every feeling picked up from others is neither necessary nor healthy, and help Twos recognize their tendency to overreach into others' lives. |
| 3 and 6 | Nines share a line with Threes and Sixes (to Six in stress, to Three in security). From Threes: confidence that they have something valuable to offer and that their full participation is welcomed. From Sixes: awareness of times to accept that not everyone can be trusted; their desire to be unaffected by life is challenged if they refuse to acknowledge this. |
| 4 | In a long-term relationship with a Four, a Nine's lack of intensity can become a problem. |
| 5 | Nines will have to ask for what they want and need with a Five; if they commit to that, good for both. |
| 7 | Share a wonderful view of the world; both open-minded, appreciate diversity. Nines must be careful about going along to avoid fragmentation. Sevens need Nines to be mindful of consequences (not Sevens' strong suit). |
| 8 | Nines cannot allow Eights to define the relationship; unfair and disrespectful to both. Can be a great combination if Nines show up and are real. |
| 9 | Each should lead the way in questioning the status quo. |

**Limitations in relationships [pp.48-50].** Harder for a Nine than any other number to know who they are in a relationship; desire to be unaffected by life and propensity to merge. Gonzalez: "like looking into a foggy mirror"; with someone of good boundaries he automatically merges because separating takes too much energy; alone he thinks clearly and knows what he wants; "a weird amnesia about me." Others like merging (we get our way), then are surprised when a conflicting strong opinion appears; consistency in verbalizing what they think, want and need is better. Communication with a Nine can be confusing: they say what you want or say nothing, seeing that as protecting the relationship; until they do personal work they do not see that they introduce **fragmentation** instead of preservation. Nines do best with someone to help hold them accountable for goals. Nines are not prone to peer pressure: merging is for matters of little consequence; they are independent thinkers, content to act alone, will not risk integrity to join a group, but are quiet about disapproval. "Nines won't jump off the bridge, but they will let you jump if you want to." Least controlling of all numbers; appreciate independence "a two-way street."

**The Path Together [pp.50-51].** Story of Patsy (teacher of visually impaired children): an ophthalmologist gave parents glasses simulating their children's vision; parental expectations turned to disbelief and pride. Bill Millican (Nine, mediator, lost sight at 37): a good mediator or peacemaker can see, understand and empathize with every side; "A chance to be understood as they talk about life as they see it." Our judgments are formed without seeing as others see. Nines see two sides to everything, "for better and for worse," making decisions hard and leading to merging; but healthy Nines who articulate what they want are usually headed in the right direction for the right reasons.

**Relationships for Nines [pp.52-53]:**
- *You can:* learn tools for avoiding some conflicts and resolving others; manage the tension between regretting being compliant and fearing being noncompliant; recognize that relationships are best when you keep your identity; engage conflict healthily (avoiding conflict often causes conflict).
- *You can't:* always avoid conflict; ignore that "later" is not a point in time; avoid fragmentation and loss (some relationships do not last and perhaps were not meant to); expect people to read your mind; be healthy and whole if you spend your life setting yourself aside to stay connected.
- *Accept that:* problems do not fix themselves; there are times you must assert yourself regardless of cost; no healthy relationship is void of anger, disappointment and conflict; your presence matters a lot (they rely on, trust, and want your full involvement).

**Relationships with Nines [pp.53-54]:** Nines need lots of affirmation and affection; if a Nine says an honest "no," say how much you appreciate it and that you are not going away.
- Encourage them to name desires, dreams, preferences and to develop their own identity in relationship.
- They appreciate, perhaps need, a peaceful environment; want and need alone time, space, independence.
- Avoid "Don't you think we should ___?" (answer will be yes, not necessarily what they think); ask "Do you think we should ___?"
- Focus on what they do, not what they forget.
- Do not interrupt; let them meander, they get to the point.
- They have a generous spirit; easy to take advantage of.
- They dislike confrontation but you should not never confront them; encourage them to share grievances.
- They want clear, direct communication about expectations.
- When distracted by nonessentials, redirect by asking questions.
- Agreement does not always lead to participation, and participation does not necessarily indicate commitment.
- They do not make personal decisions quickly and do not usually want your help.

## Chapter: 1s, "Things Could Always Be Better" [pp.55-70]

**Opening story [pp.55-57].** At an introductory Enneagram conference in Nashville, a well-dressed young couple, Amanda and Christopher, grew distressed when Ones were taught; the author suspected both were Ones. Ones hearing their number taught feel relief and surprise; they sit up when told about their gift for assessing situations and seeing potential, and about the **inner critic** that tells them they could do and be better. Amanda: "nothing is ever good enough"; Christopher: "Good enough is good enough for everybody else, but I've got to be right ... I don't want to make a C, I want to make an A+." Questions: what is it like to have a relentless inner critic; how does it affect relationships; difference between being good and being right?

**Naming and framing [pp.56-57].** Ones dislike "Perfectionist"; Ones the author knows suggested "Reformer" or "Evaluator." Perfection "is like an ice sculpture: it lasts only as long as there's no change in the atmosphere." Unlike Ones, some numbers do not notice what is out of place and others notice but do not care. Partners of Ones must contend with a critic they cannot hear that tells Ones they are flawed in an unredeemable way; their coping is to find fault in others. "They believe criticism is caring" and must be taught not everyone feels the same about correction. For couples of two Ones: shared desire for perfection but different priorities about what to perfect; challenge is accepting imperfection.

**The World of Ones [pp.57-59].** First response: "How can I make this better?" Others think "How can I finish this and move on?" or "Surely this is good enough." Ones struggle to respect people who do not give their best; accepting less makes them miserable. Story: a student-seminarian assembled a bookcase missing two nails, a screw and a decorative cover; he heard the author's voice ("Who does that?!") and built it anyway, then could not stand the sight of it, moved it out of sight, and was disappointed in himself. "There isn't another number on the Enneagram that demands so much and rewards so sparingly." Perfection "lasts about as long as a snowflake in the noonday sun."

**Anger [pp.59-61].** Image: a pressure cooker with a regulator that rocks and blows (author's mother's canning). Ones seethe; anger usually shows as **resentment**, not yelling: turned inward first, it feels like **shame** about the faults of self and others, which adds a bitter quality and "complex resentment." After finishing a project Ones often focus on what could have been done better. Story: Jenay (One; high school administrator; mother of three boys) forgot the camera on a Sedona day trip and bought a disposable camera; she was grouchy, her husband said "let this go." Ones miss the bigger picture by compulsively focusing on what is wrong. "Ones are often frustrated yet are seldom angered by what they appear to be angry about": she was angered because forgetting meant she had failed. Pattern: Ones deny, stuff, rename, move on, then manage by perfecting something in their control (checklists). Ones love deeply and well, protect and care, are thoughtful and supportive, yet are hard to get right with; they are much harder on themselves than others and regret limited patience and unreasonable expectations, especially at home. Ones are usually the first to respond to a need.

**Disconnection from thinking [pp.62-63].** Ones see life as responsibility and work, focus on what is in front of them and respond by doing; this limits energy for emotional needs in relationships. They believe they think all the time; but an ongoing internal dialogue with the critic "is not the same thing as thinking"; often it is just responding to the critic's prompts. Mary (Episcopal priest): she cannot relax, and so signals to the household that no one else can ("If I'm working you guys need to be working"). Balancing doing with awareness of feelings and discernment changes choices.

**Fear of being bad [p.63].** Fear of Ones: being bad. They came to believe meeting others' expectations would make them valuable and secure, so relationships became about "performing well than relating well and loving well." The more they focus on what others want, the more they lose touch with their own needs; goodness is measured by how they measure up; rules, standards and directives become boundaries for judging the day; they cannot meet their own standards. For those in relationship: honesty is essential, but telling Ones they are good in ways they can hear is "the greater gift."

**Stress and security [pp.64-65].**
- Healthy: relaxed, charming, fun, conscientious; make room for different opinions of a "good job." Low average: argumentative, uncompromising, many expectations, resentment, picky, unpredictable, efforts to please them fail. Excess in any number separates us from those we care about.
- High stress: Ones assign moral value to mistakes; a missed deadline escalates from "lazy or indifferent" to "useless or no good," labels that can permanently wound; this comes from how they berate themselves; other numbers do not abuse themselves so struggle to tolerate it. Natural response to stress: perfect something ("When the world is going to hell, I clean my bathroom").
- **Stress move to Four:** access feelings that do not need fixing; the critic is less loud about feelings than about actions, giving a reprieve.
- **Security move to Seven:** relax; less judgment, more acceptance and peace; fun.

## Chapter: 2s, "Your Feelings or Mine?" [pp.71-]

**Opening story [pp.71-72].** Hunter (Two; full-time pastor and part-time attorney). Law: always seeking the one fact the client has not said; intuiting what is left out. He loses himself in helping; hard to know who he is alone; wants appreciation, does not want to ask, cannot trust or savor praise (after a sermon wants a line of compliments but deflects them by redirecting to the other person). Questions: do you wonder what people leave out; does the impulse to sense others' needs sound familiar; why would saying what he needs cause disconnect; do you trust affirmation? Author: Twos read the world with feelings, connect by picking up others' feelings and respond by doing something; they express emotions so easily it seems the feelings are theirs, but "Twos feel the feelings of others and find it very difficult to tell you what they actually feel."

**The World of Twos [pp.73-74].** Everything is relational; Twos connect with almost everyone and build relationships with everyone regularly engaged; it is how they know themselves (author introduced herself by relationships, "I am Joe's wife," until about fifty). Twos do not know who they are unless they get it from someone else. A Two on silent retreat can think only about relationships; prays, reads for others; the hardest questions are "What do you want?" and "What are you feeling?" 
- **Pride** is the Two's passion (sin): everyone is their responsibility; pride in meeting needs. Hunter: childhood devastation if someone did not like him; "pride of being the best, the most helpful, and the most loved." Riso and Hudson (quoted): pride is "the inability or unwillingness to acknowledge one's own needs and suffering while tending to the needs of someone else." Two reasons personal work is needed: Twos do not see themselves as worthy of needing help; and fear that if no one responds to a stated need they cannot bear the pain. Relationships need reciprocity: ask directly, then "cultivate the grace to receive it." If not, **manipulation**, which makes connection dishonest (contrast: "I wish I didn't have to cook dinner tonight" vs. "Do you mind if we go out to dinner tonight? I'm tired..."). Manipulation and martyrdom come with anger from unmet expectations and feeling taken for granted; healthy Twos name needs and ask. Twos often assume others need their help and protection.

**The vulnerability of generosity [pp.74-75].** Giving feels like strength but contains vulnerability. When desire to give is multiplied, limits show; satisfaction fades when others' expectations exceed gratitude; having initiated a relationship, Twos struggle to disengage. The recipient is confused when the Two grows distant; the Two gives until empty, feels unappreciated, taken for granted, tired and afraid, because self-worth is determined by giving. Twos are more comfortable with affection than any other number. They try to have relationships with everyone (servers, groomer, plumber), some not appropriate, healthy or reciprocal. When Twos say drained, resentful, tired, frustrated, it is often their way of asking for help.

**Disconnection from productive thinking [pp.75-77].** Twos read others' feelings then do something; they do not think about what they experience, so lack of productive thinking shows only when they stop; cost of overdoing, overgiving, overrelating, underthinking. Anxiety and fatigue signal: stop, consider commitments, discern what is theirs to do, pass on the rest, think instead of reacting. Story in Assisi with Richard Rohr: a sour-faced man in the audience; she shouted at him assuming he did not speak English; he said "I speak English ... I don't like you and I don't like what you're saying." Rohr: "you're still going after the one" while holding two hundred people in her hand. "Twos often go after the one they don't have, risking relationships with others in the process." To avoid loneliness Twos avoid the questions Who do I belong to? Am I worth belonging to anyone? Is anyone going to be there for me? They respond by doubling efforts to help, assuming the other is needy. "Our Enneagram passions teach us lessons we need to learn."

## Chapter: 3s, "Being Everyone but Myself" [pp.86-100]

I want others to take care of me ... hard for me to have the emotional energy to do the same in return." **The Path Together [pp.97-98].** Courtney Pinkerton (Three; holistic life coach; moved to Nicaragua) on a beach asked her husband Richard about goals; he just enjoyed the moment. Her reflection: partnership roles (dreamer/doer, artist/checkbook manager, life coach/data guru) are fine if you stay nimble and take turns; roles reflect the voices we carry inside. **Relationships for Threes [pp.98-99]:** Threes help others define and reach potential. Quoting Richard Rohr: "the saddest number on the Enneagram is an unsuccessful Three"; Threes should expand their definition of success.

- Do not interrupt when working on a project.

Elizabeth: Four children who cannot express feelings at home cannot feel special, "try to capture some of what's lost by lying," get caught and shamed; they "confuse being special or unique with externalized specialness"; "It feels like the essence of who I am couldn't be received so I had to manufacture it ... you trade your authenticity for it." What is "best" differs: Eights offer strength; for Twos "best" is whatever is personal; for Fours "best" requires genuineness. "There are at least nine different understandings of what is 'best' in relationships." Tension: desire to be seen as unique versus laissez-faire attitude about making connections; adapting to belong feels like selling out; choosing the authentic self can feel like sacrificing real relationship.

**Envy [pp.104-105].** Passion/sin of Fours. Not jealousy: they do not want your job, house or car but "your comfort in the world," a less threatening, less complicated life, your happiness (as perceived). Envy reminds them they differ and makes them feel trapped: they want predictability and comfort but also an authentic life like no other. They "long so deeply for what they don't have that they often miss what they do have." In relationships, everyone feels they are failing.

**Shame [pp.105-106].** Average Fours spend energy maintaining a self-image based on feelings, daydreams and past stories, create and sustain moods that suit them, miss authentic feelings. Immature Fours may choose the victim role to feel valued when someone attends to their distress. All is an effort to escape shame from believing they do not measure up. The **Heart Triad** (2, 3, 4) need to learn that who you are has nothing to do with what people think and nothing to do with your past; in trouble, all three first believe the fault is theirs and that they must do more, be more, be exceptional to be lovable. With a Two, Three or Four, the big question: "Who are you in a relationship with?" (the real person or the one they pretend to be). Shape-shifting is harder for Fours than for Twos and Threes; great sadness when Fours try to be whoever you might love. How the triad substitutes for real feelings: Twos attend others' feelings and ignore their own; Threes find feelings burdensome and set them aside; Fours want the same satisfaction, but when a valued relationship is breached, ordinary feelings and results do not suffice. Fours exacerbate feelings to fit their sense of loss (e.g., sad music, sad movies, old photos, "very gratifying sadness"); "an extraordinary friendship ... should never be remembered in an ordinary way." Sometimes Fours overshare, over-explain, overtalk, creating obligation others cannot meet.

**Avoiding ordinariness [p.107].** A way of protecting authenticity; they avoid the current, conventional, "normal"; "I'm afraid of average and I do not see 'normal' as a goal." They want to fit in but cannot; feel slightly out of step; socializing with them, you may feel it too. Fours are very special but do not always know it, so try too hard to create specialness. They seldom are ordinary; embracing ordinary tasks and predictable routine enhances relationships.

**Stress and security [pp.107-110].** In stress (unhealthy range): excess obvious, destructive, "strangely comforting"; self-awareness becomes self-indulgence; authenticity devolves into stubborn attachment to being different; pretentious; pretend to be unavailable hoping to be pursued; repressing doing, they lack stamina and determination; shame makes them feel incapable of changing the status quo; hopelessness that others feel in trying to help. **Stress move to Two:** focus outside themselves, abandoning the myopic inner landscape (Michelle, a pastor's wife, as a child was hypervigilant to others' emotion; wondered what was wrong with everyone and with her; college mentor and women friends helped her feel likeable). Positive side of Two access: more nurturing, aware of what is outside as well as inside. **Security move to One:** more disciplined, productive, creative; the helpful One influence is having feelings without expressing or acting on them; more successful relating to others.

Conforming to fit in is their greatest liability: isolation undesirable, inauthenticity unacceptable. Bob Dylan "the consummate Four" (song "Positively 4th Street": loneliness plus fidelity to authenticity); best hope in relationship with Fours: believe they too are trying to find a way to connect. Example: youngest son BJ (Four), in the Texas Boys Choir, wanted to connect with boys he could not; "stubborn desire to be important to those who were unavailable." **The Path Together [pp.113-114].** Portrait painter Elizabeth's artist's statement: work is "an often futile but ravenous and utterly unfulfillable desire to remain wide awake" to what and who is around her, to find beauty (Baudelaire, "the correspondent of Heaven"; Maritain: love is caused by the beauty of what we love); "I go around subconsciously constructing a world that is easier for me to love"; to fall in love with a person requires first perceiving their beauty, which depends on the intentionality of our seeing; "We must occupy ourselves to occupy the other." (e.e. cummings: "to become alive; or one's self, means everything"). Author: "You know not everybody wants that, right?" and they laughed. When Fours trust the relationship, they can accommodate your awareness of their uniqueness without feeling their authenticity was sacrificed.

**Relationships for Fours [pp.114-115]:** Fours "have the gifts and the grace to hold both beauty and pain without the need to choose one over the other"; must let go of the idea that they are flawed in some significant way.
- *You can:* surround yourself with beauty (not all the time); bear witness to pain without fixing it; learn to seek normalcy inside expectations of the exceptional; create your own image and express yourself in manifold ways (not everyone will approve or understand).
- *You can't:* live an exceptional life based on fantasy; have lots of people who can accommodate your emotional volatility; have more than one or two people who give back in equal measure.
- *Accept that:* people might understand and like you (you are lovable, enough); life is ordinary, and that is okay; there is no perfect relationship (perfection lies in accepting what is and making it better); most people do not value authenticity as you do; you will have a few deep friendships rather than many shallow ones.

**Relationships with Fours [pp.115-117]:** Fours need trust and steady attention; want authenticity (suspicion of pretense may end trust); prefer fewer, mutually loving one-on-one relationships to many social contacts. Those who do not shy from intensity (and value it), stay calm when Fours are volatile, and cultivate mutuality will find Fours wonderful friends.
- Tell them when you feel pressure to be more than you can be or handle more than your part.
- They need to be both unique and authentic; some compromise at times; be honest about how their style affects your life.
- They long for what they lack and are comfortable with longing; it is not for you to fix.
- Tell them how their mood changes affect you.
- Do not say "cheer up" (usually neither sad nor depressed; comfortable with melancholy); it is okay for you to be lighthearted.
- Model balance and stay present during moodiness.
- Do not accuse them of being too sensitive or overreacting.
- They feel not good enough or not liked: acknowledge their feelings; "Affirm that the feeling is valid for them without affirming the premise it is based on"; remind them you see them as more competent, valuable, lovable than they see themselves in the moment.
- Workplace: set clear expectations, trust them to do their own work, give creative freedom, recognize strengths.
- Do not take the pull-push dynamic personally; allow room to process feelings.

## Chapter: 5s, "My Fences Have Gates" [pp.118-]

**Opening story [pp.118-120].** The author (a Two) and her friend Carolyn (a Five, friends for 48 years; the author's mother was also a Five). Carolyn left a brief voicemail, which alarmed the author because Carolyn rarely calls. Carolyn reveals mammogram follow-ups with two suspicious spots; she had told Jenny but not Joey because the movie night with Joey was going so well. The author (Two) insisted on going with her; Carolyn said she would be fine and was "saving me for the big stuff"; closed with "I love you, love you, love you" which shifted focus to the author. Questions: which person do you identify with; how would you handle a friend going alone; what solution honors both; how does the Enneagram explain? Author's reading: Fives are in the **Fear Triad** (left side) and manage fear by gathering information and knowledge, shared thoughtfully and methodically; it is standard for them to withhold pieces, share feelings with only one or two people, manage reactions with thinking. Fives' boundaries allow exchange of personal information, only on their terms. Most Five feelings are "translated into thinking before they are shared"; it is not that Fives lack feelings but that "they struggle to hold them long enough to articulate and share them." The Two nurtures feelings (journals, shares, links to the past). In this friendship the solution is not to meet in the middle (unrealistic, inauthentic) but to "allow each other the room to deal with life in our own way, make space for our differences, and respect the needs of the other."
[note: the text here labels the Fear Triad as being on the left side; elsewhere the Head Triad is 5, 6, 7.]

**The World of Fives [pp.120-122].** First response: "What do I think?" Fives, Sixes and Sevens manage everything in their heads. Fives prefer **convergent thinking** (one correct answer) to **divergent thinking** (creative ideas from exploring many sources). Lives well planned; spontaneity uncomfortable; predictable schedule, chosen route to work, organized calendar; sample morning routine (blinds, coffee, newspaper, toast, shower, bed, leave at the same time). Integrating other people is a challenge. "Relationships are risky for Fives." Nita Andrews (Five with a Four wing): "The measured risk is what a Five is after"; they test the waters and test again; as an isolated child she lived under a piano with a lamp, book, and sheet: "my fort for my Five-ness"; "only room for one person under the piano." To get a Five to emerge you generally must request time; an average Five evaluates the request's validity, weighs time and energy. Fives are misunderstood by Feeling Triad types (2, 3, 4), who do not understand assessing time and energy in relationships. Fives would do well to explain their way of seeing and needs early. Others must learn that for Fives alone time is a must.

**Independence, privacy, self-protection [pp.122-].** Fives avoid being dependent on others; they over-value independence; boundaries are second nature. Limited capacity for interaction is a significant obstacle; limited energy: Eights have the most, Nines the least, but Fives have "an allotted amount of energy for every day, and when it's gone for that day, it's gone" like manna (enough for the day, no storing leftovers). They see independence as the key to managing low energy; irony: the real solution is **interdependence** (real, authentic relationship), which most Fives do not understand until midlife; they find interaction a challenge.
Older Fives wish connecting were easier and say they missed much in life because of their need for solitude [pp.122-123]. Fives are great listeners (interested in anything, no desire to be center of attention). Comfortable with social topics (where we live, work, weather, sports); on personal narrative or controversial views they withdraw ("I don't offer my thoughts or my story to many people. It's about preserving my privacy"); sharing details takes energy and leads to more questions. **Compartmentalization** (a Five: reluctant to introduce work people to church friends, gym friends to family: "They all know me in different ways ... And I like it that way"). Too much privacy or independence is a problem for relationships.

**Avarice [p.124].** The Five's passion: not greed in general usage but believing they do not have enough inner resources for life's demands, including relationships; their greed is for privacy and independence. Passions "teach us lessons we need to learn." Many relationships are built on exchange of ideas or expertise or on allowing someone to meet a need; the commitment to provide everything for themselves hinders connection. Relationships improve when Fives consider differing ideas and accept help. Fives see life through a **lens of scarcity**, withholding resources so their needs never burden others; false: "The vulnerability of need is one of the ways we learn to love, but it has to be a two-way street."

**Disconnection from doing [pp.124-125].** With Fours and Nines, Fives see when action is needed but are blind to the possibility that they should act; they wonder, analyze, suggest, rarely initiate; they repress ability to affect the world; cycle: belief they cannot make a difference leads to inaction, which feeds the belief. Implications: Fives often do not do their part at work and home; those who love them struggle when they will not act on their behalf when most needed.

**Stress and security [pp.125-128].** Michael Gungor (Five singer-songwriter): the Enneagram is not a pigeonhole but "here's a place where you hover." **Stress move to Seven; security move to Eight**: "among the strangest moves on the Enneagram," causing big problems in relationships if not understood. In excess, the Five's world shrinks; less concerned for others' needs, more for their own privacy and security; no room for others (a student: "When I'm overwhelmed ... I just evacuate."). In stress (Seven): unusual since normally self-contained; healthier Seven side gives freedom to lean into others' suggestions, more fun, less restrained, more comfortable in the world and in company. Fives' "dry humor" can be cynical or sarcastic, misunderstood especially by feeling types; Seven influence softens it. Security (Eight): more spontaneous, outspoken, connected to feelings, less afraid of activity; abundance in relationships; invest more in others and receive greater return, "dispelling the myth that giving and connection will always be too costly." Fives maintain status quo, avoid change and risk, restrict involvement, which limits access to both Seven and Eight energy.

Story: Carolyn, tired on the way home from a camping weekend: "I'm looking forward to being home and by myself!" Relating is costly; an entire day of solitude after being present is common; also needed to process experiences against what they already hold to be true. Carolyn's **"magic shield"**: puts it on when too uncomfortable; the author's experiment: Carolyn attended a church fellowship supper wearing the shield, and none of the people asked had seen her, even those at her table; the author notes that people who ask questions at events whom she does not remember in the audience are almost always Fives; "Fives can hide in any group, whether it be seven people or seven hundred." **Relationships for Fives [pp.131-132]:** Fives are the only number capable of true neutrality (gift to offer).
- *You can:* survive being seen and known before you are completely ready; have lasting friendships of soft and subtle familiarity; find measured ways of being in the world that do not deplete energy; be in an intimate relationship without risking more than you can stand to lose.
- *You can't:* live without needing help sometimes; be competent in all areas always ("The need to learn is not incompetence, it's inexperience"); have healthy relationships without giving up some time, some privacy, and finding a way to offer and receive affection; know everything.
- *Accept that:* relationships will require more or less from you by season of life (more in midlife than in the last third; and because of grace you will have what you need); you value thinking over feeling and doing, but many do not, so balance thinking with some emotion and action; the outer world has value beyond gathering information; relationships cannot always be on your terms.

**Relationships with Fives [pp.132-133]:** Fives measure events by cost in money, energy, time, privacy and affection; they usually do not know what to give so hold back; tell them you notice when they give unasked.
- Be clear about what you need and want without being demanding.
- They do not always pick up innuendo or indirect suggestion; be forthright and direct but not wordy.
- Agree on a time to discuss a problem; give time to think; limit the conversation. Suggested language: "I want to tell you what I want and then you can tell me whether you can give it to me."
- Do not push them to socialize.
- They have trouble finding their place in what is happening: "Would you like to sit with us? We have an extra chair," followed by an introduction.
- If you ask what they feel, they tell what they think; be persistent to reach the feeling level.
- Incompetence and inadequacy are central to the Five's fear; never discuss lightly.
- They need to know what is expected; they like details.
- They want never to depend on someone for care; if they need yours, offer it with few words and little fanfare.

## Chapter: 6s, "Question Everything" [pp.134-]

**Opening story [pp.134-136].** Two musician Sixes: **Jill**, a phobic Six in Nashville, happy to come alongside others and support their vision; quotes David Wilcox: pop shows convince you "they're different and ... special, while folk musicians are trying to convince everyone that we're all the same"; she wants people to know she is just like them. **Dana**, a counterphobic Six and director of music and arts at a big downtown Dallas church: found she enjoyed helping others find their voices, diagnosing and fixing problems; as leader she controls the environment (temperature, seating, lighting, prepared music, schedule, repertoire) and "systematically create[s] a climate of welcome, teamwork, and respect." Questions: which do you identify with (Jill or Dana); folk or pop; what would be comforting or uncomfortable in Dana's choir; how would you describe the differences? Author: Sixes are the only number divisible into two types; the distinction is how they respond to fear or anxiety, which is their sin or passion; their motivation is to feel safe and secure.

**The need to feel safe [pp.137-139].** Their passion is **fear**, which can escalate. Watch news from each number's perspective: for Sixes every news event has a threatening element, creating a need for a plan. Commercials play on anxiety and distrust, exaggerated for Sixes (author's humorous list of ad-induced worries). Fear by number: Two afraid people will not want her; Nines afraid of conflict; Eights afraid of being controlled; Sixes' fear itself is the preoccupation: "What if?" Phobic Sixes tend to give in to fear and are more malleable with aggressive, sure people. Counterphobic Sixes try to overcome fear, distrust people with too many answers who come on too strong, and want to find their own way. Many Sixes combine both by circumstance. A phobic Six: worried the night before pee-wee soccer; played well anyway ("wasted energy"). Counterphobic Sheryl: likes public speaking; fear is of "what-ifs" in novel situations; prepares thoroughly; some anxiety if on the spot. "Phobic Sixes give in to their anxiety and get lost in imagining all the ways the worst could happen, while counterphobic Sixes expect the worst and expend the same energy making a plan for it." Sixes appreciate order, plans, rules for security; relationships are messy, so at times a Six's need to feel safe "trumps relationships." Like Eights, they want to affect others without being affected. When anxiety is unmanageable they lean on the belief systems of their organizations; misplaced trust in leaders who they hope will make them less afraid; loyalty makes it hard to stop trusting the wrong person. Sixes benefit from trusting their own experience; they sell themselves short and place too much hope in others. Sixes can be slow to forgive, holding past slights.

**Planning for the worst [pp.140-141].** Sixes imagine the worst and plan. A Six friend as traveler: constant scanning for clues (terminals, Metro, banks), "Scanning is a habitual response to life. Sixes are always looking for hidden danger." Other Sixes travel to the same familiar place (same campground and cabin) to reduce variables. Vacation effects: relationship problems diminish when a One goes on vacation (more relaxed) but are exacerbated when a Six travels; protection is a core need in relation to those close; phobic Sixes may be overprotective away from routine; counterphobic Sixes have no patience with your fear unless you are willing to try to overcome it. Because they overthink, Sixes are prone to procrastination that looks like lack of commitment. Sixes ask many questions; others can feel threatened by too many, too personal, or too soon.

**Disconnection from productive thinking [p.142-].** Sixes resist the teaching that they are "thinking repressed" because they seem to think constantly; but much of the thinking does not lead to action or insight. A counterphobic executive: "We are really up in our heads thinking ... 'This does not make sense,' ..."
**6s chapter, continued: thinking, routine, pairings [pp.142-144].** The counterphobic executive (cont.): "This does not make sense" is fine "except when the not making sense involves feelings"; he moves on only when it feels safe and outcomes are anticipated; tired of overthinking, he sometimes acts impulsively "as a way of stopping thinking," hasty and ill-considered. Sixes embrace thinking more productively but often do not know where to start. Routine differs by number: Nines fall into routine without thinking about what might go wrong; Fives like routine to manage limited energy; Sixes find safety in routine; some numbers need freedom as Sixes need security: "Many relationship problems are rooted in this difference between needing stability and thriving on spontaneity." Problem is not gathering information but reacting too quickly; Sixes should stop, breathe, think clearly before falling into the anxiety trap; Sixes "make up things when they become anxious and then react to the story they have manufactured" and must practice waiting before responding.

**Stress and security [p.145].** In stress Sixes first pull back, then check thoughts/feelings with trusted people because they often do not trust themselves; they ask what you think is appropriate; offer your best but do not attach value to whether they follow it: they almost always have a plan, and questioning is gathering information to support or discredit decisions already made. **Chaining** (paper chains image): Sixes link fears, memories, past hurts and disappointments; if afraid you will leave, they chain the feeling to everything that ever made them fear abandonment; relationships can collapse under the weight; naming the pattern lets them stop. **Stress move to Three:** more sure of themselves; decide and act; less second-guessing. **Security move to Nine:** trust themselves and their experience; sense everything will be okay.

**Limitations in relationships [pp.145-147].** Sixes underestimate time commitments and fail to honor priorities: estimate and double it. Struggle to forgive and forget: they believe remembering hurts protects them; forgiveness makes them vulnerable and powerless; some keep journals or calendars marking painful disconnections for years. Story: a couple stuck because the wife, previously married to a neglectful man, worried twice a week that her husband would leave; author shared that Joe once held her hands and said: "I'm not leaving ... This work is on your side of the fence. You will either decide to trust that, or not." She decided to trust.

**The Path Together [pp.147-148].** Joe, pastor of congregations with many Sixes, held every meeting twice (when remodeling or buying/selling property) so Sixes could process what they heard, formulate questions and judge whether concerns have value; they hesitate to speak in real time because they do not trust themselves. Sixes want to be informed and have a voice. Many believe there are more Sixes than any other number; hard for a fast-paced society to accommodate their many questions; but given time they "see things that the rest of us miss"; "We can all benefit from slowing down and viewing the world through the eyes of a Six."

**Relationships for Sixes [pp.148-149]:**
- *You can:* learn to trust yourself by practicing; trust your inner ways of knowing; listen to your body ("Your head and your heart will lie to you but your body will not").
- *You can't:* expect others to be as loyal and faithful to shared groups as you are; be noticed without taking some risk (author thinks Sixes want to be noticed but are not always willing to take the risk); always feel safe, but you can recognize when fear is becoming self-indulgent.
- *Accept that:* some things will work out and if not you will have what you need; fear has played a role in your life and can be diminished by awareness and productive thinking.

**Relationships with Sixes [pp.149-150]:** Trust is a big thing; they create boundaries to keep out the untrustworthy and ask many questions; the right information reassures and builds trust.
- They appreciate genuine and authentic people, do not trust appearances, watch to see you are who you say.
- Worst-case planning is comforting; take it seriously. "Everything is going to be fine" feels patronizing, disrespectful, dismissive; do focus on the best possible outcome.
- Encourage them to trust themselves and take more (measured) risks; not "You should trust yourself more" but point to previous times they did trust their own ideas and carried them through unchecked.
- At home they need emotional attentiveness, conversation, a partner in everyday details.
- They like emotionally mature, honest, not-too-needy friends.
- They often keep anxiety to themselves.
- They require a lot of observable and verbal reassurance about your commitment.
- Encourage action when overthinking (they confuse thinking about something with doing something).
- Point out the good things they bring; be kind and thoughtful answering their questions.

## Chapter: 7s, "It's All Good" [pp.151-]

**Opening story [pp.151-152].** Darrin, a Seven, a preacher's son in a small town, had a late-night lakeside escapade with three boys from the congregation; he and his dad stopped speaking; his mother told him to apologize or give up his truck keys; Darrin: "I love Dad more than anybody ... so why do I have to say I'm sorry? It wasn't about him." Mom: "Consider this a practice run." Years later at 2:30 a.m. from college he phoned to ask his mother to teach him again about apologizing because his girlfriend Traci might break up with him. Questions: which person do you identify with; why did Darrin struggle to see how his actions affected others; motive for apologizing to Dad versus Traci; how does the Enneagram explain? Author: all Sevens are motivated by a need to be happy and avoid pain. Immature or average Sevens often dismiss emotions in themselves and others; see the world as a playground; happiest on the move; avoid anything unpleasant. This leaves them a "half range of emotion," which "can be a deal breaker in relationships"; ignoring others' agendas and feelings is something average Sevens are prone to; when others cannot be honest with Sevens about feelings and needs, delayed emotional responses emerge as anger, shame, fear, or resentment.

**The World of Sevens [pp.153-155].** Energetic, charming (may overvalue charm); avoid limitations (especially imposed by others) and direct confrontation; use humor and intentional distraction to slip away from trouble; when acting out of duty their lack of enthusiasm is palpable (hold energy for a more promising future event). Challenge: take full responsibility for their part in conflict without blaming others. Mihee Kim Kort (Seven, minister, married to a Six): he needs routine; she tries to be consistent but never does; picks up kids and heads to playground or library, stays out; feels guilty, but cannot be cooped up in a house of "screaming, chasing, negotiating." Every Seven has a big heart, generous and willing to sacrifice; more than any other number they feel "trapped, caught between their seemingly endless need for stimulation and the needs of others." "I want to do whatever it takes for you to be happy" without losing themselves. They often have "long lines of suitors who feel ditched" who took the relationship more seriously; their boundaries are easily misunderstood. Time is a limitation ("two more days in the week"); unfinished projects remind them time is running out. Sevens think and then do, without a nod to feelings; must be taught to consider feelings of others and consequences, and what to do with feelings.

**A passion for pleasure [pp.155-156].** The Seven's passion: **gluttony**: the constant desire for more (filling inner emptiness with positive, stimulating experiences). Shauna Niequist (Seven): "My appetites ... I feel them more strongly"; the best and worst parts of her are related; loves variety, "terrible at routine," wants everything to feel like a special occasion; wears people out with enthusiasm; people expect a certain level of energy from her; "bringing my whole self doesn't always mean bringing my most wound-up self." Sevens in frustration or need move outward for gratification; transformation involves turning inward, disconcerting for those who rely on their playful energy. "Other people can't be you."

**Reframing as self-protection [pp.156-159].** Where many fear abandonment, Sevens fear being trapped and engulfed, and find escape in **reframing**: pain and failure are reframed almost immediately. Son Joel (Seven; basketball bench-warmer) said "Did you catch us with that killer dunk?" Sevens quickly change sad feelings into something positive; with maturity they can hold sadness or fear before remaking the experience; until then they repress, and the feelings resurface unpredictably. Joel lost at Six Flags at five: closed his eyes and imagined rides and ice cream: "Honestly, I had a great time." Every Seven has a first-time story of refocusing from fear toward something wonderful and safe. Problem: other numbers may label reframing "immature, irresponsible, and unrealistic"; the relationship can look like parent and child; only solution is "intentionally choosing to meet in the middle."

**A smokescreen of activity [pp.159-160].** Sevens avoid pain of all kinds, especially being trapped in routine; unmanageable emotional or physical pain is almost unbearable; they do not know how to make a relationship work with someone who cannot or will not be happy. They are in the **Fear Triad** with Fives and Sixes and most fear being trapped with anything unpleasant; manage by keeping options open (their understanding of abundance); a stable measured life is a turn-off; about halfway through an event they think of what is next. Relationships require smaller commitments (on time, finish what you start, honor a plan despite a more exciting offer). Once a Seven commits (must be their idea) they are all in; define "all in" with others: for Ones and Eights it means hanging in from start to finish; for Twos it includes being emotionally in. Sevens like relationships and are devastated when they end; endings are not easy for them.

**The need for optimism [p.160].** Linked to the desire to believe the world is safe, people are good and needs will be met. Young Sevens ask how they can be next to Six. Richard Rohr: "Optimism and pessimism are surprisingly not far from one another: both are intellectual mechanisms for managing the abyss and dangers of life." A woman: we need healthy optimism and dreamers with bigger visions of community and love; "I still want to hold on to the possibility of hope and joy." We miss Sevens' good because we fail to take them seriously; "Their optimism is necessary."

**Stress and security [pp.160-162].** Best: "ambassadors for hope," lighthearted, generous, caring, creative. Worst: opinionated and stubborn; with Threes and Eights, remember that when absolutely sure they are right, they are probably wrong. Stress produces frenetic activity. **Stress move to One:** slow down; more discerning about what, with whom, when; less selfish; finish things; attend to details; balance between dreaming and doing; heals common complaints (no follow-through, no attention to detail). Sometimes enthusiasm is misinterpreted as a desire to impose will. **Security move to Five:** step back from too many interests and people; give time to one or two relationships or a few things; most satisfaction, but works only for a limited time; one Seven says unless influenced by the Five move she does not make room for quality time with significant others.
Author's note [p.162]: Awareness of differences helps limit what we expect of others; "expectations are resentments waiting to happen" (learned teaching in the recovery community).

**Limitations in relationships [p.162].** Sevens repress feeling to stay in the happy range; the other eight numbers manage life with a full range of emotion. Their charm works better professionally than in relationships: in interdependent relationships "they're not the CEO"; others need not agree or do what they want; moderate the ego at home and work. Sevens believe their needs are simple and few; in fact they are complex people with complex needs. They struggle with how things can be great one moment and problematic the next, dislike emotional messiness, and sometimes need help seeing and admitting a problem; they must deal with problems as they arise, setting aside the wish that problems fix themselves. Repairing a significant breach with loved ones requires "a tenacious maturity."

**The Path Together [p.163].** Shauna Niequist (Seven, who loves gathering people at home), on the spiritual side of the Enneagram: her first step was permission not to practice it as the Fours in her life do (her mother and husband are introverted, contemplative) and instead to offer her greatest gift, **hospitality**; a meaningful dinner party means "I believe in God and I believe He's good." Author's point: by refusing fulfillment by any path but their own, Sevens model the satisfaction possible when we acknowledge the inherent value of our uniqueness; it "needs to be honored and celebrated."

**Relationships for Sevens [pp.163-165]:** Sevens struggle more than any other number to accept a limit to what they can have, and that some things they cannot have however hard they try. Helpful: a contemplative practice (meditation, centering prayer); a mantra to stop frenetic thinking and planning: **"It is what it is."**
- *You can:* live into the reality that the growing edge is where there is pain that cannot be reframed or renamed (learn what it teaches); dream new dreams (they won't always come true); have a life of abundance and joy, though you won't recognize it without also experiencing scarcity and pain.
- *You can't:* manage life with a half range of emotions (you can develop the other half); be responsible and dependable while keeping options open (you can make dependability one of your options); get where you want with excessive behavior (you can moderate).
- *Accept that:* sometimes life is boring and cannot be reframed; relationships are as valuable for personal growth as for enjoyment; people may presume you do not go deep because you are interested in many things; your behavior is both alienating and charming; avoiding painful personal feelings does not work in a long-term relationship.

**Relationships with Sevens [pp.165-166]:** When Sevens work on themselves, people say "What happened to you? You used to be so much fun"; do not expect them to show up with behavior you asked them to moderate.
- Do not push them to commit to specific routines and schedules.
- They need the other person to have their own energy and interests; do not depend on them for constant companionship.
- When criticism is necessary, be gentle and brief.
- Share your feelings with a Seven but do not process your feelings with a Seven; do that with someone else.
- They love being with people and value time alone; help both happen.
- Do not talk to them about potential (any talk of potential is wrapped in expectation).
- Best gift: encourage them to allow and experience a full range of emotions.
- Most non-Sevens have forgotten how to play; invite a Seven to teach you.
- They need space to express ideas freely; fine if you go a different direction.
- When they want something within your purview they are very persistent ("like being pecked to death by chickens").
- Attend to their stories; telling stories is often how they express and share feelings.

## Conclusion [pp.167-168]

The author describes childhood service as librarian to her brother Carroll (polio) and Grandpa Brown (dying) and her lifelong wish to write a book that would heal people; she was a divorced mother of three when she married Joe, who adopted the children (a fourth followed). They have known the Enneagram most of their thirty years together; it is "just one of the many practices" in the search for who we can be when loved, accepted and safe. Hope: use the Enneagram to offer love, acceptance and assurance. The book is for anyone wanting to do better in relationships (home, work, church, friends). Its effect depends on three commitments: (1) protect relationships from anger, fear, and shame; (2) be compassionate when others struggle "to think productively, act intentionally, and feel deeply"; (3) take seriously what others say about how they experience us and use what the Enneagram teaches to act on it. "We will do what we can with what we know for the good of all."

## Gaps and cautions

Ones' 'passion' is not given a one-word name in the 1s chapter (anger turned into resentment; "fear of being bad" appears as a heading); the Sixes chapter names fear as passion; the Nines chapter has no "disconnection" heading.
- **Opening story of the Fours chapter:** the text does not state Daphne's number; Jane is called a Four in the commentary.
- **Pairing tables** in the chapters present each relationship from the viewpoint of the chapter's own number only; the Pair index above merely aligns them and adds no new claims. Some pairs have asymmetric coverage (for example, the Nines chapter's note on Fives and the Fives chapter's note on Nines emphasize different things).
- Foreword-type endorsements, the study-guide, and publisher material were not extracted. No text passages were garbled in the source range; image-only diagrams (the Enneagram figure on pp.11-12) are not present in the text, so the diagram itself was not extracted (the stress and security lines are taken from the chapter text).
