# CHAPTER 12 — TYPE ONE: THE REFORMER [s.22]

(Everything in this chapter is also in outline in the repository's existing Enneagram material for Type One; this is the book's own formulation.)

## The One in Profile [s.22]

- **Healthy:** conscientious, strong personal convictions, intense sense of right and wrong; wish to be rational, reasonable, self-disciplined, mature and moderate. Highly principled; fair, objective, ethical; truth and justice are primary values. Sense of responsibility, integrity and higher purpose often makes them "teachers and witnesses to the truth." **At their best:** extraordinarily wise and discerning; by accepting what is, they become "transcendentally realistic," knowing the best thing to do in all circumstances; humane, inspiring, hopeful.
- **Average:** dissatisfied with reality, they become high-minded idealists who feel it is up to them to improve everything: crusaders, advocates, critics, who embrace "causes" and point out how things "ought" to be. Afraid of making a mistake; orderly and well organized but impersonal, rigid, emotionally constricted, keeping feelings and impulses in check; often workaholics ("anal-compulsive," punctual, pedantic, fastidious). Highly critical of self and others: picky, judgmental, perfectionistic, opinionated, impatient, moralizing, scolding, abrasive, indignantly angry.
- **Unhealthy:** dogmatic, self-righteous, intolerant, inflexible; deal in absolutes (they alone know "the Truth"); severe judgments of others while rationalizing their own actions. Obsessed with imperfection and others' wrongdoing; hypocritically do the opposite of what they preach. Condemnatory, punitive, cruel to rid themselves of whatever disturbs them. Severe depression, nervous breakdowns, suicide attempts likely.
- **Key motivations:** want to be right, to have integrity and balance, to strive higher and improve others, to be consistent with their ideals, to justify themselves, to be beyond criticism so as not to be condemned by anyone.
- **Examples listed:** Pope John Paul II, Gandhi, Margaret Thatcher, Al Gore, Elie Wiesel, Barbara Jordan, Bill Moyers, Katharine Hepburn, Harrison Ford, Ralph Nader, Sandra Day O'Connor, William F. Buckley, Noam Chomsky, George Bernard Shaw, Joan of Arc, "Mr. Spock."

## An Overview of the One [s.22]

- **Opening image:** a seventeenth-century New England Puritan's account of his own "wretchedness" (quoted from Philip Greven, *The Protestant Temperament*) is offered as the common theme of evangelical self-consciousness before "regeneration." The authors read the Puritans' desire for self-regeneration by striving after ideals as an expression of Type One: not content to be as they are, Ones feel obliged to be better, to rise "beyond human nature, into the realm of the Absolute."
- To a One, the advice of "Desiderata" ("be gentle with yourself... the universe is unfolding as it should") sounds "foolish and dangerous"; average to unhealthy Ones think the universe is emphatically not unfolding as it should and that people are not trying hard enough.
- Ones are locked in conflicts they do not see: good versus evil, flesh versus spirit, ideal versus real, chaotic irrational nature versus clarity of conviction, libidinous impulses versus self-control, metaphysical aspirations versus human needs, head versus heart.

### In the Instinctive Triad (the One's place among the centers) [s.22]
- Ones are the type in the Instinctive Triad who "underexpress" instincts and drives. Like Eights they are people of action who respond at gut level; but Eights give free rein to instinct, Nines are "out of touch" with it, and Ones pull it in, limit it and direct it toward goals the superego deems worthy.
- **Anger** is a powerful motivator: when circumstances disappoint them, anger is "fuel" that launches action. Anger rightly understood is the instinctual response to a situation we are dissatisfied with, the energy that lets us say no. A Gandhi quotation is used: conserved anger can be transmuted into a power that can move the world.
- Ones are often unaware of their anger and underestimate it ("I'm not angry! I'm just trying to get this right."). They are rational in the sense of common sense, not exploratory intellect; they like practical ideas and, unlike Fives, will not stay interested in concepts that do not lead to constructive action.
- They appear very sure of themselves, but the confidence lies in the tightness of the ideals, not in themselves. They relate to the world by seeing themselves as "less than" an ideal (usually an intangible universal value such as truth or justice) and subordinate their instinctive drives to it. Unlike Nines (idealistic but detached from inner drive), Ones are determined to make the ideal real. The ideal can never be fully attained.
- **The deterioration trap:** as they decline, average Ones begin to identify with the ideal so completely that unhealthy Ones think they have attained it and everyone who has not should be condemned. They believe zealous striving makes them righteous and that aligning with the ideal puts them "in the right" no matter how they fail.

### Problems with Repression and Aggression [s.22]
- Like other Instinctive types, Ones repress part of the psyche: the irrational side, instinctual impulses and personal desires, which they try to sublimate into a quest for perfection.
- Ones relate dualistically: they see themselves as less than the ideal while appearing greater than an environment they must improve. They measure the distance to the ideal and also the distance from present perfection to past imperfection; they feel they and the world must be "making progress." Anything blocking progress meets anger and criticism.
- **A double dichotomy:** (1) external: the pressure of living up to an ideal versus the conviction that the One is perfectly right and knows better; (2) internal: the tightly controlled rational face versus repressed drives and feelings. Ones are often emotional and passionate about convictions without realizing it, and keenly aware of aggressive and sexual impulses.
- Average to unhealthy Ones feel caught between: perfection versus imperfection, virtue versus sin, actions versus conscience, order versus disorder, good versus evil, God versus the Devil.
- **Jungian correlation:** One corresponds to the extroverted thinking type. A long Jung quotation (from *Psychological Types*) describes the type that elevates an objective formula into a universal law ("oughts" and "musts" bulk large; may be a useful reformer, public prosecutor or purifier of conscience; the more rigid the formula, the more he becomes "a martinet, a quibbler, and a prig"). The authors say Jung is describing different points along the One's Levels: average Ones are reformers and public prosecutors, unhealthy Ones try to force others into their mold.
- Healthy Ones are the most objective, principled and wise of the types; unhealthy Ones apply their ideals relentlessly and become intolerant; "the flesh will have its day" as repression breaks down.

### Parental Orientations [s.22]
- Ones developed as they did because as children they were **disconnected from the "protective-figure"**: the early adult responsible for setting limits, giving guidelines and disciplining (traditionally the patriarchal role; often the father but may be a mother, grandparent or sibling). They felt they could not rely on the structure from the family of origin (rules arbitrary, unfair, too strict or too unstable), so they built their own code, more rigorous than what was expected, to avoid condemnation by being blameless.
- This produced a relentless **superego** whose message is "You are not acceptable as you are; you must be better, always better." In authoritarian or chaotic families the superego messages became severe and inflexible; emotions and impulses were repressed by an internalized punishing father. (The authors note Freudian anal traits of orderliness, parsimony and obstinacy appear in Ones, especially with a Nine-wing, but say the origins need not be reduced to toilet training.)
- Disconnection could come from an absent, abusive or unfair protective-figure; from stern moral or religious upbringing and fear of eternal punishment (fear of hell for ordinary childish impulsiveness); or from a peaceful childhood plus a felt sense of some higher ideal to strive for. Often Ones were uncomfortable being children or were not allowed to be, and became "little adults." They decided they must parent themselves, better than the protective-figure did.
- They did not rebel; they internalized control through guilt. They resent the burden of perfection and resent more the freedom (the "license") of others.

### Problems with Anger and Perfection [s.22]
- Ones vent their anger most unfairly at others when angry with themselves for not being perfect. The One is not an aggressive type as such: Ones comply with their ideals; the aggression is anger at self and others for not complying perfectly.
- Perfection is a burden human nature cannot bear; Ones struggle with the interdependence of flesh and spirit. They deny what is base and human in themselves and "feel guilty for being human"; they fear being condemned because "they are not angels."
- Healthy Ones, with objective orientation, stay in touch with human reality and are tolerant of self and others and aware that ideals may not apply equally everywhere. Unhealthy Ones become "a twisted caricature" of their virtues, punishing others' least faults while absolving themselves of greater sins; they are without mercy because they have lost contact with humanity ("If ideals do not serve human beings, what purpose do they serve?").

## Analyzing the Healthy One — Levels 1 to 3 [s.22]

| Level | Name | Core content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Wise Realist** | The full range of humanity surfaces; impulses prove less chaotic than feared; the parts banished by the superego are integrated; subjective side aligns with objective reality. Realistic and accepting even of themselves. Ideals are personally fulfilling, not stifling commands; they see no single set of rules fits every situation. By becoming fully human they come as close to perfection as humanly possible ("a little less than the angels"). Wisest of the types: discernment grounded in the real rather than the ideal; others seek them out for guidance. **True tolerance** is not permissiveness but respect for the differences of opinion arrived at by well-informed people of good will; they speak truth so others can hear it. They keep ultimate values in view and can see "the greater good" at a glance (and wait for reality to answer if they do not know). Faith that truth will prevail lets them accept the right of others to be wrong. "Transcendental realists": on some level, "the universe is unfolding as it should." |
| 2 | **The Reasonable Person** | Fear of being imbalanced, corrupted or "bad" produces the desire to be right and have total integrity; sense of self rests on being reasonable and conscientious. Soul of reason: sensible, prudent, exceptionally good judgment; unafraid to make value judgments and take responsibility. Judgment comes from a felt conviction (gut), not mere logic. They believe a balanced life is required for objectivity; they can stand aside and evaluate themselves, readily admit mistakes. Healthy conscience; remorse when they fail. Righteous without being self-righteous; righteousness not necessarily religious: aligned with Divine Law and natural order; the Taoist ideal of a person in whom Heaven, Earth and Humanity are balanced. They value reason, moderation, temperance, impartiality and do not feel their restraints limiting; civilization rests on delaying personal rewards for long-term goals. They do not think virtue exempts them from suffering: not "Why me?" but "Why not me?" Not pessimists, simply realistic. |
| 3 | **The Principled Teacher** | They want not only to be right but to do right; objective values in practice; unswayed by passions. Truth and justice enter: they hate injustice anywhere, "on fire for justice and righteousness"; would rather suffer injustice than commit it (can be mistaken for healthy Eights). Enormous integrity: lying or cheating is virtually unthinkable; vote consciences not pocketbooks; as parents decide for the whole community's benefit; as religious persons act on principle even against civil authority; risk possessions, reputation, even life. Strong sense of mission. Responsible and reliable (resemble healthy Sixes, but look to an inner moral compass rather than outside reassurance); focused and driven (like healthy Threes); self-disciplined. Act as moral teachers and "witnesses to the truth." The hallmark: the conscience speaks primarily to them; they teach by example, not preaching; they trust that the truth will ultimately be heard. |

## Analyzing the Average One — Levels 4 to 6 [s.22]

| Level | Name | Core content |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | **The Idealistic Reformer** | Guided increasingly by the superego, subject to guilt and anxiety when disobeying; fear that others are indifferent ("not making a dent") drives them to higher standards. They become idealists, reformers, crusaders, with a cause. Difference from healthy Ones: they become convinced they alone have the answer; moral superiority ("I know the way things ought to be, so you should listen to me"); noblesse oblige. Their personal ideal becomes everyone's norm; weight of "shoulds" and "oughts." Distrust of others ("If I don't do it, who will?"). Olympian view: lawgivers and legislators of humanity; nothing too small to judge (smoking, drinking, seat belts, television, pornography, rock music). Frequently "point out" things; may well be right but do not trust people to find out for themselves. Progress is an important concept; always purposeful; may feel they should watch television only for education; associate with and lead high-minded causes (migrant workers, neighborhood political organizing, environmental concerns, school levies). They know where they stand on every issue, argue with the zeal of a missionary, are articulate debaters, with enormous self-confidence; treat the world "like sculptors eager to get their hands on a shapeless mass of clay," which is where difficulties begin. |
| 5 | **The Orderly Person** | They have made public stands, so want no inconsistency between private feelings and official idealistic positions; want control of every area, especially impulses and emotions. Healthy self-discipline deteriorates into brisk efficiency and orderliness. Black-or-white thinking; subjective preference is "mere self-indulgence." Impersonal discipline and order are the way they control self, others and environment. Meticulous, sticklers for detail, plan every contingency ("everything will be under control" is a favorite phrase); flow charts are a symbol of their approach; lists, schedules, always on time and insist others are too. Personify the Protestant work ethic; few vacations. Highly orderly thinking: methodical, precise, logical distinctions, troubled by ambiguity; mentally rank everything ("schoolteachers on holiday who cannot stop grading"); insist on logical causes so that reward and blame can be assigned. "The referees, accountants, and critics of reality" (Freud's anal type). Tidy, clean, neat in high-priority areas. Orderliness can have positive effects for organizations and society. Spontaneity is stiff and forced; interpersonally "proper in a slightly stuffy, 'correct' way," relying on etiquette. They take sides against their own impulses (give up what they want, force themselves to do what they do not want), so are controlled more than ever by their impulses through resistance. Ascetic, austere, "antiseptic" quality, especially around pleasure; sexual impulses may be particularly threatening; musculature tight (pursed lips, clenched teeth, stiff neck and face). They see themselves as protecting the world from their passions. **Turning point:** life and they are not as orderly as they want; impulses keep breaking through; from here they control more tightly and become obsessed with rooting out disorder everywhere. |
| 6 | **The Judgmental Perfectionist** | The inner voice has become shrill and critical; orderliness is not enough, perfection is required. Often harder on themselves than others; "nothing is ever good enough" echoes the protective-figure. They become "judges" to overcompensate for fear of being judged. The one emotion regularly allowed is anger in its forms (impatience, irritation, resentment, indignation), though often unaware of its degree. Highly critical, didactic, pontificating, lecturing; "I told you so"; lose tempers over trivialities; stern disciplinarians; see others as lazy and irresponsible; present opinions as the Truth; almost never change opinions because they rest on fixed ideals. Life is a never-ending application of the ideal to particulars, constant fixing of mistakes. Indignant and resentful, taking others' errors (littering, tax evasion, affairs) as personal affronts; abrasive manner invites defiance; "ascerbic dogmatism." Workaholics who feel guilty if not productive; perfectionism with minutiae reduces efficiency (polishing furniture until the finish comes off); specious improvements "to justify their existence"; hard to work for, thin-skinned, do not delegate. Nothing is enjoyed: they do not enjoy working or not working. They pronounce on things they know little about and condescend (like a priest instructing couples about marriage). The superego cannot be placated: standards keep rising; criticism of others displaces some of the self-condemnation. They also seek secret relief in what the superego most condemns (drink, nights out, spending, sex, pornography), hidden from others. They resent that the burden of perfection fell on them more than on others ("Since they are not having much fun, why should anyone else?"). |

## Analyzing the Unhealthy One — Levels 7 to 9 [s.22]

| Level | Name | Core content |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | **The Intolerant Misanthrope** | Cannot be proved wrong; ideals are rigid dogmas and absolutes (right or wrong, good or evil, saved or damned; no gray area, slightest imperfection ruins the whole). Their living by absolutes negates their own humanity; they "love humanity but hate individuals." Difference from Level 6: Level 6 Ones sometimes include themselves in the criticism and feel guilt; unhealthy Ones exclude themselves ("I am right, therefore everything I say and do is right"). The superego is so toxic they must displace its vitriol onto others to survive psychologically; focus on others' wrongdoing as escape from the internalized protective-figure's wrath. Anger is their prominent and perhaps only emotion, with an unadmitted element of vindictiveness. Intolerant of any who disagree, who are seen as immoral and evil; religion, justice, truth are invoked to make others feel wrong. Sophistry: bombing a village to save it, selling people into slavery to convert them, killing adults to protect fetuses. Tightness acts as a lightning rod for repressed feelings and desires erupting unexpectedly; prolonged severe depression alternates with angry drive; alcohol and drug abuse and collapse of home and professional maintenance are not uncommon. |
| 8 | **The Obsessive Hypocrite** | Neurotically preoccupied with the focus of their fury, which they cannot act on directly, so they act compulsively, controlled by irrational impulses. The double dichotomy becomes visible: a split between impulses and the force needed to repress them, and between the need to control and moments when control breaks down. Obsessions and compulsions are both attempts at control and symptoms that it is crumbling. Obsessive thoughts may be obscene, sacrilegious or brutally violent; they may feel possessed by demons (their demons are their repressed feelings). They cannot resolve them because they cannot admit their bitter resentment and hatred of others. Obsessions may shift to cleanliness, "dirt," food (anorexia, bulimia, compulsive "cleansings" with fasts and enemas), compulsive cleaning or counting. They act out enough of the obsessions to be compulsive, arbitrary, contradictory and hypocritical: preaching purity while in the grip of compulsive sexual activity; "a censor who is 'forced' to watch pornography, or a sex researcher who must hear the lurid stories of rapists, or a judge who is a shoplifter"; they may put themselves in the way of temptation to prove their strength. Scandal and ruined reputations follow; the deformity of their emotional life, not the original impulses, is what makes them dangerous. |
| 9 | **The Punitive Avenger** | Not motivated by ideals any more but by the need to restore self-control; they "solve" neurotic conflicts by doing away with the apparent cause, whipping themselves into a fury over the evildoing of others, though their own sanity is at stake. They cannot back down; others must be proved wrong and punished, and being "hideously evil" can be condemned without guilt. No love, mercy or sympathy; "They are only getting what they deserve!"; the end justifies the means. They set injustices and atrocities in motion while presenting them as the work of an impersonal agent; capable of having others imprisoned, tortured or burned at the stake. "The personality type which fears being condemned, condemns others mercilessly." |

## The Dynamics of the One [s.22]

(Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material as the 1 to 4 and 1 to 7 lines; this is the book's own account.)

### Direction of disintegration: the One goes to Four [s.22]
- Beginning at Level 4 under stress, Ones act out traits of average-to-unhealthy Fours; average Ones are the most strict and self-controlled, so the move to Four signals a hidden wish to be free of burdens and let repressed desires "play," followed by guilt and even greater strictness.
- **Level 4 One to Four:** fantasize about being someone else; seek beautiful things and an aesthetically pleasing refuge ("aesthetic elitism"); romantic longings for unavailable others that are not told and are soon repressed.
- **Level 5:** moody, temperamental, melancholy ("no one understands how hard they are trying"); discipline gives way to turbulent feelings, emotional reactivity, self-doubt; forced, affected aestheticism; Four's drama and hypersensitivity with the One's propriety make them awkward and self-conscious socially.
- **Level 6:** self-indulgence: exceptions, perks and pleasures; the sobriety preacher drinks when no one is looking; the one shocked by promiscuity seeks anonymous sex or an affair; the superego is so extreme they unconsciously seek extreme pleasures.
- **Level 7:** when rigid intolerance and rage cannot be sustained they collapse into depression, severe and long-term (Ones from dysfunctional families may mistake themselves for Fours); the depression contains rage and signals something is wrong with their views.
- **Level 8:** the One's obsessions are joined by the unhealthy Four's compulsions: irrational self-hatred, morbidity, turbulent hatred and sexual desire; they turn on themselves "with equal fury" and are plunged into the unconscious, which fills them with horror, disgust and self-loathing; panic that their ideals no longer help.
- **Level 9:** last moorings break; they see their own corruption ("Oh my God, what have I done?"), fear they cannot be forgiven, "Their convictions now convict them"; they condemn themselves as harshly as they condemned others; profound depression, extreme guilt, self-hatred; incapacitating breakdown or suicide becomes real.

### Direction of integration: the One goes to Seven [s.22]
- Integrating Ones relax and take delight in life; they trust themselves and reality; become life-affirming rather than constricted; happiness is a legitimate response to existence; pleasure can be taken without sinking into sensuality.
- They no longer need to make everything perfect; they move "from obligation to enthusiasm, from constraint to freedom of action"; more relaxed, productive, spontaneous, playful, happier. "Things do not have to be perfect to be good." They marvel at nature, the arts and others' accomplishments. They find they can be flexible without compromising real values ("The good is not the enemy of the better"). "Integrating Ones have come down from Olympus to join the human race."

## Some Final Thoughts on the One [s.22]
- The neurotic One has brought about what he most fears: condemnation by others and by his own conscience; justice now works against him.
- Many of the propositions they preached as objective truths were partly personal predilections; they should recognize the role of the subjective and irrational. "Reason alone is a trap which leads to unreasonable behavior."
- **The fundamental contradiction:** Ones want integrity (congruence of thought, word and deed), but integrity means oneness, wholeness; once they judge a part of themselves unacceptable and repress it, they have lost integrity. The way out is not more self-judgment but **self-acceptance**: "here and now, who they are is good enough."
- Underlying fear: they must hold to the strictest ideals or fall into depravity (life as walking a tightrope over a chasm). When they see that no foolproof rules exist, they may find beyond the superego "a deeper, quiet wisdom," a living relationship to the truth of each moment; then they no longer need to convince others, who become eager to learn.

## The Directions of Integration and Disintegration [s.24]
- **Sequences:** Integration 1-7-5-8-2-4-1 and 9-3-6-9. Disintegration 1-4-2-8-5-7-1 and 9-6-3-9.
- **Wings also move.** Each type's wing has its own integration and disintegration directions following the same pattern (example: a Nine with a One-wing: the One-wing integrates to Seven and disintegrates to Four).
- **Apparent contradiction (four subtypes):** One with a Two-wing, Two with a One-wing, Seven with an Eight-wing, Eight with a Seven-wing seem to have the same type as both integration and disintegration direction (a One with a Two-wing: Four is the One's disintegration and the Two-wing's integration). Resolution: Four stands for unhealthy tendencies in the One but healthy ones in the Two-wing; if healthy, unhealthy tendencies are inactive, so the healthy One integrates to Seven while only the Two-wing integrates to Four. (If unhealthy: One to Four, Two-wing to Eight.)
- The authors say it is not feasible to describe wing development/deterioration for every type; once you see how the basic type moves, you can recognize the wing's movement.
- **The disintegration type is not "unhealthy" in an absolute sense**: no type is fundamentally unhealthy. It is "relatively" unhealthy because it embodies what we most need for development but, because of our own structure, cannot yet integrate. It represents "those aspects of ourselves which are the most difficult for us to come to terms with."
- **What each type most needs to deal with (the issue its disintegration type symbolizes):**

| Type | Needs to deal with | Symbolized by |
|---|---|---|
| One | their feelings and unconscious impulses | Four |
| Two | (their aggressions; the move to Eight is the eruption of dangerously aggressive impulses) | Eight |
| Three | dissociating from their feelings | Nine |
| Four | feelings of entitlement | Two |
| Five | impulsiveness | Seven |
| Six | hostility toward those whom they fear | Three |
| Seven | obsessiveness | One |
| Eight | denied fear of being overwhelmed by others | Five |
| Nine | denied anxiety (hysteria) | Six |

- **Why we are tempted:** the disintegration type seems to offer a quick solution to emotional needs but never does; at best it buys time, at worst makes things worse (example: an unhealthy Two lashing out as an Eight drives away the very people whose love is wanted).
- **Primary vs. secondary types:** Threes, Sixes and Nines need only two steps in integration to return to their basic type; the other six need six steps. Primary types (Three of Feeling, Six of Thinking, Nine of Instinctive) are most seriously affected by their Triad's characteristic problem; secondary types (One, Four, Two, Eight, Five, Seven) less so. Primary types integrate "revolutionarily" (abrupt change) but with more difficulty, since each contains the issues of both other types of its Triad (example: a Six is anxious about both the external world, as Five, and inner turmoil, as Seven); secondary types integrate "evolutionarily" (gradual). No absolute advantage either way: all nine ultimately integrate the issues of each Triad, in a different sequence and "rhythm."
- Integration "is never ending," an open-ended upward spiral; perfection and wholeness are ideals that beckon, not states attained. **Deterioration is self-limiting:** no one goes through all stages of disintegration, because psychosis or death (schizophrenics burn out, depressives may suicide, hysterics have accidents) would intervene; healthy development has no such limit.

## The Wing [s.24]
- Everyone is a mixture of the basic type and an adjacent wing. Usually only one of the two adjacent types is the wing (a Five has a Six-wing or Four-wing, seldom both); where both seem present they are relatively weaker and the basic type stronger. Find the wing by elimination (the better fit).
- The wing is always adjacent; a Nine with a Four-wing or a Seven with a Two-wing cannot exist: such mixtures "do not occur"; traits can conflict but not be mutually exclusive (like "being an honest man and a thief at the same time").
- **Proportion:** the wing's share varies (crude figures: 51/49 versus 85/15). Terms: a "heavy" wing (high proportion), "moderate" wing, "light" wing (negligible); the basic type is always at least 51 percent. For near 50/50 cases, determine the basic type by watching behavior under stress (which direction of disintegration they act out; example: a person unsure whether she is a Four with a Five-wing or a Five with a Four-wing watches whether she behaves like an average Two, indicating Four, or like an average Seven, indicating Five).
- Counts: 18 subtypes; with heavy/moderate/light, 54 subtypes; with precise percentages, hundreds.
- Ideally there would be eighteen description chapters ("there is no such thing as ... a pure One"); since proportions vary, readers must personalize the descriptions.

## The Levels of Development [s.24]
- The Levels are the overall structure of each type (healthy, average, unhealthy), described by the authors as one of their major discoveries and "not part of traditional interpretations." They account for people's fluidity "from day to day and even from moment to moment."
- **Uses:** (1) they let you distinguish further subtypes within a type (a contentious Level 6 Five versus a perceptive Level 2 Five; a domineering Level 8 Two versus a nurturing Level 3 Two). Other typologies often describe one or two Levels as if they were a whole type: Karen Horney's "resigned type" and "well-adjusted automaton" are, in this view, Nine at Levels 5 and 6 ("the passively disengaged person" and "the resigned fatalist"); "depressive personality disorder" is Four at Levels 7 and 8 ("The Alienated Depressive" and "The Emotionally Tormented Person"). (2) They help describe people accurately: traits and defense mechanisms emerge and combine at each Level; each type is a dynamic whole, not a static group of traits; traits "metamorphose into each other" (Four: self-awareness at Level 2 becomes self-absorption at Level 5 and self-hatred at Level 8; sensitivity becomes vulnerability becomes emotional torment).
- **What happens at each Level (the book's brief summary):**

| Level | Summary |
|---|---|
| 1 | Type at its healthiest: psychological balance, freedom, emergence of special spiritual capacities or virtues; the ideal state reflecting the essential qualities of the type. |
| 2 | Still healthy, but ego and defenses begin to emerge in response to basic childhood anxieties; deepest fears and desires emerge from relationships with parents; sense of self and cognitive style manifest. |
| 3 | Still healthy, less so; ego more active, producing a characteristic persona; the healthy social characteristics the type brings to others and society. |
| 4 | Subtly unbalanced by drawing on its characteristic source of psychic energy (different for each type); the type's unwitting psychological dead end appears; increasing intrapsychic and interpersonal conflict. |
| 5 | The ego inflates as the type tries to control the environment in characteristic ways; defense mechanisms more serious; marked turning point; conflicts with others increase. |
| 6 | Overcompensation for conflicts and increasing anxiety; characteristic forms of self-centeredness; conflicts escalate as ego-defenses are acted on. |
| 7 | Highly dysfunctional, self-defeating survival tactics, different for each type, to bolster an ego assailed by anxiety; serious interpersonal conflicts. |
| 8 | Serious intrapsychic conflict and delusional defenses; attempts to remake reality rather than succumb to anxiety; deeply neurotic, out of touch with reality in a way specific to the type. |
| 9 | Fully pathological: out of touch with reality, willing to destroy self and/or others to save illusions and spare the self the anxiety of realizing what has been done; immediate or remote self-destruction; serious violence, breakdown or death. |

- **Example of flow, Type Eight:** magnanimous heart, self-confident person, constructive leader, enterprising adventurer, dominating power broker, confrontational adversary, ruthless outlaw, omnipotent megalomaniac, violent destroyer. (Self-confidence deteriorates into drive for power and then delusional megalomania.) The dissolution of each type "has a certain inevitability," like a morality play.
- **Counting subtypes:** 18 subtypes x 3 wing proportions = 54; x 9 Levels = 486 "subtypes," differences "probably too subtle to describe precisely." Closing claim: the Enneagram is a unified whole and human beings are "infinitely variable." Cross-reference: the book points to its appendix and to a later book (*UTE*) for fuller Level descriptions.

## The Instinctual Types [s.24]
- Ichazo's other form of "subtype": Self-Preservation, Social and Sexual Instincts. The authors call them valuable but not technically subtypes: a subtype depends on its type (wings are a dependent variable), whereas the instincts are an **independent variable**; one could sort a room into three instinctual groups without knowing Enneagram types. Hence the term **"Instinctual variants."** Combined with wings: six "subtypes" per type, fifty-four total (the book's own count).
- The state of research: other authors have used Ichazo's twenty-seven phrases (nine types x three instincts); some are more accurate than others; more research needed; the authors find them probably valid but not yet crystallized. Instinctual types tell in which of life's domains the personality exerts itself most powerfully. The more neurotic (lower on the Levels), the more the personality interferes with the natural expression of the instinct, so lower Levels produce the opposite of the instinct's normal function: Self-Preservation types become destructive of their health and security; Social types become antisocial; Sexual types have problems expressing sexuality (promiscuity or dysfunction). Everyone has some of all three, one is more developed; whether it can change over a life remains undetermined.
- **Self-Preservation:** survival needs in contemporary form: money, food, housing, health, physical safety, comfort; quick to notice poor lighting, uncomfortable chairs, room temperature; issues with food and drink (excess or strict diets). At healthy to average Levels the most practical (bills, home and workplace upkeep, useful skills). Unhealthy: eat and sleep poorly, obsess over health, mishandle money, self-destructive acting out. Focus: "personal security and physical comfort."
- **Social:** interactions with others and the sense of value or esteem from shared activities (work, family, hobbies, clubs). The instinct was important to human survival; includes understanding of "place" within a hierarchy; desire for attention, recognition, honor, success, fame, leadership, appreciation, the safety of belonging. Like to know what is going on and to contribute; enjoy interacting but avoid intimacy. Unhealthy: profoundly antisocial, resent society, or poor social skills. Focus: interacting in ways that build personal value, accomplishment, security of "place."
- **Sexual:** many identify with this one by confusing it with being "sexy"; the instinct can be distorted and cause the most problems. Desire for intensity of experience (not only sexual; a great conversation or exciting movie). The difference from Social is not one-on-one versus group (almost all prefer one-on-one) but the intensity of contact and desire for intimacy: the "intimacy junkies," often neglecting obligations and "maintenance" when captivated; wide-ranging exploratory approach but lack of focus on own priorities. Neurotic forms: wandering lack of focus, promiscuity, or the opposite, fearful dysfunctional attitude to sex and intimacy; intense even about avoidances. Focus: intense intimate interactions giving a powerful sense of "aliveness."
- (Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material under "3 Instinctual Variants"; the book's own formulation is above.)

# CHAPTER 14 — THE THEORY OF THE ENNEAGRAM [s.25]

Epigraph: Karl Menninger on classification ("Classifications must never be taken too seriously... but the fear to use them has prevented much more thinking").

## Why a typology, and what it must be [s.25]
- Typologies are old: Galen is credited with popularizing Hippocrates' four humors/temperaments (melancholic, choleric, phlegmatic, sanguine; black bile, yellow bile, phlegm, blood), used for about fifteen hundred years until the Enlightenment discredited it, though it survives in language because it conveys a useful insight.
- A good typology's requirements as the authors state them: categories that are discrete, meaningful, useful and comprehensive; traits should not overlap between types yet similarities must be accounted for; meaningful to laypeople and specialists; ideally scientifically verifiable; able to cover healthy, average, neurotic and psychotic states.
- The claim: the Enneagram "rings true"; its categories seem "discovered rather than invented," and it can act as a framework for other typologies, in many cases completing them. The chapter first compares the Enneagram with other typologies, then examines "why the Enneagram works."

## The Enneagram and Other Typologies [s.25]

### Karen Horney and the Enneagram
- Horney's three neurotic "solutions": "moving away from people" (withdrawn), "moving against people" (aggressive), "moving toward people" (compliant). The authors keep the emphasis but widen it: types respond to the whole environment (inner and outer), aggressive types may assert against nature or their own fears, withdrawn types may withdraw from activities as well as people, and **compliant types are compliant to the dictates of their superego** (whose origin is other people, mainly parents), not necessarily to people.
- **Each Triad is a "mixed Triad" holding one aggressive, one compliant and one withdrawn type:**

| Triad | Compliant | Aggressive | Withdrawn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling | Two (to the superego dictate to be always selfless and loving) | Three (in pursuit of goals, in competition) | Four (to protect feelings and fragile self-image) |
| Thinking | Six (to the dictate to do what is expected) | Seven (engaging the environment, satisfying appetites) | Five (away from action into thought) |
| Instinctive | One (to the ideals striven for) | Eight (asserting against others and environment) | Nine (so others will not disturb inner peace) |

- Grouped across Triads: **Compliant = One, Two, Six; Aggressive = Three, Seven, Eight; Withdrawn = Four, Five, Nine.** The authors say this reveals new "triadic" relationships beyond the Triads. (Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material as the Hornevian Groups.)
- Horney's partial sub-types: under the "expansive solution" she described "narcissistic," "perfectionistic" and "arrogant-vindictive" types (the book maps them to Three, One and Eight; a footnote disputes listing the perfectionistic type, i.e., One, as aggressive, because its motivation is compliance to its superego, "perhaps the most superego-driven type in the Enneagram"). She did not work out subtypes of the "self-effacing solution" (elements of Two, Six and Nine, though the authors argue Nine is more accurately withdrawn: Nines "absent themselves" through dissociation without physically withdrawing). Under the "resigned solution" she described the "persistently resigned" (Nine), the "rebellious" (Five), and "shallow living" (Four, subdivided: emphasis on fun = Seven; prestige/opportunistic success = Three; "well-adapted automaton" = average to unhealthy Nine). The authors think she was independently approaching a three-times-three typology, but was inconsistent about the number of categories and made separate categories for one type at different Levels of Development.

### Freud and the Enneagram
- Freud's three psychosexual fixation stages (oral, anal, phallic) and the inconsistent subtype terms (oral-dependent, oral-sadistic, anal-expulsive, anal-retentive...) are tidied by the authors into two dialectics of three: the three **dispositions of energy** (receptive, retentive, expulsive) crossed with the three **libidinal points** (oral, anal, phallic), yielding nine "neo-Freudian" types:

| | Receptive | Retentive | Expulsive |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oral** | Nine | Four | Five |
| **Anal** | Six | One | Two |
| **Phallic** | Three | Seven | Eight |

- The authors advise reading these terms as metaphors for overall style, not literal fixation of libido (avoiding, for example, "phallic" for women). Examples: the phallic-receptive type matches Ernest Jones's "urethral character" and Type Three (narcissistic, invites admiration); the classic anal-retentive type (frugality, obstinacy, orderliness) fits aspects of One (more accurately One with a Nine-wing), with frugality in the Six (caution, insecurity) and stubbornness in both the Six (negativistic, passive-aggressive) and the Nine (refusing to deal with reality); the oral-receptive is the Nine, optimistic that the environment will supply needs "like milk flowing from its mother's breast"; "receptive" is preferred to "dependent" because Nines can live through others.
- The three oral types (4, 5, 9) are the withdrawn types; the anal (1, 2, 6) the compliant; the phallic (3, 7, 8) the aggressive.
- **Freudian structural concepts:** types whose principal imbalance is in the **ego** are the aggressive types (3, 7, 8); in the **id**, the withdrawn types (4, 5, 9); in the **superego**, the compliant types (1, 2, 6). Details: Fours, Fives, Nines are withdrawn from direct expression of id impulses and assertion, compensating by dissociating from reality (Fours through imagination, Fives through thought, Nines through idealization of or identification with the other), and all three have very permeable ego boundaries open to id-related material. Ones, Twos, Sixes are compliant to an internalization in the superego: Ones to idealistic obligations ("the inner judge"), Twos to the demand to always be loving ("the inner martyr"), Sixes to internalized authority figures ("the inner committee"). Threes, Sevens, Eights respond to threat by expanding the ego: Threes (competitive, asserting a self-image against others), Sevens (acquisitive), Eights (self-assertive, making the environment reflect their egos).

### Jung and the Enneagram
- Jung's two attitudes (extroversion, introversion) times four functions (thinking, feeling, intuition, sensation) give eight types. The correlation (Table 14.2):

| Enneagram | Jungian type |
|---|---|
| One | extroverted thinking |
| Two | extroverted feeling |
| Three | no Jungian type |
| Four | introverted intuitive |
| Five | introverted thinking |
| Six | introverted feeling |
| Seven | extroverted sensation |
| Eight | extroverted intuitive |
| Nine | introverted sensation |

- The authors suggest Jung described parts of Three inside several of his types without a category of its own (it would have broken his two-times-four symmetry), and that the Three being "so malleable and changeable" and "the most adaptable" explains why it lacks a Jungian type. They say Jung describes mostly average persons of each type, shifts intuitively into the direction of disintegration at the end of each description when he mentions neurotic and psychotic developments, and sometimes confuses types (e.g., elements of Nine described as introverted feeling, i.e., Six; confusions between extroverted and introverted thinking, i.e., One and Five). (Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material in part.)

### Psychiatric designations (DSM-IV) and the Enneagram
- Complaint: the DSM-IV merged traits of different types; example, its "histrionic" disorder combines Type Two (theatrical emotion; neurotic Twos produce hysterical conversion reactions, anxiety translated into physical symptoms) and Type Seven (flamboyant, impulsive acting out; hysterical via panic reactions to anxiety attacks).
- **Table 14.3 (the book says "the roughest of approximations"):** One: compulsive and depressive; Two: partly histrionic; Three: narcissistic; Four: aspects of avoidant, depressive, narcissistic; Five: aspects of schizoid, avoidant, schizotypal; Six: aspects of paranoid, dependent, passive-aggressive; Seven: aspects of histrionic and manic-depressive; Eight: antisocial; Nine: aspects of dependent, schizotypal, passive-aggressive. Borderline personality disorder "corresponds to aspects of many of the types and seems to be less type specific." (Footnote: "passive-aggressive" is a former DSM-III category.)

## Analyzing the Enneagram: why it works [s.25]
- **Conclusion first:** there is no single theoretical explanation; the Enneagram cannot be reduced to Freudian, Jungian or Horneyan concepts; it accommodates each, which suggests it is "a universal psychological symbol." It is "itself: a comprehensive, dynamic symbol of the human psyche based on a number of philosophical and psychological antecedents that have come together in a powerful new synthesis." Three approaches follow: dialectical, developmental, dynamic Freudian.

### A dialectical approach
- Nine types because the structure is a **three-times-three** arrangement, two dialectically related groups of three. In each Triad one type has **overdeveloped**, one **underdeveloped**, and one is **most out of touch with** the Triad's faculty:

| Triad | Overexpresses | Underexpresses | Most out of touch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling | Two (only positive emotions, repressing negative) | Four (reveals self through art or aesthetic living) | Three (projects an image instead) |
| Thinking | Five (substitutes thinking for doing) | Seven (does not complete or follow through an idea; thinking driven by what they want to do) | Six (does not trust own thinking; wants an authoritative framework or "second opinion") |
| Instinctive | Eight (unrestrained instincts leading quickly to action) | One (sublimates drives to an ideal) | Nine (fears the intensity of drives would destroy calm) |

- The dialectic pattern (thesis, antithesis, synthesis) recurs: Horney's three solutions, Freud's ego/id/superego, oral/anal/phallic. "If there is a single reason why the Enneagram works, this is it."

### A developmental approach
- Nine types because there are nine fundamental orientations a child can take toward parents. Everyone has a primary orientation to the **nurturing-figure** (usually mother or substitute), the **protective-figure** (usually father or substitute), or **both equally**; crossed with the quality of the orientation, **connected, disconnected, or ambivalent**. Both sets of three are dialectics. (Table 14.4, per the text's own examples and appendix):

| | Nurturing-figure | Protective-figure | Both figures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connected | Three | Six | Nine |
| Ambivalent | Eight | Two | Five |
| Disconnected | Seven | One | Four |

(note: reconstructed from the text's examples—connected nurturing = Three, ambivalent nurturing = Eight, disconnected nurturing = Seven—and from the Appendix's "Parental Orientation" line for each type; the table itself is not shown in the text.)

- Reading the table by column: **Three-Eight-Seven (nurturing-figure-oriented)** defend aggressively (Horney's "moving against"); early bonding and mirroring shapes self-image, so these are the **ego-oriented** types, asserting the ego into the environment: Threes want attention, Sevens satisfaction and stimulation, Eights control. **Six-Two-One (protective-figure-oriented)** defend by compliance ("moving toward"); the "should-and-must" types, a mixture of aggressive and compliant tendencies who tend to explode destructively under pressure; internalized superego prohibitions from the protective-figure; they feel obligated and often act as authority figures. **Nine-Five-Four (both figures)** defend by withdrawing ("moving away"); "the loners, intellectuals, and dreamers"; overwhelmed by inner or outer forces, dissociate from reality in different ways and build elaborate "inner realities"; in Freudian terms, problems expressing and sustaining immediate id impulses, especially self-assertion and aggression.
- Person, family and culture are interdependent: personality types influence cultural change, which influences how parents raise children, continuing the cycle.

### A dynamic Freudian approach
- Freud tried to type people by which of id/ego/superego dominates ("erotic," "narcissistic," "compulsive") but Otto Fenichel (quoted) objects that psychoanalysis is dynamic, so a dynamic typology would be about the interrelationships of id, ego and superego, not one dominant agency.
- The Enneagram answers this: each type is a dynamic interrelationship of all three. Each **Triad** has a core problem: the Feeling Triad's from the **ego**, the Thinking Triad's from the **superego**, the Instinctive Triad's from the **id**. In addition, each type is dominated by one function that conflicts with the Triad's core problem.
- **Example Two:** dominated by superego (self-esteem conditional on being loved for good works), ego also important, with a potential conflict; needs to bring the id into balance (go to Four, where the id sits); in disintegration goes to Eight, adding ego expansion to an already inflated self-image and getting further out of touch with dangerous id aggression.
- **Example Five:** id in conflict with superego, needs ego, so integrates to Eight (confident ego; Fives disengage from inner monologue and connect with others); disintegrates to Seven (ego again, aggressive and impulsive until it "virtually flies to pieces"). The ego is what the Five most needs to develop (least developed interpersonal skills, weak ego structure).
- **Primary types again:** Three's special problem is the ego (out of touch with feelings, takes cues from others, preoccupied with image), Six's the superego (internalizes many authority figures; confused thinking, indecision, relies on outside guidance), Nine's the id (out of touch with drives and assertion; passive, resigned, ineffectual). The Three must integrate first to Six (superego) and then Nine (id); a direct move to Nine would be too threatening because of the emotions Threes suppress, so Threes first loosen the superego's prohibitions against feeling and the drive for recognition.
- Why the Enneagram is universal: all human beings have id-, ego- and superego-related problems, "whether we call them by their Freudian names or by the Enneagram names of feeling, thinking, and instinct."

### Closing of the chapter
- The Enneagram encompasses many viewpoints because it operates at several levels of abstraction at once (psychosexual stages, structural areas, object relations, developmental origins, interpersonal styles). Several areas (the centers, spirituality, therapeutic and business applications, relationships) are promised for future works.
- Analogy: "What Mendeleev's periodic table is to chemistry, the Enneagram may be to psychology"; too complex to have been designed by the human mind; a symbol that "reflects the symmetries and irregularities of the mind itself."

# CHAPTER 15 — POSTSCRIPT: MOVING TOWARD WISDOM [s.26]

The book's closing essay (existential and spiritual in tone).

- **The ego's paradox:** each person's ego buffers itself from the insecurity of existence by inflating itself in type-specific ways; the personality cannot exist without defending itself, yet pushing defenses to extremes brings destruction. Too much openness and it is overwhelmed; too little and it destroys itself from within. Existential anxiety "may be the proper response for beings who are aware of their own mortality" ("Like Moses before the burning bush").
- **Meaning and faith:** the way out is to hope to find a meaning connected with "something real beyond the concerns of our personality," though we cannot step outside our lives to see it (perhaps only at death). The universe may be meaningless recycling of matter and energy, or personally grounded in God, or a divine intelligence not personal in any recognizable form; the meaning of life always involves "faith," whether so named or not. Mystics say direct knowledge is possible only by transcending personality. Without it, we "create idols" as substitutes.
- **The supreme idol is pride** (ego inflating itself, seeking to be the cause of its own being). Each type's particular temptation toward pride:

| Type | Tempted to believe... |
|---|---|
| Nine | its tranquillity is an ultimate value |
| Eight | in its own strength and will |
| Seven | it will find fulfillment in exciting experiences |
| Six | it can create ultimate security for itself |
| Five | in knowledge as a source of power |
| Four | all its feelings and subjective states are significant |
| Three | in its own excellence |
| Two | in its own indispensability |
| One | in its own righteousness |

- **The book's theme:** every type creates a self-fulfilling prophecy, bringing about what it most fears while losing what it most desires. We do not so much fail to see who we are as see "only a small part of who we are," blinded by "the trance of our personality." Our true nature exists only now, includes and transcends the personality's concerns. Each type holds a source of self-deception that leads away from real fulfillment ("an irrevocable law of the psyche").
- **How each type's quest is self-defeating:** Twos search for love from others and still feel unloved; Threes pursue achievement and still feel worthless and empty; Fours seek the meaning of identity and still do not know who they are; Fives accumulate knowledge and skills and still feel helpless and incapable; Sixes toil for security and still feel anxious; Sevens seek happiness and still feel unhappy and frustrated; Eights protect themselves and still feel vulnerable and threatened; Nines sacrifice for inner peace and still feel ungrounded and insecure; Ones strive for integrity and still feel divided and at war with themselves. The way out: "dying to ourselves," that is, to the ego and its strategies.
- **Law of psychic economics:** each moment holds a finite amount of mental, emotional and physical energy; spending it on one activity means it is not spent on another; an hour of meditation and an hour of drinking beer in front of television yield very different results; the price is not immediately apparent but "the cost to ourselves is always paid in the kind of person we become."
- **What each type thinks will make it happy (and is therefore postponing happiness until demanded conditions are met):** Twos, being loved sufficiently; Threes, being admired and outstanding; Fours, being totally free to be themselves; Fives, all the knowledge and skills they want; Sixes, enough security; Sevens, experiencing all they want; Eights, completely protecting themselves; Nines, total peace of mind; Ones, being perfect enough. These are "partial goods which have been raised to the status of the prime good in life."
- **What each type genuinely needs lies in its direction of integration** (the postscript's formulation; each states what to overcome and what to move toward):

| Type | Overcome | By moving toward the (healthy) |
|---|---|---|
| Two | tendency to deceive themselves about needs, feelings, motives | self-understanding and emotional honesty of Four |
| Three | desire to surpass others and draw attention | commitment and humility of Six |
| Four | moodiness and self-indulgence | integrity and self-discipline of One |
| Five | detachment and cynicism | practicality and courage of Eight |
| Six | pessimism and suspicion of others | hopefulness and receptivity of Nine |
| Seven | superficiality and impulsiveness | depth and focus of Five |
| Eight | emotional armoring and egocentricity | compassion and concern for others of Two |
| Nine | complacency and self-forgetting | energy and self-investment of Three |
| One | criticalness and rigidity | joy and enthusiasm of Seven |

(Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material as the integration chart; the "overcome / toward" phrasing is this book's own.)

- **Self-transcendence** is difficult and frightening: foreign, contrary to old habits and identity, "a rebirth, a true transformation." It means being willing to feel the limitation and pain that ego habits cause. "In the last analysis, learning how to transcend the ego involves nothing less than learning how to be open to love"; only love can save us from ourselves, including accepting being loved. The book cites Freud's goal of therapy ("to work and to love") and criticizes modern psychology for abjuring the transcendent.
- **"The longest way round is the shortest way home"** (Alan Watts quotation): the direction of integration brings us "full circle back to ourselves." A long Watts passage on "divine discontent" (techniques can adjust parts of a life but not the whole; compare Augustine, "Thou hast made us for Thyself...") closes the argument.
- **Closing stance on the system:** psychology, self-help books and the Enneagram "cannot save us"; the types "remain but crude reflections of human nature"; the Enneagram "is merely a tool, something useful up to a certain point, whereupon we arrive at the threshold of the spirit."

# APPENDIX — THE MEANING OF THE LEVELS OF DEVELOPMENT [s.27]

(The appendix expands the short Level descriptions given in Chapter 13. It cites the authors' companion book *Understanding the Enneagram* for Basic Fear, Basic Desire, sense of self and defense mechanisms.)

## The nine Levels, with their names and fuller meanings

| Level | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **The Level of Liberation** | By confronting and surmounting the Basic Fear, the person is liberated into ego transcendence and begins to actualize the essential self; paradoxically attains the Basic Desire and fulfills real needs. Particular spiritual capacities and virtues emerge, different for each type. Healthiest state, balance and freedom; sense of self shifts from "personality" to "essence"; may incorporate healthy qualities from the type in the direction of integration. |
| **2** | **The Level of Psychological Capacity** | If the person succumbs to the Basic Fear, a Basic Desire arises to compensate. Still healthy, but ego and defenses begin to develop in response to anxieties. Sense of self and "cognitive style" (correlated to Jung's attitudes and functions) manifest. The Basic Desire is a universal human need which, acted on rightly, is "a key to transcending the self." |
| **3** | **The Level of Social Value** | Succumbing to secondary (derivative) fears and desires, the ego becomes more active, producing a characteristic persona with social and interpersonal qualities. Still healthy, less so; ego and persona protected by defenses. The healthy social characteristics the type brings to others; the ego tries to reinforce through action the positive qualities identified at Level 2. Capable of attaining or regaining Level 1 by overcoming the Basic Fear and acting properly on the Basic Desire. |
| **4** | **The Level of Imbalance** | Succumbing to a significant "Characteristic Temptation" violating the person's own interests: ego inflated, defenses increased, imbalances introduced, maintained by drawing on the type's source of psychic energy. A descent into the psychological "dead end"; strong identification with a social role that must be defended. |
| **5** | **The Level of Interpersonal Control** | The ego inflates significantly as the person tries to control the environment, especially other people, in characteristic ways (in the withdrawn types, "ego inflation" is negative: deflation of the persona and withdrawal from interaction). The person needs others to accept and reinforce the self-image. Defense mechanisms cause interpersonal and intrapsychic conflict; traits noticeably more negative; the turning point of the deterioration. |
| **6** | **The Level of Overcompensation** | Overcompensation for conflicts and anxieties from ego inflation and from failure of Level 5 behavior. A characteristic form of self-centeredness emerges; extreme behavior others find highly objectionable though not pathological; conflicts with others; each type tends to inflict on others actions related to its Basic Fear. |
| **7** | **The Level of Violation** | Defenses have not worked; each type uses a different survival tactic, an unhealthy "self-protective" response, to bolster the ego under serious anxiety; it violates the integrity of the self or others, creating serious interpersonal conflicts. Severely neurotic, not yet fully pathological; typically a response to extreme environmental stressors and/or a history of severe, chronic childhood abuse. |
| **8** | **The Level of Delusional Thinking and Compulsive Behavior** | Very serious intrapsychic conflicts; the person tries to remake reality rather than succumb to anxiety; thinking, perceiving, feeling and behavior severely distorted and unfree: fully pathological; becomes delusional in a type-specific way; behavior is "compulsive." The psychological capacity that emerged at Level 2 and inflated at Level 5 is now delusional. |
| **9** | **The Level of Pathological Destructiveness** | The final pathological state with openly destructive behavior; willing to destroy self, others or both to defend the ego structure and its delusions; immediate or remote, conscious or unconscious destructiveness (including latent self-destructiveness); breakdown, violence or death. |

- **Two observations:** the ego emerges at Level 2 and becomes increasingly inflated and destructive by Level 9; personal freedom runs the reverse way: most free at Level 1, increasingly unfree ("compulsive") toward Level 9. "Neurosis is fundamentally unfree, while health is marked by freedom—and further integration is marked by the increasing personal freedom."

## The Core Dynamics [s.27]
- **What they are:** a schematic representation of each type, with clusters of terms for behaviors and attitudes at each of the nine Levels plus the key fears and motivations at each Level driving the personality toward health or neurosis; "the dynamic component of the Levels." Uses claimed: self-discovery (understand the states and obstacles), therapy (identify the layers of fears and desires holding a client), reducing interpersonal conflict, sorting out traits properly belonging to each type versus misattributed ones.
- **Provenance statement:** the Levels of Development were discovered by Don Richard Riso in 1977 and developed over fourteen years; in 1991 Russ Hudson began collaborating to bring the Levels and Core Dynamics to their current refinement. The Levels, the Core Dynamics and virtually all type descriptions represent the authors' original work, with debt to Oscar Ichazo, Claudio Naranjo and forerunners; this book is the first publication of the Core Dynamics.
- **Layout (the template every type follows):** at the top, the type number, name and **parental orientation**; four columns: **B-term (Behaviors), A-term (Attitudes), Desires, Fears**, running down nine Levels (the appendix warns: do not confuse the nine Levels with the nine types).
  - It begins with the **Basic Fear** (upper right): the fundamental insecurity the type tries to "solve" or repress. Everyone has all nine, but one's own is strongest. All Basic Fears are variations of a deeper fear of nonbeing, "of not existing."
  - An arrow leads to the **Basic Desire**, the central motivation, the type's way of managing the Basic Fear; universal needs (to feel loved and valuable, to know who one is, to feel competent, to be secure and happy, to be free, to be good, to be at peace), legitimate in themselves; trouble starts when pursuit narrows life.
  - **The turning point:** from the Basic Desire two arrows lead: one up to **Self-actualization / Level 1** (liberation, the true self or Essence), and one left into the ego world at Level 2.
  - **Level 2 A-term** ("Level of Psychological Capacity"): five or six words with a boldfaced "key term" for the cluster; these are the capacities the type draws on to reach its Basic Desire and the base of its ego structure. Worked example, Type Two: Basic Fear "of being unwanted, unworthy of being loved," Basic Desire "to be loved unconditionally," A-term key word "Empathetic" (concerned, other-oriented, forgiving, compassionate).
  - **Level 2 B-term = the self-image, the "I am" terms** (unlike other Levels): Two's are "Loving, caring, selfless, thoughtful, warm-hearted, passionate."
  - Identifying with a self-image creates a **Secondary Fear** (for Two: "That their own needs and negative feelings will harm their relationships"). It is more immediately felt and sometimes obscures the Basic Fear.
  - Succumbing to it produces a **Secondary Desire** at Level 3 (Two: "To do good things for others"), an A-term "Supportive" (appreciative, sympathetic, nurturing) and B-term "Giving" (generous, encouraging, helpful). From Level 3 on, **A-terms are internal attitudes (generally invisible)** and **B-terms are visible behaviors or self-expression.**
  - Each Level's behaviors must again be defended, causing a new fear (Two at Level 3: "That whatever they have been doing for others is not enough to gain their love"), a compensating desire at Level 4, and so on to Level 9: a **spiral structure**: fear sets a desire in motion, leading to attitudes, then behaviors, then a new fear and the next Level down.
  - **The italicized words** at the bottom of each A-term and B-term cluster beginning at Level 4 are qualities from the type's direction of disintegration, a "parallel track" showing what arises under heightened stress.
- **Interpretation of the structure:** fear drives us deeper into restrictive ego states; children in dysfunctional environments have more fears and layer on more defenses, so adults low on the Levels developed "more elaborate systems of defense." To lighten the burden, **follow the spiral up against the arrows:** let oneself experience the fear at the current Level and each fear above it, and let go of the behaviors, attitudes and desires clung to, recognizing there are more effective ways to the Basic Desire.
- **Level 1:** to reach it one must confront the Basic Fear and let go of the self-image formed at Level 2 (for Two: confront the fear of being unwanted and the self-image "that they are not allowed to take care of themselves and their own needs"), which frees the full range of feelings and motivations without seeing oneself as "selfish." Level 1 is "the beginning of another" road: the world of the True Self, the Essence; a person liberated from the type could integrate the positive qualities of all nine types. (The text says more is "for another time.")
