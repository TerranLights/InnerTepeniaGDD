## Overview of this range

The authors present the Enneagram as an old, dynamic typology of nine character patterns that is meant to produce change, not just description. Each pattern is organized around one "root sin" or "passion" (a compulsion that gives the person energy and that was adopted in childhood as a survival strategy) and its reverse, a "fruit of the spirit" or gift. The two are said to be two sides of one coin: the gift is the sin "sublimated and transformed." The book argues that people are trapped by an "idealized self-image" (the "false self") and that self-knowledge, delivered in a humiliating but liberating way, opens the way to the "true self." The range also gives an unusual historical account (the authors place the system's roots in Evagrius Ponticus and Ramón Lull rather than in Sufi sources), the three centers (gut, heart, head), a method for reading the type descriptions, a "soul child" reading of each type (Preface to Part II), and then the full profiles of ONE and TWO and the opening of THREE. The authors say plainly that their type sketches are caricatures meant to make outlines stand out. [p.19-41, 43-66]

## Preface: A Mirror of the Soul (Andreas Ebert) [p.9-12]

- **Publication context.** First German edition, late summer 1989; more than 300,000 copies sold by this revision. The book grew out of Richard Rohr's taped Enneagram workshop (Center for Action and Contemplation, Albuquerque, 1988) and Ebert's own work, in a deliberately lively, not systematic, style. Ebert first met the Enneagram through Rohr in 1985, when Rohr led the New Jerusalem community in Cincinnati. Jesuits and Communities of Christian Life in the German-speaking world had already used it for retreats and training spiritual counselors. [p.9-10]
- **History correction.** The authors now hold that the Enneagram does not derive from medieval Islamic (Sufi) sources but can be traced "at least in part" to the desert monk Evagrius Ponticus (d. 399) and the Franciscan Ramón Lull (1236-1315). [p.9]
- **Warning against misuse.** A typology can be used to force oneself and others into a schematic mold, producing fossilization rather than growth; "When misused, the Enneagram can be more of a curse than a blessing." Self-knowledge is demanding and painful, "birth pangs." [p.10]
- **Roots and the "lingua franca" claim.** The Enneagram arises from a perspective on psychic strivings whose roots reach at least to the Desert Fathers, perhaps to Pythagoras, and was presumably passed on orally through Sufism. Because mystical currents of different religions converge, the Enneagram is described as "a lingua franca with a minimum of baggage" that serves intercultural and interreligious dialogue. [p.10]
- **The mystical model of the person.** In the first half of life one builds an "empirical ego," the sum of attitudes and behavior mechanisms; overidentification with roles, habits and character features is "the chief obstacle" to the true self. All mystical ways offer methods to unmask this illusory self (knowledge, asceticism, good works, meditation). Johannes Tauler is quoted: until the ego's threefold "something" (having, knowing, willing) dies, we have a hard time. [p.10-11]
- **[Christian framing]** Redemption from the false self is a gift of grace; how far we can prepare for it is the disputed question, usually resolved as "pray as if everything depended on God and act as though everything depended on you" (Phil. 2:12-13). Ebert argues that the Church has always "baptized" outside models (Hippocrates' four temperaments via Ole Hallesby, 1927; Fritz Riemann's basic anxieties) and that the Enneagram, with "genuinely Christian features," qualifies. He cites Rev. 21:26 (nations bring gifts into the city of God) as warrant for borrowing wisdom from other traditions. [p.11]
- **Status of the tool.** "As a mirror of the soul the Enneagram remains a tool that can be laid aside at any time. The Enneagram is not the answer, but one signpost among many." It is "only a cognitive model, though an amazingly comprehensive one." Ebert is glad that it has not been exalted into a doctrine of salvation or a sect. [p.12]

## Preface: Discernment: How to See, How to Hear (Richard Rohr) [p.13-17]

Mostly an essay on religion and transformation. The points that bear on the typology:

- **The foundational insight.** "Human beings cannot see what they are not readied to see." There is always a level beneath the obvious that predetermines how we see, what we see, and our preconscious emotional reaction. A community slogan from New Jerusalem: "The issue is hardly ever the issue." [p.14]
- **Information vs. transformation.** Being informed differs from being formed. The author draws on Michael Polanyi's "tacit knowing" (all knowing happens inside a previous silent, unspoken context; geniuses "dwell inside" things) and Eric Hoffer ("learners inherit the earth, while the learned find themselves beautifully equipped for a world that no longer exists"). [p.14-15]
- **What the Enneagram is, per Rohr.** A tool "much more demanding and much more dangerous than believing things"; it is about "unbelieving" the disguise we all are. Ernest Becker called this disguise "our vital lie"; Merton called it the false self; "the Enneagram gets right to the point and calls it our sin." It is "a parabolic form of teaching," subverting the unconscious "mythical" worldview. [p.15]
- **Three voices.** The voices of the ego are "totally for or totally against" (tiny and insecure); the voices of the superego sound like Mom, Dad, teachers and church (too large, archetypal); the Indwelling Voice (Holy Spirit) sounds "different, unpredictable, scarily new." [p.16]
- **Two forms of religion.** One believes that God will love me if I change; the other that God loves me so that I can change. "Ideas inform us, but love forms us." [p.16-17]
- **Training in the middle position.** The Center for Action and Contemplation teaches "discernment of spirits," staying in the "middle" and waiting for a "Third Something," to resist dualistic thinking; the Enneagram is "an amazing tool to lead people into such liminal space." Rohr cites restorative vs. retributive justice (New Zealand bishops, 1996). [p.17]
- **Experience warning.** "The Enneagram, like the Spirit of truth itself, will always set you free, but first it will make you miserable!" (Mark 1:13 wild beasts, then angels). [p.17]
- **[Christian framing]** Suffering and prayer as the two things strong enough to decenter ego and superego; Jesus' "cup" saying (Mark 10:38-39) as the pattern (we suffer, are transformed, and only then have a message). [p.15-16]

# PART I: THE SLEEPING GIANT [p.18-41]

## A Dynamic Typology [p.19-20]

- **Typologies as maps.** The Enneagram "is a very old typology that describes nine different characters" and shares with others the crude reduction of behavior to a limited set of types. Authors compare: astrology (twelve types); Hippocrates (four temperaments from four bodily fluids); Kretschmer (body build and temperament); Jung (three function pairs: extroverted-introverted, sensate-intuitive, thinking-feeling, giving eight combinations); Isabel Briggs Myers (adds judging-perceiving, giving sixteen); Karen Horney (submission, hostility, withdrawal; later love, submissiveness, power, distancing); Fritz Riemann (four fears: nearness, distance, change, permanence; four types schizoid, depressive, compulsive, hysterical). All are "maps"; "none of them is the thing itself"; the map never replaces the country. [p.19]
- **Danger of typologies.** They neglect uniqueness and can freeze the individual; pattern discovery matters only if the possibility of change comes into view. [p.19]
- **What is distinctive about the Enneagram, per the authors.** "Beyond the description of conditions, the Enneagram contains an inner dynamic that aims at change." It concerns "conversion or repentance" in religious language; it "demands a lot and is exhausting." [p.19-20]
- **Origin of the pattern in the person.** The starting point is the "blind alleys" into which we stumble while protecting life from internal and external threats. The true self (a "very good" creation) is exposed to assault from conception onward; **[Christian framing]** original sin points to the fact that no undamaged person exists. Parents, siblings, peers, teachers, group norms and society leave "voices" in the inner life, short sentences ("Always be nice and say thank you!" verbal; "Don't come too close to me!" nonverbal). [p.20]
- **The mechanics, compressed.** The growing person (1) internalizes ideals ("I am good, if I..."), (2) develops avoidance strategies to escape punishment, (3) builds defense mechanisms. Guilt feelings appear when the ideal is not met; the real misconduct, the nine "root sins," stays hidden. "Our 'sins' are the other side of our gift. They are the way we get our energy. They 'work' for us." [p.20]
- **Direction of attention.** More important than "whence?" (causes) is "whither?" (the goal); cites John 9:1-3. [p.20]
- **Diagram description.** "Enneagram" from Greek *ennea* (nine) and *gramma* (sign or figure). Circle with nine points 40 degrees apart, numbered clockwise from ONE to NINE with NINE at twelve o'clock. Points THREE, SIX, NINE joined by a triangle; TWO, FOUR, ONE, SEVEN, FIVE, EIGHT (and back to TWO) by an irregular six-pointed star. Each number is "a certain state of energy"; transitions are fluid; the lines point to the dynamics between points. [p.20-21]

## The Mystery of the Number 153 (history of the Enneagram) [p.21-25]

**The older legend (as the authors tell it).** Until recently the origin was veiled. Gurdjieff presented the figure in the West in 1916, as a symbol of the cosmos's harmonic structure, not a typology of character, and never named sources. His disciple J. G. Bennett said Gurdjieff learned it from Sufis. Oscar Ichazo, who developed the "Enneagram of fixations" in the early 1970s, used Gurdjieff's model and referred to hidden Sufi sources and his own visions. The legend: many millennia back in the Near East; Bennett names the Magi; Pythagoras (ca. 569-496 B.C.E.) was initiated by the Magi and founded a school in southern Italy with exoteric and esoteric sections, where the numbers one to nine had quantitative and qualitative meaning (ten for the cosmos as a whole). A thousand-year gap, then Sufis said to preserve it orally; but "in the whole body of Sufi literature there is not the faintest allusion to the Enneagram." [p.21-22]

**Ebert's discovery (1995-96).** Ebert found a text by Evagrius Ponticus that he sensed was connected; he published it in *Enneagram Monthly* no. 11 (Jan. 1996, "Are the Origins of the Enneagram Christian After All?"). Lynn Quirolo (graduate of J. G. Bennett's academy at Sherborne) independently found the same in April-May 1996 ("Pythagoras, Gurdjieff and the Enneagram") and contributed the decoding of the Pythagorean number symbolism. Jesuit Robert Ochs (early disciple of Claudio Naranjo) was already convinced of Christian roots, and Anselm Grün noted parallels with Evagrius's teaching on passions in 1992. [p.22]

**Evagrius Ponticus (born 345, Ibora, Pontus; died 399, Nitrian desert).** Son of a bishop; deacon at 34; left Constantinople after a love affair; lived as a monk in Egypt. Influenced by Origen (who was influenced by Pythagorean thought and favored allegorical reading). After his death his partisans fled to Armenia and the Arab world; condemned together with Origen at the Council of Jerusalem (553) and by three later councils. [p.22-23]

**The two points of contact with the Enneagram.** [p.23]
1. **Teaching on the passions.** A list of eight (nine in one passage of *De vitiis quae opposita sunt virtutibus*) vices or distracting "thoughts" that block the way to God and to passionless peace of heart (*hesychia*). Starting point: Matthew 12:43-45 (the unclean spirit returns with seven worse spirits). The first, gluttony, "incorporates" the other seven. The authors map the list onto the Enneagram:

| Evagrius's vice | Enneagram type |
|---|---|
| anger | ONE |
| pride | TWO |
| vanity or thirst for glory | THREE |
| sadness (self-pity) or envy | FOUR |
| avarice | FIVE |
| (fear is missing from Evagrius) | SIX |
| gluttony | SEVEN |
| lust | EIGHT |
| laziness or "acedia" | NINE |

   Pope Gregory I later shortened this to the seven deadly sins (anger, pride, envy, greed, gluttony, lust, sloth), dropping vanity and sadness. The authors state that Evagrius did **not** systematize these as the "Enneagram of fixations"; that arrangement is Ichazo's twentieth-century creation. [p.23]
2. **The number 153.** In John 21, the risen Jesus has the disciples cast their net and they catch 153 large fish. Jerome (c. 420) read 153 as the number of species of fish, thus the universality of the church. Augustine read it as 50 times three plus three (Trinity), the sum of 1 to 17 (seven plus ten). [p.23] Evagrius's introduction to his *153 Chapters on Prayer* (part of the *Philokalia*) calls 153 a number that joins a triangle and a hexagon (triangle = Trinity; hexagon = the six days of creation). He also divides 153 into 100 + 28 + 25: 100 a square, 28 a triangle, 25 a square-and-circle; the square refers to the fourfold quality of the (seven) virtues, the triangle (28) to knowledge of the Trinity, the circle to the "river of time" and true knowledge of the world. Another reading: the whole is a triangle (ascetical practice, contemplation of nature, spiritual knowledge of God; or faith, hope, love; or gold, silver, precious stones). [p.24]
   - **Pythagorean number types as given:** *triangular* numbers (sum of consecutive numbers from 1: 3, 6, 10 ...); *square* (1+3+5...: 4, 9, 16 ...); *hexagonal* (1+5+9...: 6, 16, 28 ...); *circular* (a number whose square ends in itself: 25, 36). 153 is triangular (1 through 17) and hexagonal (1+5+9+...+35). [p.24]
- **Authors' conclusion.** Evagrius developed a psychology of character on eight (once nine) evil "thoughts" and, separately, a cosmology symbolized by circle, square and hexagon; there is "no hint" that he linked them. The two most important elements of modern Enneagram lore (a cosmic symbol with triangle, circle and hexagon; a character teaching based on "deadly sins") can be traced to him, but it would be "an exaggeration" to say the Enneagram "as we know it" goes back to him in toto. [p.25]

## Ramón Lull, 1236-1315 [p.25-28]

- **Life.** Born Palma, Majorca, in a milieu marked by Moorish culture; married 1253, court page, "worldly" life; converted in June 1263 after five appearances of the crucified Christ; became a Franciscan tertiary; sought arguments for Christianity to aid conversion of Jews and Muslims, founded Arabic and Hebrew language schools; pilgrimages to Jerusalem and Santiago. [p.25]
- **Method.** Free, respectful, rational dialogue in which all participants learn; "hermeneutics of the Other." *The Book of the Pagan and the Three Wise Men*: a seeker listens in turn to a Jew, a Christian and a Muslim; no one interrupts; the seeker alone may ask critical questions; the choice stays open (compare Lessing's parable of the rings). Lull held that interreligious "Babel" was a cause of war and that monotheistic religions could build a common world ethos on nonviolence. [p.26]
- **Figures A and T.** Figure A: God's essence "A" plus nine qualities (letters B to K) arranged clockwise around a circle, linked to a center and to one another. Figure T: a circle filled with three triangles for "relative" principles: difference / harmony / contrariness; being-greater / equality / being-smaller; beginning / middle / end. The authors call these "proto-Enneagrams." [p.26-28]
- **Virtues and vices.** Lull drew the seven classic virtues and seven deadly sins as trees; the religion bearing the most convincing fruits of virtue is the right one, which moves competition from dogma to ethics. His closeness to Cabbala (tree of life) and Sufism (the ninety-nine names) is noted; Umberto Eco is cited calling Lull's sketches the second great European attempt, after the Cabbala, at a new language of faith. [p.26-27]
- **[Christian framing]** Lull is held up for a humble, learning, powerless church; he is said to have been valued by Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross, Las Casas, Nicholas of Cusa and Schopenhauer. [p.28]

## Gurdjieff, the Sufis, Ichazo, Naranjo, and the Jesuits [p.28-30]

- **Gurdjieff** (born 1877 to an Armenian mother and Greek father at the Armenian-Georgian frontier, where Evagrius is still venerated) trained to be a doctor and a Russian Orthodox priest; traveled to Egypt, India, Tibet; said to have encountered the tradition in a monastery of the Sufi "Sarmoun" brotherhood. Called himself a "Pythagorean Greek" and an "esoteric Christian." Called the Enneagram a "perpetuum mobile" and compared it to the "philosopher's stone"; parts of his dance and movement forms were based on it. As a teaching about psychological types, it "played no role" in his thought; he never described nine personality types. [p.28-29]
- **Quirolo is quoted** that Evagrius contains a developed contemplative psychology and a Pythagorean reading of a biblical number, fragments of both of which appear in Gurdjieff and Ouspensky. [p.29]
- **Sufis (as described).** Began within about a hundred years of Muhammad; wore coarse wool (*suf*); wandering preachers or brotherhoods; many women; love of God central (a prayer of Rabia al-Adawiyya is quoted: adore for God's own sake, not for fear of hell nor hope of paradise); orders of dervishes and fakirs; a tradition of spiritual guidance, oral, master to disciple. [p.29]
- **Ichazo.** Taught in La Paz, Bolivia, and Arica, Chile (1950s-60s); to the United States in 1971; claims he learned the system from Sufi masters in the Pamir and had read Gurdjieff before. The form "best known today" (the Enneagram of fixations) goes back to him. [p.29]
- **Naranjo and the Jesuits.** The psychiatrist Claudio Naranjo (Esalen, Big Sur) adopted and developed Ichazo's model; American Jesuits, above all Robert Ochs, met it through him and after "long years of testing and theological scrutiny" adopted it for spiritual counseling and retreats. Since the mid-1980s a flood of books came from religious orders and from psychoanalysis and humanistic psychology. [p.29-30]
- **Scientific status.** No claim to scientific corroboration; clinical studies under way in the US; the First International Enneagram Conference was hosted at Stanford in 1994. [p.30]
- **Stance on integration.** In Eastern and Western spiritual traditions, psychic maturity and spiritual maturity belong together; one would not find someone analytically integrated but spiritually atrophied, or religious but visibly unstable. [p.30]

## Breakthrough to the Totally Other [p.30-31]

- The question the Enneagram asks: why do we keep running up against ourselves instead of breaking through to God? Egocentric society leaves God as a projection (a God we desire, fear, or culturally need).
- The old masters called habitual perception from an egocentric viewpoint **"passions"** (a medieval usage): unrecognized passions let me mistake my limited perspective for the whole. The task is to overcome them and perceive reality more objectively. [p.30]
- **Ignatian discernment of spirits in three steps** (as summarized): (1) sense the stirrings in the soul; (2) recognize them (origin, goal, constructive or destructive); (3) take a position (accept or reject). The authors present the Enneagram as "a related tool, and in some ways a still more precise tool"; some retreat masters use it alongside the Spiritual Exercises. [p.30-31] **[Christian framing]**

## A Cardinal Wakes Up [p.31]

- Rohr was introduced to the Enneagram by Jesuit director Jim O'Brien, S.J., in the early 1970s, with the injunction not to pass it on in writing or reveal the source. Using it in pastoral counseling, he could grasp a person's "energy" or "mode of perception" quickly, which looked like clairvoyance ("seeing the heart").
- On the history of its use: originally esoteric, passed on only within groups; the Sufis never wrote it down. Once Americans got it, it was offered for mass consumption.
- The approach is "negative": it does not flatter the empirical ego but supports letting go of what Merton calls the false self. At a retreat for twenty bishops, interest awoke only when the Enneagram was introduced on the third day; two cardinals "suddenly woke up."

## A Sobering Aha-Experience [p.31-32]

- Rohr's discovery, as a ONE, was "I had always done the right thing ... but for false motives." Rule of thumb: "If you don't sense the whole thing as somehow humiliating, you haven't yet found your number." Someone who says "It's wonderful that I'm a THREE" either is not a THREE or has not seen how disastrous the pattern is. [p.32]
- **Method of presentation.** Say "ONEs, like me, are often opinionated and dogmatic," putting the shoe on one's own foot first. The authors say that people who see their own life-lie make fewer snap judgments about others.
- **On "typos."** Beginners will pigeonhole everyone; in about three-quarters of cases they will first mistype, because only certain external features catch the eye; one must press past traits to the energy behind them. Humor helps. Once self-knowledge is deep, "we can confidently put aside the tool."
- **Overall aim:** to become more loving (of others, self, God). **[Christian framing]** "God makes use of our sins," a shame-and-freedom discovery; God loves "something imperfect." [p.32]

## Gifted Sinners [p.32-34]

- **Core claim.** The Desert Fathers and Sufis held that people are destroyed by their gifts as much as by their sins, because they identify with what they do well. We cling to what comes naturally: "a natural prejudice ... natural models of behavior, a natural standpoint, a natural passion," formed mostly in the first thirty years of life. [p.32-33]
- **Life-stage claim.** The Enneagram is not very useful early in life: young people must build and strengthen the ego (John 21:18 cited). Around the thirties or at the latest forty, the game "gets increasingly dull"; ego boundaries harden; the middle of life is "the great opportunity" to reflect critically and mature. [p.33]
- **Self-image.** "There is nothing on which people are so fixated on as on their self-image"; people will go through hell not to give it up. The question: "Do I have the freedom to be anything other than this role and this image?" [p.33]
- **Gift and sin as one coin.** "Every gift that we get excessively fixated on paradoxically becomes our sin. Our gift and our sin are two sides of the same coin." To meet your gift you must "chew, eat, and digest your sin." "Our gift is our sin sublimated and transformed by grace." The old self dies, which feels like death; you realize how many people you used to maintain your self-image (those who did not play along were banned from your circle). [p.33]
- **Enemies as teachers.** Types who threaten us discover our game or don't need it; "our enemies are our best friends" because they carry a message from the "not-I." Rohr notes some people find his way (a ONE's moral voice) poisonous. [p.34] **[Christian framing]** (Love your enemies; the Body of Christ with differing gifts.)

## The Truth Is Simple and Beautiful [p.34]

- Einstein-style argument: a true universal formula must be simple and beautiful. The Enneagram "terrifies and challenges us" but is simple and beautiful because it shows us as small, partial, broken, "all in the same boat."
- "We have to limit our gift; otherwise our sin becomes a trap while we call it 'virtue.'" Aquinas and the Scholastics: everyone chooses something that appears good; no one willingly does evil; each of us builds a construct explaining why what we do is necessary and good.
- **Inner observer / "fair witness."** The authors recommend installing an honest part of oneself that notes both negatives and positives ("You really love God ... Stop butchering yourself"). This helps tell moralizing from morality, guilt feelings from real guilt, false pride from genuine strength. The goal is letting go of what only seems good to find what is really good.
- Religious people are said to be hard to work with because of the moralizing voices ("Good, better, best, right, false, holy, mortal sin, venial sin ..."); creation spirituality, native American spirituality and Franciscan spirituality are named as correctives where nature "has the word."

## People Are Creatures of Habit [p.35]

- "Our sin ... is also, paradoxically, the method that helps us to get to our driving force." When we commit our "favorite sin" we are "fully there"; it belongs to the survival strategy adopted as a child, so it cannot be simply dropped. We find our gift where our sin is, "while fully aware of the danger and nature of addiction."
- **Worked example (ONE, Rohr).** ONEs are idealists and perfectionists, irritated (secretly) that the world is not perfect; "geniuses of perception"; hypercritical carpers when fixated. "Excess turns all gifts into curses" (all nine types). A ONE gets energy through anger, the capital sin, but must be able to say "That's enough, Richard!"; the "objective observer" lets responsibility and freedom work together.
- **Ideologues.** Those who supervise life "from an imaginary control tower in their head" (right-wing, left-wing, liberal, conservative). "Otherwise in the end you won't have any feelings; they'll have you." Healthy people "put themselves forward and relativize themselves" at once. This is the work of "detachment, self-emptying, and 'fasting.'" [p.35]

## Obsessions [p.35-36]

- The capital sin functions "like a sexual obsession": it determines us at least every ten minutes, probably every three or five. It lives in limbs, bones, blood, skin, breath, gestures. It is like an addiction, which may be why it is called "passion."
- **On "a little bit of everything."** To say "I'm a bit of a FOUR, a bit of a SIX, and a bit of a TWO" is true, but the crucial point is to see "our one big sin": "a key dilemma, a main root of evil, a favorite vice with each of us." It colors all areas of life and is so present that we do not see it. "We were always this way." Catching it is "a great aha-experience": the same behavior models were present in childhood; "the crimson thread" explains why one chose certain friends or sports. "I did all the right things for the wrong reasons!" [p.35-36]
- When stuck in the pitfall, "we aren't free": external events and others decide whether we are rewarded or punished. [p.36]

## The Way to Self-Worth [p.36]

- **[Christian framing]** The "unconditioned love of God" frees us to experience ourselves as "authentically strong"; the Holy Spirit as *dynamis*; the "devil" or evil keeps us from this power and dignity. Working through the compulsion leads to "a purified passion, a chastened power, our best and true self," the "soul," where human and God meet. "Self-worth is not created; it is discovered." The authors say they have seen people change this way.

## Wrong Ways and Ways Out (the nine sins; sin concepts; virtues) [p.36-38]

- **Definition of the types.** "The Enneagram defines its nine human types on the basis of nine 'traps,' 'passions,' or 'deadly sins.'" They are emergency solutions of early childhood, necessary for survival but later "the emergency from which we have to be redeemed." Nine sins = the seven deadly sins (pride, envy, anger, sloth, avarice, lack of moderation/"gluttony," unchastity) plus two missing from church teaching: **deceit** (THREE) and **fear** (SIX). [p.36]
- **A warning.** "Don't 'go after' your sin directly": you will attack your ONE in a ONE way, "understand" your FIVE as another FIVE trip, try to "succeed" at being a redeemed THREE. "The key is to recognize, name, and let go." [p.36]
- **History of lists.** Evagrius and his disciple John Cassian: eight root sins. Gregory the Great: pride as primal sin radiating into haughtiness, envy, anger, avarice, unchastity, incontinence, laziness; seven became standard (Dante's *Purgatorio*, Chaucer's Parson's Tale, Bosch). [p.36]
- **"Root sin" vs. "mortal sin."** "Root sin" assumes a tree whose main roots give rise to other sins; "mortal sin" concerns consequences (Rom. 6:23; James 1:13-15 desire to sin to death; 1 John 5:16). The authors use "root sin" to stress radical (from *radix*, root) renewal. They argue sin has a concrete deadly reach (killed forests, arsenals, poor paying for the envy and greed of rich nations, our laziness allowing this). [p.36-37]
- **Redefining "sin."** Greek *hamartia* = "missing the mark" (archery); Augustine: "Seek what you are seeking, but don't seek it where you are seeking it!" German *Sünde* contains *sund* (cleft, separation). "In this book we understand by 'sin' the self-erected blockades that cut us off from God and hence from our own authentic potential." Fear (SIX) is "not a moral category" but can still block love and life. "Sins are a kind of misleading packaging: they make a promise that they can't keep." Our sin is partly a reaction to others' guilt, yet we "have chosen it" and are responsible; blaming others keeps us trapped. [p.37]
- **Virtue catalogs mentioned.** Seven messianic gifts (Isa. 11:2); nine fruits of the spirit (Gal. 5:22: love, joy, peace, patience, amiability, kindness, fidelity, gentleness, self-control); four cardinal virtues (justice, prudence, temperance, fortitude) plus three theological (faith, hope, love). [p.37]
- **Chaucer's pairing (Parson's Tale), "practically identical" with the Enneagram pairs per the authors:** humility vs. pride; love of God vs. envy; patience vs. anger; fortitude vs. laziness; compassion vs. avarice; sobriety/moderation vs. gluttony; chastity vs. lechery. In the type chapters the authors speak of the **"fruits of the spirit"** (gift or "virtue") of each type, tied to the image of the tree of life ("A good tree brings forth good fruit," Matt. 7:17). [p.37-38]

## The Three Centers: Gut, Heart, Head [p.38-40]

- **Grouping.** Types are arranged clockwise on the circle and grouped into three: **gut** EIGHT, NINE, ONE ("sexual types": immediate, spontaneous, felt, intuitive); **heart** TWO, THREE, FOUR ("social types"); **head** FIVE, SIX, SEVEN ("self-preserving types"). [p.38]
- Horney is cited for the original three "neurotic solutions" (turning away, hostile, turning toward); Gurdjieff for head, heart, gut as three kinds of intelligence (mental, emotional, sexual/instinctive/motivational), with one predominating in each person. [p.38]

## The Nine Faces of the Soul (how to read the profiles) [p.40-41]

- The profiles are "rough sketches, sometimes caricatures," exaggeration used so outlines "stand out boldly, like woodcuts"; not every feature fits every member; even "I'm not like that" is self-knowledge. [p.40]
- **Symbols.** Each type has traditionally assigned animals (matching real or generally recognized traits), a symbolic nation (taken "with a grain of salt," not meant to stir up ethnic prejudice), colors, biblical figures, saints and historical, literary and contemporary personalities, presented as the authors' subjective estimates; readers are invited to find their own (e.g., using the Enneagram to name forces in fairy tales or relate music and dance to the nine energies). [p.40]
- **Order and wings.** They begin with ONE ("we could also begin somewhere else") and go round the circle. "Every type also contains traits of both its neighbors, the so-called wings." [p.40]
- **How to find one's type.** Read all nine descriptions first; "if in reading the description of a type I get uneasy or am even humiliated, it could be that I'm on home ground." The aha often arrives weeks or months later and after talking with people who know you; the sequence is self-evaluation, then people close to you, then someone who knows the Enneagram, and only then a questionnaire ("control-test"). [p.40-41]
- **Mature/immature spectrum.** Each type spans a continuous scale from immature to mature. An immature person, whatever the type, is "trapped in himself or herself" (Luther: *homo incurvatus in se ipsum*); takes self too seriously and thinks the perspective is the whole picture. The Sufis supposedly called the Enneagram "the face of God." Redemption means gradually leaving one's standpoint; the nearest step is toward the wings; farther numbers are alien, but being able to "put on all nine pairs of shoes" would be to see "with the eyes of God." [p.41]
- **Mature end.** None have reached it; spiritual maturity can occur without knowing the Enneagram. Community helps: "Solitary fighters will rarely undergo real conversion." **[Christian framing]** Redemption is grace: "falling into the Center: into God." [p.41]
- **Names.** "Unlike other authors we dispense with giving the nine types binding names as well as numbers." Numbers signal that the types are not evaluations: no type is better or worse; all are "fallen men and women"; all have unique gifts. [p.41]
- **Age.** The Enneagram is most meaningful in the middle years and after; ask "What was I like when I was twenty?" because at about twenty we live most immediately from our main energy. [p.41]
- **Structure of each profile.** (1) Overview; (2) Dilemma (temptation, root sin, avoidance, defense mechanism, first references to the gift or fruit of the spirit, and the "pitfall" or fixation = the ingrained perception and action model, the unconscious life program); (3) Symbols and Examples (animals, countries, colors, literature, history, Bible); (4) Conversion and Maturation (the type's "invitation," life tasks, suggestions, and a saint who used the gift creatively). Summary tables at the end of the book (outside this range). [p.41]

## Preface to Part II: Original Sin (Richard Rohr) [p.43-45]

**Main idea.** Compulsive identification is a reaction **against** one's original "soul space," which corresponds to the number one "normally goes toward for consolation." This is offered as what tradition meant by "original sin": we have abandoned our "soul child" for a defended, deceitful false identity. Rohr credits Sandra Maitri (*The Spiritual Dimension of the Enneagram*) for partly developing this pattern, and says she is "more behavioral and psychological" while he is "more theological," a spiritual-direction approach. He links the nine "deep knowings" to the nine qualities of God in the Kabbala; the tenth, feminine SOPHIA or Wisdom, is where the whole typology leads. Each type once knew a quality of God and now resists it. [p.43-44]

## TYPE ONE: The Need to Be Perfect [p.46-53]

### Overview [p.46-47]
- **Motivation and gifts.** ONEs are "idealists, motivated and driven on by longing for a true, just, and moral world." Honest, fair; spur others to work and mature; often gifted teachers who go forward setting a good example. They find imperfection hard to accept, others' and especially their own; only when focused and at rest can they accept living in a (still) imperfect present and trust the gradual growth of the good. **[Christian framing]** "the kingdom of God." [p.46]
- **Childhood story.** Model children who internalized "Be good! Behave yourself! Try hard! Don't be childish! Do it better!" and tried to earn love by meeting expectations; they built criteria for good and bad. A parent is often moralistic, perfectionistic or eternally dissatisfied, stingy with praise, taking above-average goodness for granted. Precocious achievers who didn't want to lose love. Alice Miller's "drama of the gifted child" is invoked: the child meets parental needs and loses access to its own feelings and true self. The child's signal: "You're okay only when you're perfect." Childhood "driven out"; early adult responsibility (a missing parent, being the eldest and a role model). [p.46-47]
- **Psychological notes.** Freud on cleanliness training; Riso describes ONEs as anal retentive (the refusal to produce); young Luther, "a classic ONE," had constipation. Self-control and supposed moral superiority become "compensatory pleasure" for the denied lower pleasures. [p.46]
- **Inner court.** Their parents' demands become internal: "Inside ONEs, court is continually in session; they are their own prosecutor, defender, and judge." The voices bicker and correct one another; an "objective observer of the trial" must step in and remind that the standards are subjective views, not objective truth. [p.46-47]
- **Examples.** Erich Kästner (model boy, moralist, wrote daily to his mother; his child heroes act like little adults). Rohr's own habits: coupon clipping (his mother's imprint), spotless house ("you can eat off the floor"), tidying before leaving in case he dies and someone enters; fanatical about order; cleans ashtrays and dishes and has learned to laugh at it. [p.47]

### Dilemma [p.47-50]
- **Temptation.** The search for perfection; the "Don Quixote" who tilts at windmills and dreams an "impossible dream." Joy when something approximates perfection (nature, art, music, a person), disappointment when flaws appear; above all disappointed by their own imperfection. Religion attracts them because "at least God is perfect." [p.47]
- **Everyday behavior.** Conscious of duty and responsibility; often compulsively punctual; pressured by time; precise schedule, detailed diary. Serious people who seldom tell jokes (or forget the punch line). Allow relaxation only after tasks are done, which seldom happens; hobbies are practical, helpful to others. Inclined to deny and punish themselves, to repress their needs and feelings; by nature ascetics and Puritans (Goethe: "Whoever striving toils away, him we can redeem"); guilt at slacking. Rohr calls John Calvin "surely a ONE." [p.47-48]
- **Meditation example.** A ONE woman (Helen Palmer) hears the critic: "Not deep enough"; "Sit up straighter"; "You're not trying." "Yes I am." Contemplative prayer without an axe to grind can teach acceptance. Task: learn to rest to observe imperfection (easy) and accept it (hard). [p.48]
- **Root sin: anger.** "Anger is the root sin of ONEs." Their sin and their avoidance coincide: they cannot admit the vexation that drives them; anger is itself imperfect; "Model children are not full of rage." They instantly reject a suggestion that they are enraged; ashamed of the rage; force "objectivity" ("I'm not annoyed at you, but actually I'd have the right ..."). [p.48]
- **Defense mechanism: reaction control.** A split-second censorship decides what is expressed and how. This produces great pressure: "walking steam kettles," repressed rancor that attacks the inner voices; workaholism is a way to let off steam. [p.48]
- **Self-redemption loop.** The pressure to do "good works" (drove Luther to despair); Rohr's own improver-of-the-world pattern, naming the community "New Jerusalem," "the perfection of perfection." The inner voice questions motives ("you're doing it just to appear in a favorable light"): "the vicious circle of self-redemption." [p.48]
- **Double life (immature ONEs).** Correct in public; the repressed shadow (e.g., sexual wishes) emerges when unobserved or in a foreign setting; the televangelist scandals; "Unhealthy ONEs are hypocrites" (John 8:7 stone-casting). [p.49]
- **Gift / fruit of the spirit: cheerful tranquility.** The gift "is always the reverse of the root sin." ONEs who discover their anger can eventually deal with it better and more constructively than others; it does not go away but need not be taken so seriously. Three things said to help integrated ONEs: **prayer, love, nature** (Col. 3:14 love as bond of perfection; fall in love daily "even if it's only a tree"; nature lovers, gardens, ecology movement). Without nature, love and God they stay aggressive idealists and ideologues. [p.49]
- **Other gifts.** Rational, just, balanced; good teachers or pastors, or bookkeepers; fair and look at the other side because many voices have already tried the case internally, so their opinion has "gone through the fire of their internal criticism" and "it's hard to refute ONEs." Prophets of conversion and renewal who keep awake the memory of past guilt (a strength that can make them "awful pests"). [p.49]
- **Shadow and difficulties.** Immature ONEs are "moral prigs," speak "with upraised index finger," identify themselves with their ideals; "All ONEs live close to the edge of self-righteousness." They have a hard time making important decisions because they might make mistakes; hesitate; stuck on old mistakes. [p.49]
- **Pitfall: hypersensitivity** (perceiving every flaw; beam and mote, Matt. 7:3-5). Neighbors feel criticized even when nothing is said; people say they fear "you're secretly condemning me." Remedy: build a relationship of trust so criticism can serve the other's joy. [p.49-50]
- **White-knight image.** The secret pleasure of "wiping out evil root and branch"; patrons St. George and Archangel Michael (dragon-slayers). [p.50]
- **In relationships.** Fall in love with a seemingly perfect person; when "the lacquer starts peeling," carp in order to change the partner. Forgive generously if the other confesses, begs forgiveness and proves change, but "seldom completely unconditional." They may keep a list of others' mistakes: "They can forgive, but they're bad at forgetting." Anger is the energy source and the perception of self; when ONEs smolder and overidentify with the anger, others stop taking them seriously. [p.50]

### Symbols and Examples [p.50-51]
- **Animal:** the yelping terrier (unredeemed). **Ants and bees** (industriousness; bees test all flowers and keep only the best, honey).
- **Nation:** Switzerland (order, cleanliness, precision watches, strict neutrality "both sides are right!", flawless defense; but skeletons in the closet).
- **Color:** silver (cool, sober, clear; moonlight borrowed from the sun, i.e., the highest ideal; redeemed ONE stands for change and growth).
- **Literature:** Lucy van Pelt of *Peanuts* (caricature; Linus's "At least you have a little brother who loves you" makes her sob; "The ONE longs for unconditional love but cannot believe it when it comes").
- **History/Bible (examples):** Martin Luther (angry young man longing for an unconditionally loving God; strict earthly father and wrathful heavenly father; Erikson's reading of the Reformation as a ONE's entanglement; "tower experience" perhaps on the toilet; temptations "libido, ira, impatientia"); the apostle Paul (a Pharisee, "ONEs are born Pharisees"; zealot transformed; opposed Peter, "a SIX," at Antioch, Gal. 2:11; sarcastic when attacked); Luther's "Here I stand." "In both men we see how closely root sin and fruit of the spirit are fused together." [p.50-51] **[Christian framing]** for all biblical and church-history examples.

### Conversion and Maturation [p.51-53]
- **What a ONE must learn.** There is not just one right way ("many roads lead to Rome"); make friends with anger and acknowledge it before judging; unredeemed ONEs project negative feelings onto the first person nearby; unacknowledged anger is taken out on children, spouse or an untidy house. "The greatest freedom of ONEs lies in being able to laugh at themselves." [p.51]
- **Rohr's motives, honest.** His ONE energy drove community-building and being available seven days a week; "God's humble realism": God knows "the most he will get from us will be a 'mustard seed' of faith." "One can build only on the truth." [p.51-52] **[Christian framing]**
- **Stop wanting all or nothing.** They cannot create perfection alone; dependent on others' patience (and God's). [p.52]
- **The invitation: "growth."** Their love of nature hints that seeing things grow is good; "Whatever grows is not yet perfect, but it's on the way" (Mark 4:26-29, the seed growing by itself). **[Christian framing]** "A ONE who allows growth shares in God's tranquility." [p.52]
- **Transformed anger = righteous indignation.** The Bible shows God's anger over injustice; holy wrath (cleansing of the Temple; Paul at Athens; Luther on indulgences). "Holy anger is an expression of love ... It never wants to destroy, but to restore the original justice." [p.52]
- **Saint example: Ignatius Loyola** (1491-1556): Basque knight wounded at Pamplona; conversion through reading saints' legends; pilgrim; Spiritual Exercises; Jesuits approved 1540. "No overlooking the energy of the ONE in Ignatian spirituality": discernment of spirits, thorough self-examination, readiness to submit to the work; Christ as continual partner prevents it from happening "in a vacuum." [p.52]
- **Life task.** Occasionally ignore duty, order, world improvement and "play, celebrate, and enjoy life"; dismantle judgmentalism and resentment and take back projections, then compassion is possible; "go to school with the lighthearted SEVENs" (cheerful *joie de vivre*). [p.52]
- **Second example: Karl Barth** (Swiss Reformed theologian, "a ONE"): his Romans commentary and *Church Dogmatics*; yet an obsessive Mozart devotee ("Mozart is a typical SEVEN"), playing Mozart every morning before dogmatics. Thomas Merton's dream-essay on Barth (Barth dreams of examining Mozart, who says nothing) is quoted at length; the point: "There is in us a Mozart who will be our salvation." [p.52-53]

## TYPE TWO: The Need to Be Needed [p.54-63]

### Overview [p.54-55]
- **Gifts.** TWOs use their gifts for the needs of others, their health, nourishment, education, welfare; they give acceptance and appreciation that help others believe in their own value; they "give their last shirt"; stand by others in suffering. Their love "also has dark sides." [p.54]
- **Motivation.** "TWOs desperately want to be liked and have an exaggerated need for validation." [p.54]
- **Childhood.** Many remember a gray, sad childhood, lacking real security or home; others report only conditional love bought by good behavior (the "beautiful" childhood that prevents rage). Some had to support the emotional needs of family members. The key mechanism is **"parentizing"** (Bert Hellinger): the child "mothers" the adults and denies its own needs; the message: "I am loved when I am tender, understanding, and ready to be helpful, and defer my own needs." The child gets a "greatness" it is not entitled to and feels powerful while the adults look weak: "fertile soil for the sort of false pride that is the root sin." They "secretly look down on those whom they 'serve.'" "For TWOs 'being good' is an emotional, not a moral, category." [p.54]
- **Hellinger on equilibrium.** Every system presses toward balance of giving and taking; those who give too much burden a community as much as those who only take (pattern FIVE); the givers eventually demand repayment; the "takers" become aggressive toward the eternal givers who forced them into debt. Classic image: the "Jewish mother" caricature: "How could you do that to me, after all I've done for you?" An unredeemed TWO spoils others unasked; if others pull away, the TWO feels betrayed and exploited. [p.54]
- **Everyday examples.** Priests' housekeepers; the pastor's cook or minister's wife; Rohr's secretary at New Jerusalem, a "classic TWO" who ruled the community by competence, kept all details, made herself indispensable; "she controlled me and kept me in line." [p.54-55]
- **Heart-type mechanism.** TWOs hold "the thermometer in the air" to measure social temperature; identity rests on how others react. Heart types (TWO, THREE, FOUR) are "other-directed." [p.55]
- **The magic formula.** Immature TWOs emit a subtle energy: "Notice me, stroke me," but the real formula is "Need me." They are themselves manipulable: say "I need you" and resistance fades; they then go home and kick themselves for being exploited. [p.55]
- **Style.** Cry easily; teddy bears who cuddle; like to talk about relationships and love; benefactors, givers, helpers; "our social network would collapse" without them. They must resist styling themselves as saints and martyrs. Reference to Schmidbauer's "helper syndrome" (*The Helpless Helpers*). [p.55]
- **Multiple self.** Immature TWOs change continually to meet whoever is present (Palmer's "multiple self"); prefer one other person; confused with several close people present; see the variety as enrichment. Large circle of acquaintances, quick to say "friends," guard relationships jealously, want to be especially important, proud that people confide in them, physically sense others' needs, quick to give advice. [p.55]

### Dilemma [p.55-59]
- **Temptation.** "Continually to help others and in this way to evade themselves." Identity lies outside, in others' wishes and needs; chaotic emotional life; no center; when alone, "the ceiling falls on their head"; quiet prayer makes them anxious because they fear finding "a black hole or alarming unrest." [p.55]
- **Seduction.** A tendency to seduce; in neurotic cases to abuse; the helplessness of children can appeal (often making a helpless child a substitute object of their own needs); "basically they love only themselves"; "they give others precisely what they want for themselves." A homeless child within them; "Whoever can help must have power. Rule by serving!" [p.55-56]
- **Fusion and matchmaking.** Long for fusion but experience it outside themselves; match people up; subtly prevent contacts when they fear losing someone; read love novels; chat online to nourish the illusion of contact. [p.56]
- **Maturity.** Mature TWOs love without conditions or hidden agendas; the line between love of neighbor and manipulative helper complex is tricky. A deaconess motto (Wilhelm Löhe, "I serve neither for reward nor for thanks") is called "poison for TWOs." [p.56]
- **Partnership.** Possessive; often choose weak or dependent partners; classic case: a female TWO and an addict (co-dependence): she keeps him dependent, because if he were well he might leave. [p.56]
- **When hurt.** Stop being sweet and "show their claws"; can "turn into Furies"; a quoted revolution line: "Be my brother! And if you resist / I'll smash your skull to bloody bits!" [p.56]
- **Pride in giving but not receiving.** "They may be everybody's garbage disposal, but they shy away from really depending on others." Behind this: shame at showing neediness, fear of rejection, "nobody likes me anyway." High threshold anxiety before confession, counseling, therapy; surrender only when sure of acceptance; need at least one trusted person to tell everything; sharp criticism pulls the rug out; need much "soft love" before "hard love." [p.56]
- **Gender note.** More women than men in group TWO because society has encouraged women to be TWOs (idealized "intuition" and "devotion"; power through "loving"). Robin Norwood's *Women Who Love Too Much*; consuming oneself for a man, bulimia. [p.57] **[Christian framing]** The Gospel is often preached as if everyone should become a TWO; Sufis reportedly considered Jesus a "redeemed TWO"; two-thirds of churchgoers are women, so TWO mechanisms are reinforced in church circles. [p.57]
- **Root sin: pride.** "Pride is different from conceit or narcissism. Pride is an expression of a 'puffed-up self,' an 'inflationary ego.'" Messianic self-perception: "I'm more loving and sensitive than all of you; my love will save the world ... I will make my love so indispensable ... that you won't be able to get along without me." They manipulate by attention and considerateness. Root: parentization. Hellinger's resolution: the child bows before the parents and says, "I am only the child; I'm the little one and you're big" (an infinite relief). [p.57]
- **Obstacle to self-knowledge.** Knowing their sin would mean seeing their pride, which blocks the seeing. They need an "objective inner observer." They don't "need God" (they are loving and energetic themselves); they believe God needs them. "We TWOs are practical atheists" (a young TWO theologian: "I once found myself praying, 'Lord, I have mercy on you'!"). They expect gratitude from everyone, including God, and gratitude toward life comes hard; they block themselves off from true joy. [p.57] **[Christian framing]**
- **Avoidance: suppress own needs and project them onto others.** The pressure turned against themselves becomes subtle pressure on others; "ONEs hide their anger; TWOs hide the fact that they are so needy." The golden rule at face value (Matt. 7:12) is "poison for TWOs." [p.57]
- **Needs and substitutes.** Needs are sensory-emotional (tenderness, sex, attachment); replacements: eating, drinking, shopping, chocolate; "I've earned this" in the evening; many weight problems from unrequited love; "No one longs for love as much as a TWO. And no one is as incapable of relationships as a neurotic TWO." [p.58]
- **Defense mechanism: repression** (aggression and sexuality). It is hard to say "You're driving me crazy" or "You turn me on." They show moods indirectly; an offended TWO can poison a group atmosphere without a nasty word and answer "Nothing's wrong!" [p.58]
- **Pitfall: obligingness or flattery.** They deny themselves to please. Then comes the other extreme: sudden independence, fighting "with tooth and nail for their 'freedom'," a bolt from the blue that appears in every TWO at some point. They can't say no, promise more than they can keep, then feel irritated and guilty. [p.58]
- **Leadership.** Compulsion to be used (by the poor or a great figure); can be good leaders if they curb partiality and favorites; gather a circle of "understanding" disciples; fear of loss holds people "by an invisible leash"; find leadership burdensome and exposed; one critic can feel like "everybody's against me." [p.58]
- **Gift / fruit of the spirit: humility.** The reverse of pride. When a TWO recognizes the real motive ("I give so I can get"), there comes "a sobering more profound than can be imagined"; if endured ("chew it, taste it, digest it"), transformation is possible. Illustration: a woman at New Jerusalem wept three days at her office hour over her pride and her belief that she was the most lovable person; "a real conversion." Caution: "the tears of TWOs are often tears of self-pity"; redemption is near when they cry "tears of self-knowledge." [p.58] **[Christian framing]** (tears of repentance in Eastern Orthodoxy; experiencing God as "the real lover" whose love we only share.)
- **False vs. genuine humility.** "False pride and false humility are twins." Genuine humility = realistic self-appraisal and healthy self-worth; false humility is "restored and 'sanctified' pride." Redeemed TWOs know their value, no longer need constant reinforcement, and their autonomy "is now no longer an act of defiance." [p.59]

### Symbols and Examples [p.59-60]
- **Animals:** cat (ambivalence between distance and closeness; can't be trained), donkey (patient beast of burden who goes on strike; Jesus on an ass), licking puppy (urgent proofs of love that become sticky).
- **Nation:** Italy (the caricature of the fat Italian mamma who rules the family; the effort to appear warm, lovable, charming; giving directions by grabbing your arm, "Over there!", with "image more important than the facts").
- **Color:** red (life, power, passion; blood; Hebrew *dam*, *adamah*, *adam*; love and martyrdom; fire and the Holy Spirit; also Mars, bullfighters, revolution).
- **Bible:** Mary Magdalen, Martha (served resentfully, "Do you not care that my sister has left me to serve alone?"; Luke 10:38-42), and John the beloved disciple (lay close to Jesus; stayed at the cross; themes of love and incarnation; yet his TWO shadow: bid for the best seat, "bid fire come down from heaven" on a village, exclusive "brother" love, sharp inside/outside line, no love of enemy; the authors tie this to the roots of Christian anti-Semitism and quote Nazi use of John 8:44). The authors note it is significant that with TWO one thinks first of women figures. [p.59-60] **[Christian framing]**

### Conversion and Maturation [p.60-63]
- **The invitation: freedom.** Freedom ends the game of manipulation and false love and of "violent attempts at self-liberation." Reached only through experience of unconditional love ("grace"); a sign is **gratitude**. Redeemed TWOs rejoice at small signs of attention, set others free, and are glad when people go their own way. [p.60]
- **Lifelong tasks.** Achieve objectivity; free themselves from gossip, flattery, false intimacy, sentimentalism, the quest for reinforcement; practice **unobtrusive service** ("Can I do something for others that does not get noticed and rewarded?"; Matt. 6:3). TWOs "do good and talk about it." Deep, painful **experiences of loss** are usually needed to cut the dependency on confirmation; grief work to sever symbiotic relationships. [p.60]
- **Head work.** Like all heart types they need silence and objectivity, to be alone and "think with their heads"; in aggressive phases they can switch off the head ("Stop getting on my nerves with the facts"). [p.61]
- **Hypersensitivity.** They read eyebrows like weather; hurt by hints of rejection; need patience but also repeated challenge not to confuse feelings with objective reality. [p.61]
- **Two warning signals: shame** (about their own needs) and **the need to assign blame** (to others or God). "TWOs can hate as intensely as they can love"; become "uncommonly cruel and brutal toward themselves and others"; "that is the most terrible deformation of the TWOs." [p.61]
- **Say no and name needs.** Practice saying no and formulating needs; at first it is exaggerated and combative; others must be patient (Schellenbaum on symbiotic relationships). They are best at loving and serving, yet that is where manipulation also appears; so they need "supervision" to build an inner observer who asks "Why are you really there for others?" and to turn off the fuel supply sometimes; motivation drops at first when they give without reward. Burnout is "false motives ... getting their revenge" (Bronsberg and Vestlund). [p.61]
- **The redeemed TWO.** "A tremendous beloved, a wonderful lover, an enviable friend"; feel your pains and care because they know the pain of relationships and loneliness; want to spare others what they went through. [p.61]
- **Example: Mother Teresa.** First edition cited her as an integrated TWO; the authors now believe she was an EIGHT. Explanation: integrated EIGHTs take on the positive features of mature TWOs; **"TWO is the 'consolation point' of EIGHT"** (to be examined later). They summarize her life work (Skopje; Loreto/"English Ladies"; teaching in Calcutta; leaving the convent in 1946 to live among the poor; the Missionaries of Charity; care of abandoned children, hospices for the dying; "The only thing that really converts is love"; "Don't count the cost"; strength drawn from silence and Eucharist; action and contemplation together). Quoted statements include her opposition to abortion and her asylum appeal to Lothar Späth. [p.61-62] **[Christian framing]**
- **Better "saint" examples for TWO.** Florence Nightingale (1820-1910; avoided public receptions, worked silently; organization of nursing in the Crimean War; discipline, ambition, commitment to the limits of her strength) and Vincent de Paul (1581-; charity foundations, lived in extreme wretchedness; "We have to beg poor people's pardon for all the things we do for them," "the knowledge of a mature TWO"). The authors admit they have been looking for new TWO saints "not too successfully," suggesting that a start from "sham saintliness" makes genuine sainthood harder. [p.62-63]

## TYPE THREE: The Need to Succeed [p.64-66, range ends mid-chapter]

### Overview [p.64-65]
- **Gifts.** Ease and assurance that inspires confidence; a good atmosphere; efficient, competent job completion; reach personal goals and inspire and motivate others. A "sixth sense" for tasks and work-group dynamics; identify with the firm, organization or community; create a good business climate; network the members; charisma and argument carry projects to success. [p.64]
- **Position in the heart group.** THREE is "the central type of the heart group," but they have "the greatest difficulties of all the Enneagram types in perceiving their own feelings." Like TWOs they hold an imaginary thermometer, but they ask "Am I successful? Am I getting across?" ("How'm I doin'?", Ed Koch). [p.64]
- **Childhood.** Loved not for themselves but praised and rewarded for success ("You're a good boy. We're proud of you."); motto: **"I'm good when I win."** They draw life energy from success; "show-people, achievers, careerists, status-seekers," who handle each role better than their true self, which they "scarcely know"; slip into any mask; role protects and motivates; "The life of THREEs is a competitive struggle." A THREE woman's self-description: loved winning at mental arithmetic, bored by music where she could not be among the best, liked grading systems that "helped me to define myself"; sees herself as someone who gets ahead on the team; impatient with stragglers; prefers to work alone at her own tempo. [p.64]
- **Appearance and work.** Hard workers; often seem more competent than they are and convince others of their cause. Many physically attractive; "super-kids" told "You can do it"; often self-fulfilling; optimistic, youthful, intelligent, dynamic, productive. Careers: agents, salespeople, managers, designers, media and advertising; "super-housewives and super-mothers." In intimacy they perform roles skillfully (romantic when romance is called for). [p.64-65]
- **Chameleon quality.** They become the prototype of whatever group they are in, adopting the current social definition of masculinity and femininity. Group identification need not be mainstream (e.g., a Christian community or a radical group): the THREE embodies the values of the new primary group. If they belong to several groups they change image and role "with lightning speed" at the threshold. Rohr's friend "Mr. Perfect" (a THREE from Cincinnati): "When I walk into a room ... I know in fractions of a second how I have to behave ... If I leave the room and go one door down, then I can play the same game and be a completely different person." [p.65]
- **Effortless look.** They work hard but want it to look easy; do not show effort. They send out "commercials" for themselves, count influenced people, projects, distinctions. "THREEs cannot be praised enough. They suck up endorsements like a dried-up sponge," yet praise often doesn't come because others see them as self-assured. "Praise is the gas that makes the THREE's motor go. THREEs are even more dependent on the reactions of other people than TWOs are, though it's seldom noticed." [p.65]

### Dilemma (first part) [p.65-66]
- **Temptation: efficiency.** The capitalist system rests on the "THREE dogma" ("Those who exert themselves enough can work their way up"); the United States is the symbolic country of THREEs (the authors apply the pattern to American society: "We admire winners and despise losers," blaming the victim, the poor as responsible for their situation; the "American Gospel" of achievement, affluence and success). Religion becomes "a sort of spiritual consumer product." **[Christian framing]** The cross, "a symbol of failure," no longer plays any role; the middle class has "bought our way out of the experience of failure." [p.65-66]
- **Defense mechanism: identification.** They protect themselves by becoming fully involved in their projects, are reluctant to accept criticism of their group or company. Example: a fellow Franciscan who was a THREE "sold" New Jerusalem to a diocesan commission so beautifully that a priest said, "Father, you're trying too hard. It can't be that good." THREEs believe wholeheartedly and "completely smooth out the shadow side, because 'shadow' is the same as 'failure.'" [p.66]
- **Avoidance: failure.** "There is nothing more tragic than an unsuccessful THREE." Unredeemed THREEs "avoid, fear, and hate defeat like the plague." Three standard ways out: polishing defeats into "partial victories," shifting responsibility to others, leaving the scene quickly for a new promising project. Overestimate themselves; spoiled by success, they believe everything they do is great. [p.66]
- **Root sin: untruth or deceit.** The pressure to succeed leads to it; to win, THREEs "deal generously with the truth": an image that looks good, can be sold, and wins. "These are seldom bald-faced lies: rather they are the subtle nuancing, the airbrushing out of the problematic side of a project, the exaggerated stress on advantages." [p.66]
- **Deceit and fear as "missing" sins.** Deceit (THREE) and fear (SIX) are lacking in the classic seven; the authors say their classification as sins derives from the Sufi tradition; the West "never unmasked and named these sins"; they are "the deadly sins of our society, which are all the more dangerous because we don't see them." Sufis assume one cannot recognize one's own sin. Dante is the exception: the "cowards" (SIXes) at the entrance of Hell (canto III) and "forgers and traitors" in the deepest circle (cantos XXIX to XXXIV: Judas, Brutus, Cassius, frozen in the ninth circle). [p.66]
- **Self-deception.** "Unredeemed THREEs first and foremost deceive themselves"; they convince themselves the lie is true; example given: an American politician before the press microphones (the authors cite Bill Clinton's handling of the Lewinsky affair: redefining "sexual relationship"). "Immature THREEs have no longing for depth ... image without content sells"; "whatever works is true"; the question of objective truth isn't raised. The text continues with M. Scott Peck's *The People of the Lie* just beyond the end of this range. [p.66]
- **Not in this range (continues in the next part):** the remainder of THREE's Dilemma (the gift/fruit of the spirit, pitfall, and so on), Symbols and Examples, Conversion and Maturation; then Types FOUR through NINE, and Part III (including wings, arrows or integration and disintegration, levels, "Triple Continuum"). The wings are announced as "an important phenomenon" to be addressed "in more detail in connection with the nine sketches." [p.40]

## The Christian-theological layer (collected so it can be used or ignored)

All of the following is theological framing, not description of the typology:
- The doctrine of original sin as the reason for the childhood damage; the Preface to Part II's "soul child" as original sin and the Kabbalistic nine qualities of God with SOPHIA as the tenth. [p.20, 43-45]
- Grace and redemption as the means of getting free from the false self; "dynamis" and the Holy Spirit; the Indwelling Voice; discernment of spirits (Ignatian method). [p.11, 16, 30-31, 36, 41]
- Reading "sin" as separation from God and "root sins" as blockages; Chaucer's remedies and the nine fruits of the spirit in Galatians 5:22 as the types' "gifts." [p.37-38]
- Biblical and church-history figures offered as type examples: Paul (ONE), Peter (SIX), Luther (ONE), Calvin (ONE), Ignatius (ONE), Barth (ONE), Mary Magdalen, Martha and John (TWO), Jesus as "redeemed TWO" (reported Sufi view), Nightingale and Vincent de Paul (TWO), Mother Teresa (EIGHT, with TWO qualities), Mozart (SEVEN). [p.50-63]
- Prayer and contemplation prescriptions (meditation for ONEs; Matt. 6:3 and 6:6 for heart types; cross and failure for THREEs); the Church "baptizing" outside models; interreligious dialogue (Lull). [p.11, 26, 39, 48, 60, 66]
- Rohr's essay on religion as transformation rather than belonging. [p.13-17]

## Gaps and cautions

- **Range ends mid-chapter.** Type THREE is incomplete (no gift/fruit, pitfall, symbols or conversion section; the text breaks at the start of a reference to M. Scott Peck, p.66-67). Types FOUR to NINE and Part III are in the next parts.
- **No wings, arrows, levels in detail yet.** In this range wings are mentioned only generally (neighbors; "every type also contains traits of both its neighbors," p.40, 41) and the "consolation point" is mentioned once (TWO as EIGHT's, p.61). The soul-child pairings in the Preface to Part II (p.43-44) are my reading as relationships between types; the text itself does not call them arrows or integration and disintegration. The triangle and star are described only geometrically (p.20).
- **The authors' own use of numbers.** They use numerals as names, deliberately, and capitalize the words ("ONE," "TWO"); this extraction follows that.
- **Caricature and cultural stereotypes.** The authors' national symbols (Switzerland, Italy, USA), "Jewish mother," and gender claims (for TWO) are stereotypes the authors flag as playful or "subjective"; they were recorded as the book's own devices, not endorsed. The John-and-anti-Semitism passage (p.60) is the authors' interpretation; recorded in brief.
- **Historical claims.** The Evagrius and Lull accounts are the authors' own argument (and they themselves say the "Enneagram of fixations" is Ichazo's twentieth-century creation); a few claims (Sufis' "face of God," Sufi view of Jesus as a "redeemed TWO") are given as reported ("supposedly," "reportedly").
- **Mother Teresa.** The authors revised their first-edition claim (TWO) to EIGHT; both statements appear.
- **Omitted for length:** the long Merton quotation and Polanyi/Hoffer detail in Rohr's preface (summarized); Lull's Latin terms (summarized); full Luther/Erikson biographical detail (summarized); the Nightingale and Vincent biographies (summarized to a line); endnote text (not extracted; notes are at the end of the book, outside this range); the Hellinger and Schellenbaum references (named only).
- **Source quality.** No OCR garbling found in this range. The Lull figures A and T and the Enneagram figure (Fig. 1) are images and are not in the text; the "Figure" labels at p.21, 24-25, 27-28 contain only captions.
- **Dates and attributions to verify before reuse:** Evagrius's dates (345-399), Lull's conversion year (1263; some sources give other dates), Ichazo's chronology, and the 1989 first-edition claims; all are as stated in the book.
