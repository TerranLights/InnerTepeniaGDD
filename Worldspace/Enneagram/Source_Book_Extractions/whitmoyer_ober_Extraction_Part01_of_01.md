## Overview

A popular-level, friendly, anecdotal book. Its argument: the Enneagram is not a personality test but a map of the *motivations* behind behavior, so it can improve how we relate to colleagues, friends, romantic partners and family. Each of Chapters 2 to 10 treats one type with a fixed template: core fear and desire, character traits, common mistypes, childhood, worldview, strengths and weaknesses, conflict style, famous examples, wings, three "words" that characterize the type in relationships, then four relationship settings (colleagues, friends, romantic, family), a "dating" box, and closing tips ("Five Tips to Be Your Best Self in the Real World" and "Practical Relationship Strategies"). Each setting is illustrated by a short anecdote about a named person (often a pairing of two types). The book states there are no "more compatible" types, only healthier and unhealthier states of each [s.12]. It does not supply a type-by-type compatibility chart; pairing material appears only through anecdotes (collected in a table near the end of this file).

## Introduction [s.8]

- Author's route: psychology BA, community psychology and social change MA, then Enneagram coaching with Beth McCord [s.8].
- Framing claim: Myers-Briggs "tells us who we are; the Enneagram tells us the motivations behind what we do." Example: three people avoiding eye contact in a corner at a party may each have a different motivation [s.8].
- Quote she uses (Ian Morgan Cron): "The Enneagram doesn't put you in a box. It shows you the box you're already in and how to get out of it." [s.8]
- Promise of the book: how to be in healthy relationships with each type, in work, friendship, romantic and family relationships, plus background on strengths and weaknesses and childhood tendencies [s.8].
- Disclaimer: not a crystal ball or cure-all; seek professional help for chronic stress or abusive or unhealthy relationships [s.8].
- Instruction to read the whole book, not just your own or your partner's chapter [s.8].

## Chapter 1: An Introduction to the Enneagram [s.11-12]

**What it is.** An "ancient typing system" rooted in psychology, spirituality and self-observation, with "Asian and Middle Eastern roots dating back 2,000 to 4,000 years"; a model of the human psyche with nine types that finds motivations, not just tendencies [s.12].

**What it isn't.** Not the same category as MBTI or StrengthsFinder 2.0. Traditional tests describe who we are; the Enneagram explores the why and how, and offers growth through behavior and experience [s.12].

**Brief history (as the author gives it)** [s.12]:
- Introduced to the United States by researchers in the 1960s; more popular in the 1980s; mass popularity in the last decade, credited largely to technology and social media.
- Some experts trace it to Sufism; others see a link to Christianity, specifically the seven deadly sins; described as religious in origin and now widely secular, used toward self-actualization.
- George Gurdjieff (Russia) met it traveling in Afghanistan in the 1920s; compared it to a dance and called himself a "dance teacher."
- Oscar Ichazo, "credited with putting the modern Enneagram system together," taught it at his Arica School in South America in the 1960s; Claudio Naranjo (American psychiatrist, trained with Ichazo in Chile) first brought it to the United States.
- Don Richard Riso and Russ Hudson: introduced to it in 1975, opened The Enneagram Institute in New York in 1997, developed the RHETI (Riso-Hudson Enneagram Type Indicator). Other named teachers: Beatrice Chestnut, Ginger Lapid-Bogda, Beth McCord, David Daniels, Jerry Wagner.

**The figure.** "Enneagram" from Greek *ennea* (nine) and *gram* (written or drawn). Three parts [s.12]:
- Outer circle: the oneness of life.
- Triangle: three separate sources; the author reads these as the **triads** (fear, anger, shame).
- Hexad (six-pointed figure): how we move through life; its lines are the "integration/disintegration arrows or stress/growth arrows," showing where each type goes under stress and in growth.

**Centers of intelligence.** Called centers because they are "always at the center of our biggest assets and greatest liabilities" [s.12]:

| Center | Types | Best and worst qualities come from | Associated emotion (as the author states it) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instinctive | Eight, Nine, One | instinctual drives | anger ("anger triad") |
| Feeling | Two, Three, Four | feelings | shame ("shame triad") |
| Thinking | Five, Six, Seven | thought processes and thinking patterns | fear ("fear triad") |

**Why nine types (core point, outer focus, inner focus)** [s.12]: the corner points Three, Six and Nine form the inner triangle. Each triad has a core point plus an outer focus and an inner focus:

| Center / triad | Core point | Outer focus | Inner focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling / shame | Three | Two | Four |
| Thinking / fear | Six | Five | Seven |
| Instinctive / anger | Nine | Eight | One |

**Harmony triads** (three groupings, called here the optimistic, competency and intensity triads) [s.12]:

| Triad | Types | Shared trait as stated |
|---|---|---|
| Optimistic | Seven, Nine, Two | positive view of life, most optimistic types; Seven is "the topmost optimistic number" |
| Competency | One, Three, Five | value being competent and being seen as competent. One: competence means being right; Three: knowing how to achieve the success they desire; Five: deep understanding |
| Intensity | Four, Six, Eight | most intense types, each differently: Four emotionally; Six from a worried, doubt-filled mind; Eight is felt by others in their presence |

**Wings** [s.12]: the numbers directly adjacent to your type; characteristics of the wings feed into the personality. A wing must be adjacent (not "second-highest test score" or "the type you resonate with next"). You need not have a wing; some people feel they do not use either; usually one wing is more dominant. Growth work includes incorporating healthy qualities of the wings; using both wings allows "substantial personal growth." Example given: a One can draw on Nine and/or Two. Later chapters use a paint-color metaphor (same type = same color, many shades) [s.15].

**The nine types in one-line form** [s.12]:

| Type | Name | One-line motivation as stated |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reformer | structured, practical, balanced; desire to be good; black-and-white view |
| 2 | Helper | giving of time, energy, resources; need to be wanted and loved |
| 3 | Achiever | goal-oriented, hardworking, competitive; desire to be seen as valuable |
| 4 | Individualist | artistic, emotional, creative; need to be seen as unique and unlike anyone else |
| 5 | Investigator | intellectual, withdrawn, independent; need to be seen as competent |
| 6 | Loyalist | committed, responsible, anxious; need to feel secure in relationships and environment |
| 7 | Enthusiast | spontaneous, scattered, seeks adventure; need to feel satisfied and content; biggest fear of missing out |
| 8 | Challenger | strong, assertive, resourceful; desire to protect themselves and not be seen as weak or vulnerable |
| 9 | Peacemaker | calm, conflict-averse, open-minded; desire to maintain peace and wholeness |

**Compatibility** [s.12]: "anyone can be compatible with anyone else if they are living within a healthy state of their type," and any type can be incompatible when unhealthy. Some pairings are more common, but "there are never types that are more compatible than others." Advice: become your best self before judging compatibility.

**Finding your type** [s.12]: the book has no test. Recommends a test as a start (RHETI; Beth McCord's free test at YourEnneagramCoach.com for a faith-based view), then reflection on biggest fears and desires, ideally reviewed with a trained coach.

**The biggest temptation** [s.12]: never type other people yourself; motivation is invisible (an assertive friend may be a Three wanting to be valued, not an Eight afraid of weakness). Never type a child or tell a child they act like a type (they may perform it for approval). Do not put people in boxes. The author lists famous people by type because she is trained to speculate from public behavior.

## Chapter 2: Type One, The Reformer [s.14-15]

**Names.** Reformer; also "The Perfectionist" among some experts [s.14].
**Core.** Knowing right from wrong, structure in their world ("whatever is 'right' to them"). **Fear:** being corrupt or "bad." **Desire:** to be good and have integrity [s.14].
**Healthy qualities:** efficiency, productivity, honesty, orderliness, practicality, strong work ethic. **Under stress/unhealthy:** judgmental, obsessive, critical of self and others, controlling, high strung [s.14].
**Trait list given:** principled, perfectionist, impatient, wise, realistic, competent, rule-follower, ambitious, practical, independent [s.15].

**Meaning of the name.** A reformer makes changes to improve something; Ones try to improve everything, including themselves, others, environment and relationships, which can cause trouble when no one asked for help, or when they judge people whose lives differ [s.15].

**Common mistypes** [s.15]: general organized or rule-following people; extreme planners. Real confusions: Six (both loyal and worried; Sixes are not big risk-takers, Ones will take risks to improve the world), Eight (both feel it is up to them to create change; Eights struggle with vulnerability, Ones are quick to be vulnerable with those closest), Three (both achievement-driven; Threes care about image, Ones about doing what is right).

**Childhood** [s.15]: criticize themselves when imperfect; strive for perfection in projects, academics, sports; seem competitive but mostly compete with themselves; feel they cannot meet caregivers' and friends' expectations; may struggle to finish things because not "perfect" enough; often play "parent" to friends or younger siblings; extreme rule-followers who keep others in line; guilt or anger at themselves when they break a rule (no longer "good").

**Worldview.** The world is flawed and it is their job to correct it (injustice, recycling, putting the toilet paper roll on "correctly") [s.15].

**Strengths** [s.15]: loyal, dependable; make a plan and stick to it; accomplish tasks big and small; want to be their best so they bring out the best in others.
**Weaknesses** [s.15]: critical inner voice constantly running; judgmental of others who are not "as perfect"; unreasonably high expectations; the anger-triad emotion comes out when expectations are unmet, or turns inward into depression and withdrawal "much like the unhealthy qualities of an Enneagram Four"; the constant need to "do" (cannot stop to breathe or put off mopping a day).

**Conflict.** Head-on, to move on quickly; they will say when you hurt their feelings [s.15].

**Famous Ones** [s.15]: Julie Andrews (self-proclaimed perfectionist); Kate Middleton (charity, children's causes); Mary Poppins (fictional nanny who adds structure to chaos). Recognition cue: a famous One has a mission or purpose (politicians with a platform, actors with a cause).

**Wings** [s.15]: Two wing: more giving and helpful, makes sure others' needs are met, may struggle more with caring what others think. Nine wing: focused on a peaceful environment; more passive-aggressive and less visibly angry; a bit less judgmental, more understanding.

**Three words in relationships:** order, dedication, responsibility [s.15].
- *Order:* beyond tidiness. You will not wonder where things are, and the relationship itself is "well-thought-out and structured"; Ones do not begin relationships without thinking them through.
- *Dedication:* 100 percent once in; dedicated to everyone's needs met, problems resolved, the relationship growing. Illustrated by the author's friend Molly (One): never late, sees right and wrong with no in-between, longest-lasting friendships.
- *Responsibility:* feel responsible for the relationship and for the people in it; thrive on demonstrating truth; "brutally honest," assume their opinions are facts (the person to ask about an outfit, a date, a career move).
- *Trouble spots:* critical and judgmental when friends or partners do not do things their way, often unaware; so used to feeling judged that they judge; unmet expectations lead to resentment, which brings out negative Four characteristics; effective communication is key.

**Colleagues** [s.15]: attention to detail, work ethic, rule-following; supervisors love their reliability. Conflict risk: love control, so clash with other would-be controllers; may take over projects because they expect to be let down; under stress a One "will shut down and withdraw," leaving problems unresolved and breeding resentment.
- *Anecdote:* Susan (One) and Linda (Eight) on a joint project; both want to lead, both are anger-triad, Susan could not accept Linda doing tasks differently; harsh words exchanged. Author reads it as a control contest ("who the alpha was") plus anger-triad gut reactions [s.15].

**Friends** [s.15]: among the most loyal; reliable, intentional with your time; the honest opinion friend (outfit, new partner, hair color); direct because they think silence would be mean; frustrated by lateness and rule-breaking; "always in your corner." Anecdote: Molly (One) and the author (Two), friends since high school; Molly was older, drove, kept the group in line (homework, school, still fun), and kept the group connected across colleges: "The glue that keeps people together."

**Romance** [s.15]: take things slowly, cautious, fearful of being criticized as much as they criticize themselves; difficulty relaxing; very loyal once relaxed. Very specific wants and "a laundry list of nonnegotiables," so they can search for a "perfect" partner and make partners feel not good enough. Remember a One is probably criticizing themselves more. With communication: respect, fairness, truthfulness; you always know where they stand; they try to keep arguments fair; household runs on a schedule.
- *Anecdote:* a man whose One partner "doesn't like to sit down": reliable and consistent but conflict arises when he does not help with chores or grasp unstated expectations; Ones believe no one will do things as they do yet still want assistance or recognition for handling everything; they want their actions perceived as "good" and "right."
- *Dating box:* structure; plan their own dates; occasional surprises only of familiar experiences; meaningful conversation; favorite restaurant, favorite games, hobby-linked activity.

**Family** [s.15]: "glue" of the family, creating structure out of chaos; usually the family leader; parents who teach ethics, responsibility and morals and follow the "rules" of parenting. Struggle with a chaotic house, burnout from going and going, resentment that others do not care about chores equally; lack of stated expectations is the cause (what is "perfect" does not necessarily mean spotless).

**Tips (real world)** [s.15]: (1) separate what you want from what you feel you should; (2) notice how anger shows (sarcasm, words) and trace its root; (3) practice doing things imperfectly; (4) put yourself in others' shoes; (5) mindfulness, yoga, Pilates, exercise.
**Relationship strategies** [s.15]: silence the self-critic; forgive and be kind to yourself; communicate encouragement instead of harsh words, soften tone; be direct when upset rather than letting anger build; do not try to change people.

## Chapter 3: Type Two, The Helper [s.18-19]

**Names.** Helper; also The Giver [s.18]. Descriptor words: Helper, Giver, Supporter, Right-hand person, Provider [s.19].
**Core.** Giving words, gifts, acts of service, resources and time, but requiring appreciation in order to "give," leading to unmet expectations, bitterness and burnout. **Desire:** to feel loved and needed. **Fear:** being unwanted in life [s.18].
**Healthy:** kind, generous, selfless. **Unhealthy:** needy, manipulative, burn out by neglecting own needs [s.19].
**Trait list:** loving, caring, enthusiastic, empathetic, people-pleasing, sentimental, unselfish, patient, overly accommodating, dependent [s.19].

**Common mistypes** [s.19]: women in general mistype as Two because of a social stigma that women must be generous and caring. Real confusions: Nine (both please; Twos to be loved and wanted, Nines to keep the peace), Seven (both bubbly; Twos fear being disliked, Sevens fear missing out), Six (both warm; Twos seek acceptance, Sixes certainty and security).

**Childhood** [s.19]: want love and wanting from authority figures; help around the house and do favors to be noticed; very sensitive to criticism from friends, family and teachers and hold it long; go along with what others want; do things to gain attention (children always wearing a bandage or always sick). Caution: type is with us from birth, so the childhood picture is about the Two's perception, not proof they were unloved.

**Worldview.** Everyone needs something and the Two has what it takes to make each person whole; responsibility to make an impact and lighten burdens [s.19].

**Strengths** [s.19]: connected to others, predict feelings, read a room, great listeners, enthusiastic, fun-loving, outgoing. **Weaknesses:** care too much what people think; neglect themselves; cannot say no at their limits; insert themselves to get validation.
**Conflict.** Dislike it (fear it makes them unloved); hard to speak up; let others address it first [s.19].

**Wings** [s.19]: One wing: more task-oriented and organized, less concerned about opinion, better at saying no. Three wing: more outgoing, comfortable in the spotlight, focused on goals and being seen as valuable and successful, cares more what others think.

**Famous Twos** [s.19]: Eleanor Roosevelt (civil rights activist); Mary Kay Ash (built her company to help women feel good in their skin); Nancy Reagan (anti-drug cause, devoted caregiver to her husband).

**Three words in relationships:** sensitivity, selflessness, affection [s.19].
- *Sensitivity:* acutely tuned to others' physical and emotional needs, but take offense at any criticism, even constructive, because criticism feels like "not wanted."
- *Selflessness:* put others first, moving them toward stability and growth; can become "too selfless," forget their own priorities, hold in their own feelings; need balance.
- *Affection:* not necessarily physical; showing others how much they mean; Twos also need to receive affection, which is central to their well-being and motivation.
- They go "all in, heart-first," and say publicly how they feel about you.

**Colleagues** [s.19]: hard to be a Two at work (people need not like you; criticism and conflict will come). Need more validation and are more dependent than other types; supervisors should encourage them to stand alone. Strong at helping, but overcommit.
- *Anecdote:* Joanna (Three) with a Two coworker; both want to be valued, but the Two wants constant praise; she says yes to everything and burns out; Joanna removes items from her list and reminds her she need not always help.

**Friends** [s.19]: easy, playful, kind, nurturing; the friend you call after a breakup; will watch your child, pet sit, drive. Unhealthy Twos can be jealous of your other friendships out of fear you will love them less; reassure them.
- *Anecdote:* Amanda (Two) with Seven friends; both outgoing and optimistic-triad, but Sevens seek novelty, last-minute plans; Amanda feels left out (unwanted). Advice: talk about it, compromise, stretch her comfort zone.

**Romance** [s.19]: attentive, ask about your day, "alarmingly good at predicting their feelings." Unhealthy: clingy, over-devoted, controlling, possessive; poor at discussing own needs so become manipulative to get partners to guess. Healthy: excellent companions.
- *Anecdote:* April (Two) with Derek (Six): she more outgoing and chaotic, he stable and quieter; she admits past manipulation and possessiveness and that Twos can smother; he needs alone time. Success needs communication, healthy boundaries, learned independence.
- *Dating box:* Twos are not used to being cared for; they want dates about being together; listen to what they want; "the simple act of being together is enough."

**Family** [s.19]: host meals, help with homework, surprise gifts; attentive, affectionate, devoted parents; protective; worry constantly whether they do enough and whether family members are mad. Author's own childhood: rule-following (she links this to her One wing) but driven by pleasing parents; a mother's disappointment would crush her; contrast: a One follows rules because one "is supposed to"; a Two to be loved and wanted.

**Tips (real world)** [s.19]: (1) practice saying no, do things for yourself; (2) do not help people who do not want it; wait to be asked; (3) do not be the rescuer; (4) find a passionate career and voice your own needs; (5) set work limits and watch for burnout.
**Relationship strategies** [s.19]: you will be okay without external validation; take romance slowly and remember who you are outside relationships (Twos may define themselves by them); set aside time to serve yourself; notice the "guilt trip" urge and accept that others show love differently; ask who you are when not helping your partner.

## Chapter 4: Type Three, The Achiever [s.22-23]

**Names.** Achiever; also The Performer (knowing how to "perform" to be seen a certain way) [s.22]. Descriptors: Achiever, Performer, Doer, Go-Getter, Wheeler and dealer [s.23].
**Core.** Strong motivation to reach goals; competency triad member (value being seen as competent; Threes specifically value achieving success). **Fear:** being seen as worthless. **Desire:** a valuable, worthwhile life [s.22].
**Healthy:** efficient, positive, hardworking. **Unhealthy:** overzealous, self-absorbed, think they are better than others [s.22-23]. Success means "whatever success means to that person"; they also love helping others reach goals [s.23].
**Traits:** optimistic, goal-oriented, focused, charming, outgoing, driven, motivated, energetic, confident, image-conscious, charismatic [s.23].

**Common mistypes** [s.23]: anyone goal-oriented. Real confusions: Eight (both assertive and take charge; Threes to be valued, Eights to be in control), Seven (both outgoing and fun; Threes care how they are seen, Sevens go with the flow), Five (both strong work ethic; Threes love to stand out, Fives want to blend in).

**Childhood** [s.23]: can be confused with Ones for desire to be perfect, but a Three wants to be seen as valuable to caregivers (have something to offer), a One as good. Gain attention by stating or showing achievements; want to be appreciated for what they accomplished more than any other type; many activities, do not know when to stop (chase the attention), burn out and lose free time with friends; charismatic and well-liked, feeding a later need to be liked.

**Worldview.** There is a result to everything; activities exist to produce results (going to the gym for results, wanting to be the best parent and raise the most perfect child); up to them to attain results [s.23].

**Strengths** [s.23]: efficiency, motivation, natural optimism (hard in negativity), self-motivators who push others, charismatic, talk to anyone about anything, skilled storytellers. **Weaknesses:** self-focused (must be the best), put their job before everything; "chameleons" who change personality to be liked and can lose themselves, which is exhausting for others.
**Conflict.** Either act as though it never happened, or go straight to the source; the choice depends on what makes them look better [s.23].

**Wings** [s.23]: Two wing: more generous, empathetic, sociable, an encourager. Four wing: more artistic, feelings-focused, sensitive, quiet, creative. Balanced wings: generosity, creativity, sensitivity and goal-orientation together.

**Famous Threes** [s.23]: Arnold Schwarzenegger (never stops being the best; bodybuilder, actor, governor); Taylor Swift (songwriter, businessperson, generous); Oprah Winfrey (overcame adversity; highest-rated talk show; philanthropist, which marks a healthy Three).

**Relationships with the Achiever** [s.23]: social, playful, charming, radiating contagious energy; shared activities such as house projects, competitive sports or races, bucket-list items. Introverted Threes still have high energy and recharge differently. Seven versus Three on bucket lists: Sevens for adventure, Threes for the feeling of valuable accomplishment.
- *Social:* comfortable starting conversation with strangers; friend, romantic, family or work relationships will be full of social activity (dinners, game nights).
- *Playful:* loud, "class clowns," dad-joke energy; playful in the way they know you want it ("they know you like jokes, they'll crack jokes all day"); everything is about being seen as the "best," yet they do not take life too seriously; under stress they take on traits of a Nine ("the most laid-back number").
- *Charming:* know how to act, what to say, how to react to make others like them; a skill, but a negative when they forget who they are.

**Colleagues** [s.23]: always appear to have everything in check (appearances); goals of every size; stay late, check email on weekends, never under 40 hours; natural leaders, often in charge.
- *Anecdote:* Julia's brother Steve (Three) made a vision board in his youth, knew his college, major and life plan; later made a risky career change to the family business with their Three father.

**Friends** [s.23]: you must plan time together because their one-track mind can neglect close relationships; competition (miniature golf) reveals competitiveness; show appreciation since they want to be valued.
- *Anecdote:* Christina (Three) with a Two, a Four and an Eight as friends; she cannot do spur-of-the-moment happy hours, plans weeks ahead, arrives late from work; friends feel neglected, she feels they do not see how much work matters. A communication failure resolvable by each stating wants by type.

**Romance** [s.23]: intense partners who push you to greatness and climb alongside; need to feel valuable to partners and believe they must do things to earn it; often on the go, working late. Appearances matter, so they conceal issues and sweep them under the rug, especially when stressed: "the integration to their stress line, which connects them with the qualities of Enneagram Nine." At their best they value partners, are helpful, push their partner kindly.
- *Anecdote:* Shawn (Three) and Ashley (One), married five years, two children. Author's reading: Threes respond with feelings but are not good at showing them toward others; Ones respond with anger (not always outward). Shawn shows love through humor and playfulness.
- *Dating box:* excitement, competition, activity-focused (new restaurant, hike, escape room).

**Family** [s.23]: Three fathers: "dad jokes." As parents they push children to be the best, wear the best trends, get good grades; careful of pressure and criticism, especially with a sensitive child; practice showing true self so the children do not feel they must pretend. Healthy Achievers are loyal and cheer everyone on.
- *Anecdote:* a friend with two Three parents: constant friendly competition (races, sports, game nights) and pressure; never felt good enough; improved after she told them.

**Tips (real world)** [s.23]: (1) unplug from work; (2) practice being instead of doing; (3) ask who you are with the mask off; (4) focus on others' achievements and tell them; (5) diligently notice and change traits you dislike.
**Relationship strategies** [s.23]: schedule one-on-one time with partner and each friend; remember you are loved for who you are; listen without giving advice; remember how much your partner does; you need not always be the best, allow your partner to do things for you, focus on equality.

## Chapter 5: Type Four, The Individualist [s.25-26]

**Names.** Individualist; also The Romantic [s.25-26]. Called "the most unique type," "the most misunderstood yet are dying to be understood" [s.25].
**Core.** Motivated by the need to feel known and avoid feeling ordinary [s.25]; want to be seen as unique and different, not into the mainstream or trends [s.26]. (The text does not state a separate "fear" and "desire" line for Four beyond this.)
**Healthy:** empathetic, warm, friendly, creative. **Unhealthy:** moody, stubborn, self-absorbed, attention-seeking, "woe is me"; often feel something is wrong with them, which deepens feeling misunderstood [s.25].
**Traits:** sensitive, self-aware, authentic, deep, artistic, melancholic, passionate, supportive, compassionate, warm, envious, inspiring, friendly [s.26].

**Common mistypes** [s.26]: anyone creative. Real confusions: Nine (both empathetic; Fours do not necessarily hate conflict since it relates to emotion, Nines avoid it at all costs), Seven (both focused on what they want and intense; Fours sit with their negative emotions, Sevens avoid emotional pain), Six (both self-doubting; Fours focus on feelings and what is missing, Sixes on thoughts and what could go wrong). Also: Nines blend in, Fours stand out [s.43].

**Childhood** [s.26]: elaborate imaginations; crafts, finger painting, Play-Doh; sensitive, quick to name their feelings and notice sadness in classmates (seeds of empathy); feel misunderstood or like outcasts; the "comparison trap" (wishing for what others have). Parents should be intentional about developing social skills and relationships.

**Worldview.** The world is a place to create meaningful connections; if connections are not made their ideal world is unfulfilled; connection feels impossible because of their uniqueness, and only if they can connect can they truly understand others [s.26].

**Strengths** [s.26]: empathy and sensitivity (cry alongside others); enjoy small pleasures (sunsets, fall leaves, scents, rainy days); creativity and artistry used to create meaning; comfortable with feelings and melancholy. **Weaknesses:** staying too long in negative feelings, a "deep, dark place"; **envy** (they want what others have or experience, yet never want to be like anyone else), damaging relationships.
**Conflict.** React with feelings first; some withdraw to protect their emotions, others deal with it at once [s.26].

**Wings** [s.26]: gray-color metaphor. Three wing, called **The Aristocrat**: more driven, goal-oriented, accomplishment-focused, outgoing and ambitious. Five wing, called **The Bohemian**: quieter, more introverted, withdrawn, reserved, intellectual or academic focus.

**Famous Fours** [s.26]: Vincent van Gogh (art as outlet for depression and despair); Edgar Allan Poe (author spelled "Allen" in the source; melancholic, mysterious poetry); James Taylor (lyricist who always felt a little misunderstood). Many famous Fours are artists, actors or musicians.

**Three words in relationships:** empathetic, passionate, authentic [s.26]. Relationships are confusing at first and very rewarding because Fours want both to be noticed and fit in and to be unique.
- *Empathy versus sympathy:* sympathy is feeling sorry for someone; empathy is actually feeling what others go through and walking alongside; Fours "pride themselves in feeling hard" and become a big support system.
- *Passion:* the "Romantic" label; passion appears in every relationship: for friends, making sure they are okay; at work, about the work itself; in romance, being truly known and close, which is true intimacy to a Four.
- *Authentic:* what you see is what you get; they will not pretend; do not want to be compared; you will never wonder where you stand.

**Colleagues** [s.26]: hard to understand, and feel misunderstood themselves (risk of a toxic environment with too little communication); compassionate, pick up on coworkers' feelings, tell people it is okay to feel; if hurt they withdraw alone; do not push them to "get over it" and do not take it personally.
- *Anecdote:* David and a new Four coworker at a small advertising firm: seems to open up then withdraw, especially when someone has a marketing idea the Four did not have. Author's diagnosis: the Four's "deadly sin," envy, triggers withdrawal. Advice: draw them out and encourage them to say why; "always seek to understand."

**Friends** [s.26]: bring creativity and excitement; value deep and meaningful friendships and so have few; serious conversation but also lighthearted activities; the most unique, original friend you will have; other friends may not understand them.
- *Anecdote:* Audrey (Four), a friend of the author's mother (a Three with a bit of Four): they ask each other deep questions; Audrey has a quirky sense of humor that serves her wish to be set apart.

**Romance** [s.26]: quirky, one-of-a-kind; healthy Fours empathetic and sensitive to partner's needs and desires; passion and friendliness; impactful bonds. **Stress:** under stress Fours "take on the unhealthy qualities of a Two": self-focused, overly needy, overwhelming need to help others (especially when unwanted).
- *Anecdote:* Lauren (Four, twenty-something, single): high expectations (the author links these to her "access line to the Enneagram One" and to Four uniqueness); dates left her feeling not understood. Lesson: Fours want to be understood more than anything.
- *Dating box:* special and unique dates with deep conversation (ambiance restaurant, watching the sunset) and creative outlets (pottery, canvas painting, something from their favorite movie).

**Family** [s.26]: often the "black sheep" ("one of these things is not like the other"); love-hate relationship with being unlike anyone; value family connection; set aside time for deep conversations; environment is important, so the home is filled with warm, inviting scents, colors, feelings.
- *Anecdote:* Tiffany (Four, wife, mother, aspiring interior designer): candles, dim lights, inviting decor; environments are about how others feel in that space; family supports this.

**Tips (real world)** [s.26]: (1) sit with emotions, but not forever; (2) do not withdraw when upset, be direct; (3) replace envy with focus on your own abilities; (4) give space to creativity or a creative career; (5) practice the present, not past or future, and not envy of others' present.
**Relationship strategies** [s.26]: keep partner expectations realistic; invite your partner in rather than pushing them away when in your emotions; as an empath you carry others' burdens, so have someone carrying yours; communicate needs even when you cannot identify any, since relationships need equality; remember you are unique and show it if others do not see it.

## Chapter 6: Type Five, The Investigator [s.28-29]

**Names.** Investigator; also the observer, the intellectual [s.28-29].
**Core.** Motivated by the need to be seen as competent or an intellectual [s.28]. Deep desire to understand every aspect of life and keep gaining information until they do [s.29]. (The text does not state a separate "fear" line for Five; it states the resource-scarcity worldview below.)
**Healthy:** smart, sensitive, independent. **Unhealthy:** stubborn, know-it-all, critical of others [s.28]. Private about every aspect of life; people drain them, so they keep people out [s.29].
**Traits:** fair, knowledgeable, persevering, curious, intense, researcher, thinker, subject expert, open-minded, self-reliant [s.29].

**Common mistypes** [s.29]: anyone introverted. Real confusions: One (both intellectual and hardworking; Ones are much more attuned to others' feelings), Three (both work-focused; Threes care about outward appearance, Fives do not), Nine (both private and introverted; Nines avoid conflict and react mostly with anger, Fives do not mind conflict and retreat into thoughts).

**Childhood** [s.29]: more withdrawn and isolated; feel they do not relate to peers; read a lot; learn independence and comfort alone; excel in school; small social circles, meaningful connections.

**Worldview.** Resources are scarce, so they must conserve time, energy and money, or these will be taken; keep to themselves to protect resources; conservative with finances; up to them alone to maintain all of their resources [s.29].

**Strengths** [s.29]: observers who remember what others missed; do not succumb to social pressure of who they "should" be; concerned with what is good and right (like Ones), want good ethics and morals; never stop investigating until they understand; information they give is probably true. **Weaknesses:** lack of access to emotions (do not know how to identify or display them); struggle in social situations and need to recharge; know-it-all attitude and defensiveness push people away.
**Conflict.** Rational: collect their thoughts before responding, think about how to approach it; they do not love it but are prepared [s.29].

**Wings** [s.29]: Four wing: more in tune with feelings, creative, sensitive, empathetic. Six wing: more outgoing and extroverted, good at solving problems, but can take on Six worry and anxiety.

**Famous Fives** [s.29]: Albert Einstein (vast knowledge, stated never-ending curiosity); Jane Goodall (lifelong chimpanzee research); Bill Gates (reading and researching for his career). Note: "some of the most influential people in the business world... are Enneagram Fives."

**Three words in relationships:** mysterious, self-sufficient, curious [s.29].
- *Mysterious:* thoughts and feelings are kept to themselves; poor small talk and communication; will not give validation to types that need it; a healthy level of mystery keeps things entertaining.
- *Self-sufficient:* no issue being self-reliant, do not want help, annoyed when smothered; do things for themselves first, not intentionally unkind; hate being controlled and pull back when boundaries or independence feel threatened.
- *Curious:* genuinely curious about you but silently observing rather than asking.

**Colleagues** [s.29]: skilled at setting boundaries and focused on the task; give space, respect boundaries, accept group projects may be a nightmare; do not drop by their space to chat.
- *Anecdote:* Carl (Five) in an office with Sevens and Twos who lunch together; he eats alone or out because social time depletes him; after the company learned the Enneagram, colleagues stopped thinking he was mad at them.

**Friends** [s.29]: friendships built on shared interests because easier; very loyal, decades-long; easier to maintain old friends than start new ones; come out of their shell with trusted people.
- *Anecdote:* Mark (Five), friends from high school and college who watch sports in silence; he has since sought situations that force him to meet new people ("Is it comfortable? Absolutely not. Is it essential to growth? Absolutely."). Lesson: Fives can befriend people unlike them if you make a conscious effort to learn their interests.

**Romance** [s.29]: mysterious, but an intimate relationship can break through the boundaries they keep with others; they analyze before proceeding so are sure when they commit; need time apart to recharge; a constantly helping partner pushes them away; need to say what they want from a partner; vulnerability takes a lot, but possible with the right partner.
- *Anecdote:* Janine (Five) and her husband (Two): he gives unsolicited help; she finds it annoying and can do things herself; "The Two pushes, the Five pulls away. The Two pushes further, and the Five pulls further away." Unspoken pattern; remedy is communication.
- *Dating box:* dates need a purpose, which means learning something (museums, libraries, new cities, other cultures).

**Family** [s.29]: "a walking Google search"; parents with an answer to every child question; usually in the background at gatherings, silent observers, waiting to be engaged; events deplete them; family may read them as strange or offended; seek them out and start conversations.
- *Anecdote:* Max (Five) married into a large family (10 adults); hates being the center of attention (awkward opening gifts); in-laws first thought he did not like them; now know a gentle nudge brings him in for a minute.

**Tips (real world)** [s.29]: (1) speak up; (2) practice expressing feelings, seek support; (3) spend time with people who do not share your interests; (4) accept being quiet; (5) trust yourself and your actions more.
**Relationship strategies** [s.29]: identify your own and your partner's feelings and needs; tell and show people how you feel; explain your boundaries and why; do not prove you know something about everything at family functions or double dates; be patient with yourself.

## Chapter 7: Type Six, The Loyalist [s.31-32]

**Names.** Loyalist; also The Loyal Skeptic and The Loyal Guardian [s.31]. "Loyal" is always in the title.
**Core.** Motivated by the need to feel safe and secure. **Fear:** being without support or guidance [s.31]. Loyal to people, causes, beliefs or the environment [s.32].
**Authority.** Strong or resistant relationships with authority figures, no in-between [s.32].
**Anxiety.** Not the defining trait; usually arises when feeling unsafe or without security. The chapter rejects the stereotype that Sixes sit at home worrying [s.31-32].
**Traits:** hardworking, engaging, trustworthy, skeptical, guarded, problem-solver, doubtful, warm, courageous, structured; also reliable, responsible, always prepared [s.32].

**Common mistypes** [s.32]: anyone anxious (the behavior is not the type; the motivation is). Real confusions: One (Ones worry about mistakes, Sixes about most everything; Ones take more risks), Two (both warm; Twos need acceptance, Sixes certainty; Twos more outgoing), Four (both self-doubting; Four feeling center, Six thinking center; Fours are loyal to themselves before others, Sixes to others first and themselves last).

**Childhood** [s.32]: hesitant and careful; stay in comfort zone; kind and likeable; hard to make friends because they second-guess whether they said the right thing; defining relationship with authority (look to it for guidance and protection, or rebel); heightened anxiety and fear of small things.

**Worldview.** The world is dangerous, shaky and unknown; they have a hard time not knowing, but believe they can give it structure, stability and support and be safe because of that: "The world is scary, but they can make it better" [s.32].

**The Counterphobic Six** [s.32]: "the only type on the Enneagram that actually has two types within one." The Counterphobic Six has the same motivations and desires as the Phobic (traditional) Six but confronts fears head-on, assertively and directly; can look like an Eight but the assertiveness comes from a different place; confronting fear is what makes them feel safe. Described as the biggest and best strength of that subtype.

**Strengths** [s.32]: deeply committed to loved ones; always prepared (both subtypes). **Weaknesses:** hard time making decisions because they lack confidence in their decision-making, and will not decide without all information; self-confidence worry is about how they see themselves, not others' opinions.
**Conflict.** Seek others' opinions immediately because of their relationship with authority; trust others more than themselves; carefully prepare how to handle it [s.32].

**Wings** [s.32]: Five wing: more reserved and internal, gain knowledge to be prepared. Seven wing: more positive and upbeat, more willing to leave the comfort zone, less anxious, more social.

**Famous Sixes** [s.32]: Ellen DeGeneres (steadfast in values; author guesses a Seven wing for her upbeat personality); Princess Diana (altruistic; difficulty trusting others and her own decisions; only her sister was truly trusted); George Costanza from *Seinfeld* (an "unhealthy Six": low self-esteem, narcissism, habitual lying, yet loyal to friends and acting that way to maintain it; the author's phrase is "sociopath tendencies").

**Three words in relationships:** supportive, responsible, playful [s.32]. "Sixes were made for relationships."
- *Supportive:* unending support; cheering loudly for others' successes; they crave the same in return; the "golden rule" (treat others as you want to be treated) and the "platinum rule" (treat others as they want to be treated), which Sixes are good at; nonjudgmental support.
- *Responsible:* "the Boy and Girl Scouts of the Enneagram," always prepared; responsibility makes them feel secure; they feel responsible for maintaining the relationship and put pressure on themselves; looks like making sure a partner has what they need, emotions attended to, helping with daily responsibilities.
- *Playful:* funnier than people realize, often without trying; playfulness can mask anxiety and lighten serious moments; only silly with people they feel most connected to (it feels vulnerable), so playfulness means they feel comfortable with you.

**Colleagues** [s.32]: bond strongly with coworkers and are there for them, but keep work friends at work for protection; can feel inadequate and unconfident at work; connect with like-minded work ethic (on time, hours, lunch breaks); where Ones follow rules to be "good," Sixes like rules because they feel secure and show loyalty to company and colleagues.
- *Anecdote:* Jim (Six) among Twos, Sevens, Eights; will devise the plan or strategy but refuses public speaking; sits alone at mixers; colleagues misread him because he seems sociable in the office, where he feels secure.

**Friends** [s.32]: "the most loyal type"; no letting go once friends; often friends from childhood because time builds safety; need your constant support and security and equal loyalty.
- *Anecdote:* Megan (One) and Carly (Six), friends since college; shared interests and planning; Carly plans around worst-case scenarios (what could go wrong, forgetting things, weather), Megan plans because it feels right. Mutual teaching: Sixes teach Ones to be more playful and relaxed; Ones teach Sixes consistency and confidence in decisions.

**Romance** [s.32]: warm and playful; loyalty is a strength but makes leaving a toxic relationship hard. Best: supportive, honest, reliable, committed. Worst: stubborn, sarcastic, "my way or the highway"; worry and anxiety creep in. They bring their own values and passions and want to share them.
- *Anecdote:* Danny (Six) and Andrea (Seven), many years; Andrea could not see why Danny resisted her adventures; after learning the Enneagram he could name his hesitation and fear of the unknown, she learned to give him space to evaluate and make sure he knew what he was getting into; he always showed love and support.
- *Dating box:* surprises are not welcome; they like to be told about plans; meaningful, thoughtful dates; repeat places and restaurants.

**Family** [s.32]: the family member who worries most (not always outwardly); as parents, hard to let children explore; hard to set boundaries because of difficulty speaking up when uncomfortable; extremely prepared (reservation, sunscreen, double-checked tickets, Advil).
- *Anecdote:* Laura's father (Six): always prepared (Pepto-Bismol, Tums, umbrella, bug spray); routine (same beach house each summer, same restaurant for his birthday, same coffee order, same car in different colors); always there, supportive, nurturing, funny.

**Tips (real world)** [s.32]: (1) practice mantras (positive self-talk, "like going to the gym for your brain"); (2) it is okay to have fears; no guilt; (3) when anxious, ask why, when last, and what to do next time; (4) physical activity to stay out of your head; (5) break tasks into to-do lists.
**Relationship strategies** [s.32]: be patient with others who are less prepared; your stress affects those around you; communicate how you feel; set clear boundaries so others' needs do not drain you; take risks.

## Chapter 8: Type Seven, The Enthusiast [s.34-35]

**Names.** Enthusiast; "king or queen of the optimistic triad" [s.34].
**Core.** Motivated by a need to move on to the next best thing. **Desire:** to always have their needs fulfilled. **Fear:** experiencing negative emotions and boredom [s.34]. At their best: fulfilled and content where they are, can rest without "doing." (The type overview in Chapter 1 also gives "fear of missing out" [s.12].)
**Healthy:** optimistic, carefree, everyone's cheerleader. **Unhealthy:** cover up negative experiences instead of working through them; scattered; fear of commitment [s.35].
**Traits:** spontaneous, positive, upbeat, adventurous, flexible, encouraging, playful, scattered, practical, impatient, optimistic, impulsive, generous [s.35].

**Common mistypes** [s.35]: anyone positive and outgoing, "high on life." Real confusions: Two (Twos focus on others' needs, Sevens on what they need, usually a new adventure; Twos fear dislike, Sevens fear missing out), Three (both energetic; Threes efficient and purposeful, Sevens go with the flow), Nine (both avoid conflict and want enjoyable life; Nines slower, enjoy the ride; Sevens focus on what is next).

**Childhood** [s.35]: always on the go, love activities; the class clown; thrive on attention; boredom; feel personally responsible for other children having fun and get in trouble when they pursue fun by any path.

**Worldview.** The world is empty and they are responsible for filling it up; not enough possibilities and experiences exist and they must generate them; the world is automatically negative and boring unless they bring happiness and joy [s.35].

**Strengths** [s.35]: turn negative experiences into positive (but a weakness if pain is not worked through); carefree, meet new people, go with the flow; exceptional cheerleaders. **Weaknesses:** always on the go; scattered; difficulty finishing what they start (great ideas, poor follow-through, want to move on).
**Conflict.** Want everyone to like them; difficult to address conflict with loved ones; use humor to take the pressure off and resolve fast and move on to fun [s.35].

**Wings** [s.35]: Six wing: more hesitant, "half-empty" moments, more aware of what could go wrong. Eight wing: more assertive, direct and decisive, powerful, better at following through.

**Famous Sevens** [s.35]: Jim Carrey (energy, impressions); Amelia Earhart (curious for adventure from a young age; first female pilot to attempt a solo Atlantic crossing); Katy Perry (electric personality, eccentric outfits).

**Three words in relationships:** outgoing, generous, resilient [s.35].
- *Outgoing:* "the most extroverted type"; start conversation with anyone (server, cashier, passerby); seek you out at first; introduce you to many new people.
- *Generous:* looks like Two helpfulness but the motivation differs (Twos help to feel needed; Sevens help because it is another adventure); the midnight dead-battery friend, the post-breakup friend; want to share life experiences with you for what you would get from them.
- *Resilient:* pull themselves back up; part of this may be the Six wing (fiercely loyal, friends' and family's happiness matters as much as their own); hurt does not deter them from deepening other relationships; "Sevens were not made to stay sad. And they won't let you stay sad, either."

**Colleagues** [s.35]: want to be friends with everyone, inside and outside work; remember every birthday; cheer you on bad days; exceptional in emergencies. Offer them a well-thought-out plan because they tend to be scattered with assignments.
- *Anecdote:* Carly (Two) and Lindsey (Seven, "the ultimate Enneagram Seven"): both helpful and bright spots, but Lindsey loved any amount of people time, big laugh, visible excitement; Carly was more reserved and focused on needs rather than the experience of being with people.

**Friends** [s.35]: always ready for an adventure; elaborate plans to distract after a heartbreak; try new concerts and restaurants for novelty; hard time with negative feelings (not like Nines who avoid conflict altogether, but they fear being trapped in emotional pain and ignore negative feelings); assure them pain will pass.
- *Anecdote:* Nicole (Seven) and Chelsey (Six), friends since high school; Nicole planned a zoo, amusement park and shopping in one day; they argued; friendships with Sevens need balance.

**Romance** [s.35]: unhealthy Sevens fear commitment, being tied down, staying in one place ("an extreme fear of boredom"). Healthy: caring, generous, fun, corny jokes, positive, support systems; need to keep things exciting, "not the type to sit on the couch and watch Netflix every night."
- *Anecdote:* Brandon (Seven) and Morgan (Five), married a few years; the author notes Sevens take on healthy Five qualities when doing well; he wants to attend everything (family events, mixers, weddings), she is exhausted by that and grew up in a large family and keeps to the familiar; he fears missing out; they balance: she teaches him to slow down and be present, he introduces her to new activities.
- *Dating box:* adventurous dates (go-karts, miniature golf, trivia, axe throwing, dessert or bar hopping); they love surprises.

**Family** [s.35]: "the family clown"; great ideas but poor follow-through; scattered rooms; teenage Sevens are never home; positivity; "Life is one big party, and everyone's invited."
- *Anecdote:* Michelle (One) raised with a Seven mother and sister, who butted heads; Michelle mediated "right from wrong" and "a fair fight"; household full of laughter, short tears.

**Tips (real world)** [s.35]: (1) here and now over future; (2) do not cover up pain, reach out; (3) not everyone is as energetic as you; (4) pause before impulsive acts; (5) get comfortable in silence.
**Relationship strategies** [s.35]: really listen without thinking of what is next; say which activities you expect your partner at and which you can attend solo; communicate and listen to wants and needs; do things your partner wants; pause before cheering someone up, especially a Four or Five, who may need time to process.

## Chapter 9: Type Eight, The Challenger [s.38-39]

**Names.** Challenger [s.38]. The author reinterprets "challenger" as someone who "disputes to get to the truth" [s.39].
**Core.** Motivated by a desire to protect themselves and the environment around them. **Fear:** being controlled by others [s.38] (the Chapter 1 summary also says not wanting to be seen as weak or vulnerable [s.12]). Fiercely independent, "captains of their own ships" [s.38].
**Healthy:** loyal, confident, hardworking, energetic; heroic, mentors. **Unhealthy:** angry, controlling, harsh, self-centered, insensitive. A softer side exists but is hidden by their fear of vulnerability [s.38-39]. Protectors of the underdog, like Ones [s.39].
**Traits:** confident, protective, leader, passionate, decisive, truthful, inspiring, assertive, independent, bossy, productive [s.39].

**Common mistypes** [s.39]: anyone assertive and determined. Real confusions: One (both protectors fighting injustice; Ones suppress opinions and are not resistant to vulnerability, Eights are direct and opinionated and resist vulnerability), Three (both assertive and take charge; Threes do not love confrontation, Eights find it necessary; Threes focus on their own goals, Eights on justice for others).

**Childhood** [s.39]: feel they must protect themselves and others; big energy and personalities; feel no one stands up for them or does things their way so they do it themselves; challenge parents on everything, so parent-child conflict recurs through life; keep feelings to themselves and act as if they can handle anything.

**Worldview.** Two kinds of people, the powerful and the weak; the powerful take advantage of the weak and it is their responsibility to act; irony: others may call Eights powerful, but they see themselves as part of the group working to change the power structure [s.39].

**Strengths** [s.39]: protect and empower the underdog, weak and vulnerable; fiercely independent, authentic ("what you see is what you get"), straightforward (you never wonder where you stand). **Weaknesses:** bluntness intimidates; impatience tied to need for control.
**Conflict.** Embrace it as a way of life, deal with it quickly and directly [s.39].

**Wings** [s.39]: Seven wing: more outgoing, driven by excitement and adventure, always seeking new activities. Nine wing: more laid-back, calmer, less need to be right (sees multiple sides).

**Famous Eights** [s.39]: Martin Luther King Jr. (civil rights leader, nonviolent; the author guesses strong Seven and Nine wings); Serena Williams (activism, does not care about opinions); Alecia Beth Moore, "Pink" (pushes boundaries, does her own thing, what you see is what you get).

**Three words in relationships:** committed, independent, passionate [s.39]. Intense one minute, bending over backward the next; feel solely responsible for the relationship's success; best relationships are built on mutual respect and understanding.
- *Committed:* break a planned commitment and you will hear about it; they will never break theirs; committed to the relationship's success and the individuals.
- *Independent:* never dependent on you for happiness; value time apart; will not be swayed by others' opinions; need to be in control of themselves.
- *Passionate:* "Passion. Intensity. Fire."; you will know when they care and when they are angry; may raise their voice without intending it; cheerleaders and protectors who help you stand up for yourself.

**Colleagues** [s.39]: natural leaders who delegate and conquer tasks; committed to timeliness; intensity can be off-putting.
- *Anecdote:* Amy (Four, soon to be supervisor) and new colleague Jennifer (Eight), who announced at the first meeting that some people would not like her, took over, and assigned tasks (a new practice the team actually liked). Amy says without the Enneagram she would have been intimidated.

**Friends** [s.39]: the friend for those who cannot stick up for themselves; they confront anyone speaking ill of you; argue their opinion rather than be dishonest even if the friendship is at risk; strong principles they think everyone should share.
- *Anecdote:* Bethany (Eight) and Briana (Six), best friends for nearly two decades; Bethany disagrees with Briana's relationship and said so assertively, then gave her space; "once their piece is shared, they are not going to keep sharing it."

**Romance** [s.39]: passion; express wants and want partners to do the same; extremely high expectations and will not settle; independent, encourage partners to keep their own hobbies, activities and friends; like to call the shots and dislike being told what to do; encourage and empower their partners.
- *Anecdote:* Jeff (Eight) and Christine (Nine), married 10+ years, three children; Christine feels he yells, he says that is just how he speaks; he embraces disagreement, she retreats, so he pushes harder and she retreats more; remedy: she speaks up directly when hurt, he works on vulnerability; Eights "tend to be overcommunicators."
- *Dating box:* they want control, prefer to know the plan or make it, want to feel connection and do what they want in the moment.

**Family** [s.39]: very protective (criticize their family and you will hear about it); the brother with your back, the parent who instills standing up, the sister with honest opinions; often the family leader, but can be seen as the aggressor if plans go awry; honesty regardless of how it lands.
- *Anecdote:* Brittany (Eight) and her sister Anna (Two): Brittany fought Anna's battles and taught her to use her voice; Anna disliked conflict, was sensitive to criticism and quick to apologize; Brittany relished conflict, saw her criticism as constructive, and felt apologizing was weak.

**Tips (real world)** [s.39]: (1) be patient; (2) explore vulnerability; (3) identify the root of your anger; (4) relinquish control sometimes; (5) do not bottle up emotions, which leads to explosive reactions.
**Relationship strategies** [s.39]: surround yourself with like-minded people who empower you; adjust tone while keeping assertiveness; not everyone is trying to control you; express appreciation and love; teach people to stand up for themselves rather than always doing it for them.

## Chapter 10: Type Nine, The Peacemaker [s.42-43]

**Names.** Peacemaker. "Everyone wants to be a Nine," with good reputations for gentleness [s.42].
**Core.** Motivated by a need for inner stability and peace of mind. **Fear:** being separated or in conflict. Despise conflict and avoid it [s.42]. Lingering conflict is their "worst nightmare" and needs immediate resolution [s.43].
**Healthy:** generous, patient, stable, glass half full. **Unhealthy:** passive-aggressive, unable to speak up, forgetful, indecisive [s.43].
**Traits:** selfless, giving, open-minded, attentive, empathetic, advice-giving, calming, stubborn, unassertive, indifferent, peaceful, moderating, agreeable, welcoming [s.43].

**Common mistypes** [s.43]: anyone who hates conflict or pleases people. Real confusions: Seven (both optimistic, avoid conflict; Sevens fast-paced and task-oriented, Nines slow starters who enjoy the ride), Four (both empathetic; Fours like to stand out, Nines blend in), Two ("probably the most common mistype"; both avoid conflict and give of time and skill; Nines please to avoid conflict or negativity, Twos to be wanted). Rule: return to core motivation and fear.

**Childhood** [s.43]: quiet, keep to themselves; feel ignored or that their opinions do not matter; tune out conflict; mediate between family members; bottle up anger at siblings; learned their feelings are unimportant.

**Worldview.** The world is full of strife and they must remedy it by peaceful demeanor and mediation; every person deserves respect and a voice, fulfilled by empathy and letting others be heard [s.43].

**Strengths** [s.43]: peaceful, see both sides (devil's advocate, weighing pros and cons), nonjudgmental, accepting, slow down and enjoy small things, relaxing. **Weaknesses:** indecisive (fear of creating internal conflict if they choose wrongly); care too much what others think; slow starters (motivation present, cannot begin).
**Conflict.** Avoid at all costs because it is physically and emotionally draining; may be passive-aggressive due to bottled feelings [s.43].

**Wings** [s.43]: Eight wing: more assertive, better at displaying opinions and ideas ("a powerful combination"). One wing: more structured, detail-oriented, focused.

**Famous Nines** [s.43]: Walt Disney (healthy Nine using Three qualities: driven, entrepreneurial); Audrey Hepburn (healthy Nine with ambitious qualities; generous, charitable); Fred Rogers (patience and "silence in a noisy world," quoting Maxwell King). Nines "are harder to spot among famous people" because they stay in the background.

**Three words in relationships:** supportive, passive, comfortable [s.43].
- *Supportive:* empathy, carry others' emotion, nonjudgmental, unbiased advice, "no questions asked, no judgment received."
- *Passive:* go along with what the other wants to keep the peace; opinions exist but are not pressed; easygoing, opposite of assertive.
- *Comfortable:* comfort for self and others (environment and physical), relaxation, helping others slow down; mediate and offer unbiased advice.

**Colleagues** [s.43]: keep to themselves, lie low; difficult to start tasks and need a colleague's urging; offer a clear mind and unbiased opinion; more willing to help with your tasks than do their own; absent in coworker drama; kind, patient, objective.
- *Anecdote:* Tina (Eight) and Joy (Nine) at a marketing firm; Tina relies on Joy's kindness and authenticity with clients but not on closing contracts; Tina leads, Joy enjoys structure and direction.

**Friends** [s.43]: a listening ear and sound advice; do not take advantage or they may withdraw; they want to be heard but find it hard to ask; you will likely make most decisions because they do not want to rock the boat.
- *Anecdote:* Kayla (Three) and Brian (Nine), new friends; Kayla thinks Brian flaky, Brian removes himself from conflict; Brian thinks Kayla too competitive; she draws him out and shows discussions need not mean conflict, he teaches her it is okay to relax.

**Romance** [s.43]: kind, welcoming; comfort and support; calm and collected; hate conflict more in romance and will resolve it at once or sweep it under the rug; great listeners and advice-givers; feel at home with someone in their corner.
- *Anecdote:* John (Three) and Tom (Nine), married a few years: connected on the Enneagram (the author states: when John is stressed he takes on negative Nine qualities; when Tom is healthy he takes on positive Three qualities); John is driven, Tom needs a gentle nudge to start and more time and guidance for decisions.
- *Dating box:* they prefer to do whatever the partner wants and dislike deciding; cozy dates (movie and pizza at home, new cafe, picnic).

**Family** [s.43]: calm presence; mediate sibling conflict and play devil's advocate; as parents supportive and reassuring, defer discipline to their partner, the parent who will not say no.
- *Anecdote:* Shannon (Four, in college) and her mother Nancy (Nine): kind support but pushes Shannon to see multiple perspectives and does not get why friend drama matters; Shannon goes to her for advice, "just not when she wants to be right."

**Tips (real world)** [s.43]: (1) eliminate distractions on tasks; (2) practice decision-making from small to big, by gut; (3) practice expressing emotions, especially anger; (4) learn to ask for advice; (5) time management with to-do lists and rewards.
**Relationship strategies** [s.43]: do not sweep conflict under the rug; communicate wants and needs; do not always go along; get out of your comfort zone with your partner's help; identify anger and say it rather than being passive-aggressive.

## Chapter 11: Where Do I Go from Here? [s.45-46]

- You cannot assume anyone's type; do not pressure partners, friends or colleagues; the Enneagram reveals motivations, not all answers [s.45].

## Gaps and cautions

- **No type-by-type compatibility matrix, no instinct subtypes, no levels of development scheme, no tritype, no full stress/growth arrow table.** The book mentions the arrows only in passing (Table B). "Healthy" and "unhealthy" are used loosely; levels are not formalized. Instincts (self-preservation, social, sexual) are not discussed in this range. The only subtype-like division is the Phobic and Counterphobic Six [s.32].
- Fear and desire lines for Four and Five are not given in the same explicit "biggest fear / biggest desire" form as the others; they are inferred only from the statements recorded above (Four: feel known, avoid feeling ordinary; Five: be seen as competent, scarce-resources worldview).
- The author's wording is sometimes inconsistent across chapters (for example, the optimistic triad is also called "optimist triad"; the Eight's fear is stated as "being controlled by others" in Chapter 9 but "not be seen as weak or vulnerable" in Chapter 1; the Seven's fear is stated in Chapter 1 as missing out and in Chapter 8 as negative emotions and boredom). Both forms are recorded.
- A few type-chapter claims are the author's own and not standard Enneagram doctrine (for example, "anyone can be compatible with anyone else if healthy"; the Six as "two types in one"; the Four's "access line to One"; Nine wing described as making a One more passive-aggressive). They are reproduced as stated.
- The source has typographical errors preserved or lightly normalized: "Edgar Allen Poe" (spelled Allen in the source), "Sevens" used as a singular in a few places ("a Sevens named Lindsey"), "diffcult" and "unful-filling" from ligature loss. No passages were garbled enough to be unreadable.
- The contents page and chapter headings lost type numerals in the EPUB conversion [s.7, s.14, s.18, etc.]. The type is identified from the body text.
- Anecdotes are summarized, not quoted; the names are as the author gives them (several are composites or private acquaintances).
- Not extracted in detail: copyright page, dedication, acknowledgments, author biography, resource list, references (one line each above).
