## Overview

Part III turns the type descriptions of Parts I and II into a practice. Chapter 16 argues that the Enneagram is "not a spiritual path" in itself but a tool that must be paired with daily practice, and reduces that pairing to three acts: be present, see the personality in action, do not act out the impulses [p.353]. It gives criteria for choosing a practice, **seven tools for transformation**, advice on excuses and addictions, a type-by-type table of eating disorders and addictions, a long treatment of the **superego** (with a table of each type's "marching orders" and a list of "healing attitudes"), and sections on working with the body (conscious eating, relaxation), the quiet mind (including a guided centering meditation and the art of "not knowing"), the heart (grief, compassion, forgiveness with a set of affirmations), and a nine-step process model, **The Enneagram of Letting Go**. Chapter 17 asks how to tell whether the work is real, discusses why people cling to suffering, and offers a nine-layer map of inner recovery (the **nine strata** of "excavation and recovery of the true self"), then a closing reflection on Essence, "moments that live," spiritual maturity, generational healing, and the larger historical role the authors see for the Enneagram. A final poem-like sequence, "The Stages of the Work," closes the book.

## CHAPTER 16: THE ENNEAGRAM AND SPIRITUAL PRACTICE [pp.353-376]

### Opening claim: three elements [p.353]

The Enneagram "is not a spiritual path" by itself; it is "an exceptional tool" for any path, and its insights must be combined with daily practice. Practice grounds the information in daily experience and returns us to the truths the system reveals. Combining the two consists of:

1. Becoming **present and aware** as much as possible through the day.
2. **Seeing your personality in action.**
3. **Not acting out your impulses.**

These three underlie every other tool in the book. When we notice an aspect of personality, we remember to breathe and relax while continuing to observe and contain the impulse until something shifts. Analysis matters less than awareness, relaxing the body, and not acting out. The Levels of Development make the Enneagram's insights unusually specific and "on target," which is why they catalyze growth.

### Choosing a practice: three criteria [p.354]

Any practice (meditation, prayer, yoga, inspirational reading, etc.) is judged by three questions:

1. **Does it make us more mindful, awake and open?** Or does it support cherished illusions about ourselves, even negative ones? Does it cultivate Presence and contact with life here and now?
2. **Does it help us explore the uncomfortable aspects and limits of personality?** Many paths offer "spiritual glamour" (feeling separate from and better than others, expecting cosmic powers). Extraordinary powers are possible but are usually "a distracting sidetrack" rather than a mark of realization. A path that continually shames or judges us is also probably unbalanced.
3. **Does it encourage us to think for ourselves?** Ready-made answers from gurus or hidebound doctrines discourage looking into our own nature; they soothe the personality, cover anxieties and wounds, and fail when a real crisis arrives.

Life itself is called "our greatest teacher": whatever we are doing (office, spouse, freeway) is instructive if we are present; if not, every moment is like every other. No single tool fits everyone at all times. When mind and heart are quiet, meditation, contemplation or visualization work; when tired, prayer, chanting or walking meditation may suit better.

**Type tendencies in choosing practices [pp.354-355]** (grouped by the Hornevian-style groups used in the book):

| Group | Types | Tendency and what can help |
|---|---|---|
| Withdrawn | Four, Five, Nine | Out of touch with bodies; benefit greatly from walking meditation, yoga, stretching, even jogging. They prefer sedentary practices and may argue these "do not count." |
| Assertive | Three, Seven, Eight | Getting in touch with the heart through loving-kindness meditation and acts of charity may not match their idea of spiritual practice but can be invaluable. As action-oriented people they may see meditation as "just sitting around and doing nothing." |
| Compliant | One, Two, Six | May not consider a silent retreat or a massage spiritual; to these conscience-driven types, sitting in contemplation seems the opposite of dutiful concern for the welfare of others. |

General rule: anything done with attention can become a practice if it grounds us in the body, quiets the mind and opens the heart [p.355].

### Seven tools for transformation [pp.355-357]

The authors say the "map of the soul" is useful only when combined with these ingredients.

1. **Seeking Truth** [p.355]. A love of truth: curiosity about what is going on in us and around us, not settling for the personality's automatic answers. Stock explanations for our behavior are often a form of resistance. Example: the stock answer "I am really angry at my father" may cover the deeper truth "I really love him and desperately want his love"; both may be hard to accept. Accepting what is real now lets us accept whatever arises, since we know it is not the whole of us. Truth includes both our fearful reactions and the greater resources of the soul; being with the whole truth gives more inner resources [pp.355-356].
2. **"Not Doing"** [p.356]. Resolves the apparent paradox of struggle versus allowing and letting go. The real struggle is to relax into greater awareness so we can see personality manifestations. By neither acting on automatic impulses nor suppressing them, we begin to see what causes them. Not acting creates openings for glimpses of "what we are really up to," which often become our most important lessons. (Refers to Don's story in the Preface.)
3. **Willing to Be Open** [p.356]. A primary function of personality is to separate us from aspects of our true nature by blocking from awareness anything not fitting the self-image. Relaxing the body, quieting the mind chatter and letting the heart be more sensitive opens us to inner qualities and resources. Every moment can delight, nurture, support us "if we are here to see it"; most of us watch "a mental movie of our lives" instead.
4. **Getting Proper Support** [p.356]. Inner Work is easier with support. Dysfunctional work or family environments make it harder but not impossible; most of us cannot leave them easily, but can find others who encourage us and witness our growth, join groups, attend workshops, and structure our days to leave room for nurturing practices.
5. **Learning from Everything** [p.356]. Whatever occurs in the present moment is what we must deal with now, and whatever arises in heart or mind is raw material. Common flights: into imagination, romanticizing or dramatizing the situation, justifying ourselves, or escaping into "spirituality." Staying with our real experience teaches what we need.
6. **Cultivating a Real Love of Self** [p.357]. Not mainly self-esteem or "emotional goodies" to compensate for deficiency. A central aspect of mature self-love is caring about our growth enough not to flee the discomfort or pain of our actual condition: "We must love ourselves enough not to abandon ourselves." We abandon ourselves to the degree we are not present; when caught in worry, fantasy, tension and anxiety we dissociate from body, feelings and finally true nature. It also entails profound acceptance, returning to Presence and settling into ourselves as we are without trying to change the experience, aided by seeking company of people who have some of this quality.
7. **Having a Practice** [p.357]. Set aside time daily to reestablish connection with true nature. Regular practice plus some teaching or group reminds us repeatedly that we are hypnotized by personality, interferes with ingrained habits and gives more frequent and longer wake-ups. Every time we practice we learn something new; every time we neglect it we miss an opportunity. *Obstacle:* expecting a specific result. Especially after a breakthrough, the personality seizes on it and wants to re-create it on demand, but breakthroughs occur only when completely open to the present moment, and anticipating a payoff distracts from that. The personality may also use a breakthrough to justify stopping ("Now you're 'fixed'"). Reading and talking about transformation is not enough; "Procrastination is a great defense of the ego"; the only time to use the tools is now.

### Walking Your Walk (boxed exercise) [p.358]

Spiritual teachers have given guidelines: the Buddha's **Eightfold Path** (Right Understanding, Right Thoughts, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Endeavor, Right Mindfulness, Right Concentration); Moses's Ten Commandments; Christ's two primary commandments (love God with the whole heart and the neighbor as oneself). The Enneagram, being nondenominational, carries no such commandments. **Journal exercise:** explore what it means to you to be on a spiritual path; your personal "minimum daily requirement" for being authentic about spiritual work; your personal ideals; what you sincerely require of yourself; what you actually commit to when "walking the walk."

### Excuses and more excuses [pp.358-360]

- **"Not enough energy."** The authors claim we are given more than enough energy each day to transform ourselves but waste about **98 percent** of it on tensions, emotional reactions unrelated to what is occurring, daydreaming and mental chatter. Energy goes either into maintaining personality structures or, if we disidentify, into development. Hence "building our spiritual bank account": keeping some life force in reserve. A Gurdjieff epigraph: realizing you may die at any moment is a way to arouse the wish to work on yourself [p.358].
- **Conditions and requirements** ("I'll get serious about meditating when all my other problems are straightened out..."): a form of spiritual procrastination; circumstances are never perfect. What we can do is show up regularly with Presence, which we most resist [pp.358-360].
- **"I will become present to my life only when..." (the nine conditions)** [p.359]:

| Type | The condition the personality sets before it will "show up" |
|---|---|
| 1 | I have attained complete balance and integrity, make no mistakes, and have everything sensibly organized. |
| 2 | I am loved unconditionally, feel it, and others totally appreciate my affection and sacrifices and meet all my emotional needs. |
| 3 | I have accomplished enough to feel successful and worthwhile, have all the admiration and attention I want, and feel completely outstanding. |
| 4 | I have resolved all my emotional issues, found my true significance, and am completely free to express all my feelings to everyone whenever I want. |
| 5 | I feel completely confident and capable of dealing with the world, having completely understood and mastered everything I might need to know. |
| 6 | I have enough support to feel completely secure and stable, with every area handled and nothing able to take me by surprise. |
| 7 | I am totally happy and fulfilled, certain I have found what I am supposed to do with my life, and completely satisfied. |
| 8 | I am totally independent and rely on no one for anything; I feel completely in control of everything and my will is never challenged. |
| 9 | I am completely at peace and without conflicts or problems; nothing bothers or upsets me and everyone in my world is happy and at peace. |

  The irony: when we actually show up, we find the very qualities we were seeking, because they belong to the world of **Essence**, not personality, and Essence can be experienced only in the present moment [p.360].
- **Fear of becoming too healthy** [p.360]. Many resist opening to life because they fear people "will not know how much we have been hurt." If we become healthy we cannot go on punishing parents and other past figures for making us suffer (overeating, drinking, smoking to show them how unhappy we are). Letting this dictate our lives only means taking over the job of abusing ourselves.

### Facing addictions [pp.360-362]

- Active abuse of medications, alcohol or controlled substances makes the transformational work "not possible"; one needs to become "sober" regularly before sustaining in-depth inquiry into true nature. Abusing or neglecting the body makes it almost impossible to develop the sensitivity and attention needed for self-observation [p.360].
- Resources named: books, workshops, support groups, therapy, inpatient care. The Enneagram is not a substitute but, combined with them, helps understand the roots of an addictive pattern [pp.360, 362].
- All nine types can have any addiction and all can be codependent; the table gives tendencies only, as "a beginning guideline" and not all-inclusive. You are also susceptible to the pattern of the type in your **Direction of Disintegration** [p.362].

### Working with the superego [pp.362-365]

**Definition and function.** The superego is the inner voice that puts us down for not meeting standards and rewards the ego when we fulfill its demands ("Good boy! (or girl!)"). When it disapproves it condemns in the first person ("Look at what I've done!"). Rephrase "I" as "you" and the voice is recognizable as the harsh words first directed at us in childhood: it is the **internalized voice** of parents and other authority figures, old and new. It was meant to keep parents loving and protecting; we unconsciously identified with those voices so as not to risk losing love, learning to punish ourselves rather than be punished and suffer the consequences [p.362]. Even parts useful at age two are not useful today, yet remain powerful; the superego is "one of the most powerful agents of the personality," the "inner critic" that keeps us restricted [p.362].

**Working with it.** Much of early transformational work is becoming aware of the superego's voice in its guises, positive and negative, since it draws us back into identification and self-defeating acting out. When present, we hear the voices without identifying with them, seeing its stances "as if they were characters in a play waiting in the wings," ready to jump in and control or attack; when present we hear the voice but give it no energy, so it becomes "just another aspect of the moment" [pp.362, 364].

**New layers.** Watch for new superego layers from psychological and spiritual work: the **spiritual superego** or **therapy superego**, berating us with the voices of Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, Freud or our therapist. A major danger in using the Enneagram is the superego "taking over" the work and criticizing us for not moving up the Levels of Development or toward Integration fast enough. Presence reveals these voices' irrelevance; they lose power and we regain the space and quiet to be receptive to life-giving forces [p.364].

**The superego's "marching orders."** These dictate most ordinary activities, the "meat and potatoes of our mental life." Hallmark: they initially sound reasonable and make you feel "normal" but constricted; on closer listening they are arbitrary, subjective, coercive and damaging, presenting increasingly impossible standards. If we feel anxious, depressed, lost, hopeless, fearful, wretched or weak, "the superego is on duty" [p.364].

**The "Marching Orders" of the Nine Types and the contradictions to ask about [p.363]:**

| Type | Marching order | Contradiction / questions the authors raise |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "You are good or okay if you do what is right." | How do you know what is right? Who says so? Are the standards objective or subjective? Where did the ideas come from? Ones struggle to be good but are never good enough for their own superegos. |
| 2 | "You are good or okay if you are loved by others and close to them." | Why does your value depend on someone loving you, and how do you know if they do? Even if they don't, what has that to do with you? Twos struggle to get closer but still feel unloved. |
| 3 | "You are good or okay if you do something valuable." | What makes you think an activity makes you valuable? Why must you do something to feel valuable? How much must you accomplish? Threes are often overachievers who feel empty inside. |
| 4 | "You are good or okay if you are true to yourself." | What does "true to yourself" mean? What is the self some other part is being true to? Does it mean holding on to old reactions and feelings? Fours try so hard to be unique they cut off many life options. |
| 5 | "You are good or okay if you have thoroughly mastered something." | How do you know when you have fully mastered something? When are you finished? How does it relate to real needs? Fives work on a subject for years and still lack self-confidence. |
| 6 | "You are good or okay if you cover all the bases and do what is expected of you." | How can you cover all the bases? Does all the scurrying and worry make you more secure? Is doing what's expected meaningful? Sixes build situations to feel secure in but still feel anxious and fearful. |
| 7 | "You are good or okay if you feel good and are getting what you want." | Can you distinguish a need from a want? Would you be okay if a need were not met; if so is it a need? Sevens pursue what they believe will satisfy yet remain unsatisfied and frustrated. |
| 8 | "You are good or okay as long as you are strong and in control of your situation." | When do you know you are strong and protected? How much control do you need? Does the drive for control enhance well-being? Eights pursue more control but still don't feel safe. |
| 9 | "You are good or okay as long as everyone around you is good or okay." | How can you ensure everyone is okay? How do you know they are? Why is your well-being dependent on others' prior well-being? The impossibility of this task leads Nines to "tune out" problems. |

**Healing Attitudes [pp.364-365].** Another way to loosen the superego: become aware of automatic reactions to problems or conflicts, then contemplate a "healing attitude." **Practice:** for one week explore the healing attitude of your own type, noting what it brings up in relationships, work and home, recording observations in the Inner Work Journal; later explore the other types' attitudes. All are phrased as "Maybe...":

| Type | Healing attitude |
|---|---|
| 1 | Maybe others are right. Maybe someone else has a better idea. Maybe others will learn for themselves. Maybe I've done all that can be done. |
| 2 | Maybe I could let someone else do this. Maybe this person is actually already showing me love in their own way. Maybe I could do something good for myself, too. |
| 3 | Maybe I don't have to be the best. Maybe people will accept me just the way I am. Maybe others' opinions of me aren't so important. |
| 4 | Maybe there's nothing wrong with me. Maybe others do understand me and are supporting me. Maybe I'm not the only one who feels this way. |
| 5 | Maybe I can trust people and let them know what I need. Maybe I can live happily in the world. Maybe my future will be okay. |
| 6 | Maybe this will work out fine. Maybe I don't have to foresee every possible problem. Maybe I can trust myself and my own judgments. |
| 7 | Maybe what I already have is enough. Maybe there's nowhere else I need to be right now. Maybe I'm not missing out on anything worthwhile. |
| 8 | Maybe this person isn't out to take advantage of me. Maybe I can let down my guard a little more. Maybe I could let my heart be touched more deeply. |
| 9 | Maybe I can make a difference. Maybe I need to get energized and be involved. Maybe I am more powerful than I realize. |

### Working with the body [pp.365-367]

**Why the body.** It is "a reliable reality check" in ways mind and emotions (the other two centers) are not, because the body is always here and now; awareness of bodily sensation is "solid evidence" of being present [p.365].

**Eating consciously [p.366].** A good diet, regular exercise and rest are often forgotten in talk of psychological or spiritual growth; when we eat sensibly, emotions are steadier, minds clearer and transformation smoother. Eating habits are among the most deeply habitual, unconscious aspects of personality: we may eat much more or less than the body needs, too fast without tasting or dawdling, eat things that disagree with us. Different people need different diets (vegetarian or macrobiotic for some, high protein for others); awareness brings intelligence and sensitivity to eating.

**Relaxation [pp.366-367].** "Perhaps the most important technique" for contacting the body. It is a quality we can bring to anything, not only yoga or meditation: we can do anything from centeredness or from frantic inner tension. Conscious relaxation means coming back to the here and now again and again, opening to a deeper impression of reality.
- **Numbness versus relaxation:** many confuse them; they are "polar opposites." When muscular tension is severe and long-standing the body numbs the muscles, so we walk around "in painful knots of all kinds" covered by numbness; tensions not felt are not released and wear down health and vitality.
- **Paradox:** the more relaxed we become, the more we realize how tense we are, so first experiences of relaxation feel more uncomfortable and tempt us to numb out again; liberation requires staying present to whatever we find, including tensions, and with persistence tensions "miraculously" dissolve and personality becomes lighter and more flexible [p.367].
- **Test:** "we are relaxed to the degree that we can experience sensations from all parts of our body in the present moment." Relaxation = an uninterrupted flow of sensation from head to feet, full awareness of self and environment, "the river of Presence and Being," fully occupying the body front, back and between. This freedom is the result of "many years of consistent practice" [p.367].

**Boxed note on body approaches [p.367]:** massage, acupuncture, yoga, dance, tai chi, martial arts can all help; two considerations for long-range effectiveness: (a) how does your body respond (more comfortable, more flexible, easier to be present to yourself and surroundings)? (b) can you commit to it long enough for lasting benefit?

### Cultivating the quiet mind [pp.367-370]

- **Observation:** inner dialogue, commentary and judgment run through nearly every waking moment. Reasons: working out what to do next, assessing the situation, rehearsing responses, replaying the past. The chatter drowns out inner wisdom ("like frantically looking for keys that are in our pocket") [pp.367-368].
- **Misconception:** stopping mental associations seems boring; the opposite is true. The *repetitive* quality of ordinary thought and predictable preoccupations makes the world dull; chatter blocks the impressions we need for growth [p.368].
- **"Monkey mind"** (inner chatter, worry, aimless imagination, visualizing future scenarios, reliving past ones) versus **quiet mind** ("the mysterious space from which our knowing arises"). The normal mind is "trancelike, unfocused, and chaotic"; quiet mind has sobriety, clarity and steadiness. In stillness our intelligence aligns with a greater intelligence that sees objectively what to do or not do; senses sharpen and everything seems fresh [p.368].
- **Two classical mind-quieting meditations** [p.368]: **vipassana** (insight meditation) develops nonjudgmental, simply open awareness of whatever is experienced, letting thoughts and impressions pass without attachment. **Samata** [sic; the usual spelling is samatha, not changed here] develops concentration and focus, by repeating sounds or syllables (**mantra**) or inner visualization of a sacred image or diagram (**mandala**), disciplining the mind by concentrating to the exclusion of other thoughts. Both valuable; the authors prefer vipassana in combination with the Enneagram "as a way of nonjudgmentally observing our personality at work."

**A Centering Meditation (boxed; insight-style mindfulness meditation) [p.369].** Based on simple guidelines: stay with impressions and sensations of the moment, follow the breath, remain in contact with the environment while keeping silent. Steps:
1. Choose a place to sit relaxed, open and comfortable. Posture matters: quietly attentive, not tense. Often helpful: feet flat on floor, neck and back straight but not tense, shoulders loosened, arms hanging freely; eyes may be closed. Sit in a way that honors the long tradition of meditation across religious paths.
2. Take two or three deep breaths, drawing air deep into the belly and exhaling slowly; inhale several times letting the chest fill, exhale releasing tension; stress and anxiety begin to let go.
3. As voices in the head fade, notice yourself and surroundings: being here now, sounds, smells, temperature, the actual presence of yourself and its particular quality. "Check in" with your experience more deeply. No place to get to, no finish line, no particular way you are supposed to be, no inspiration or "spiritual feeling" required; if tired, be aware of tiredness; if agitated, of agitation.
4. Questions to ask: What impressions and sensations are coming to the body right now? Can you feel yourself sitting in the chair? Your feet on the floor (cold or warm, tense or relaxed, tingling or without sensation)? What is your presence like: fast and revved up, quiet and expansive, thick and heavy, light and flowing?
5. As you relax, tensions reveal themselves (face held a certain way, tilt of head and neck, scrunched or unbalanced shoulders, blocked or numb areas). Do not react to them or try to change them; allow awareness to enter them more deeply.
6. Continue sitting silently observing yourself and your thoughts, settling fully into yourself, fully inhabiting this moment, "fully tasting your presence," letting something more profound and essential arise.
- **Duration guidance:** beginners about **ten minutes a day**, ideally in the morning before the day gets under way; extend as comfort grows; the more the habit, the more one wants longer meditation; meditation becomes "a respite and an oasis" rather than a chore [p.369].

**The art of "not knowing" [pp.368, 370].** One of the main tools for entering quiet mind. Ordinarily the mind is full of opinions about who we are, what we are doing, what is important, what is right and how things should turn out, leaving no room for fresh impressions, so we learn nothing new and cannot really see other people, especially those we love; freshly experiencing someone we know can transform our state and theirs and sometimes saves a relationship. "Not knowing" = suspending opinions and letting curiosity lead; trusting a deeper wisdom; what we need to know will arise if we stay curious and receptive. Examples: a problem solved after giving up and relaxing; creative inspiration. When we stop relying on the ego's survival mental strategies, "not knowing" becomes an "invitation," "a magnet" that attracts higher knowledge.

### Opening the heart [pp.370-376 area; sub-sections below]

**Principle [p.370].** Change cannot occur without emotional transformation, without the heart being touched. We feel the call in the heart and only the heart can answer. "E-motion" = "the movement of our Essence, the movement of love." A closed heart means accumulated knowledge makes no real difference. An open heart lets us participate fully, connect with people, "taste" experiences and discern what is true and valuable: "it is the heart, not the mind, that knows."

**Healing our grief [pp.370-371].** Opening the heart means meeting our own pain and others' pain. Much of personality is designed to avoid this suffering; we close down heart sensitivity to block pain but never entirely succeed, aware of suffering "just enough to make ourselves and everyone around us miserable." The authors cite Jung's dictum that "neurosis is the substitute for legitimate suffering." Shutting out real pain also blocks joy, compassion and love. Limit: "not to wallow"; spiritual work is not designed to make us masochists; the aim is to transform suffering, not prolong it; explore the roots of the suffering we already have, looking beneath defenses at the fears and hurts driving us. The more suffering carried from the past, the more rigid and controlling the personality, but structures are "not invincible" and pain can be relieved if explored "a little at a time."

**Compassion [p.371].** Essence supports exploration of the pain and fear underlying personality: whenever we explore immediate experience without conditions or judgments, the Essential quality of **compassion** arises naturally and healing follows. Compassion is "not the same as sentimentality or sympathy or self-pity" but an aspect of Divine love that melts defenses and resistance when anyone's suffering is truly seen. Personality cannot create it; it arises when we are completely open and truthful about what we feel. "Truth without compassion is not really truth, and compassion without truth is not really compassion." Love is both the motivating energy and the goal of the work.

**About forgiveness [pp.371, 373].** Letting go of the past inevitably means wrestling with forgiving those who hurt us. We cannot "decide" to forgive any more than to be loving; forgiveness arises from Essential nature, from a deeper understanding of the truth. It requires fully experiencing the depth of resentment, hatred, vindictiveness and desire for revenge **without acting them out**; exploring the background of our feelings toward the person and seeing exactly how they manifest in us now loosens the structures holding resentments in place; "Presence fills us and releases us from our bondage to the past" [p.373].

**Affirmations of Forgiveness (boxed) [p.372].** Five sets of five lines. Each set follows the same form:
1. "I am willing to be willing to ..."
2. "I am willing to ..."
3. "I [do it]" (let go of the thing holding you back)
4. "I see [the thing] as ..." (a positive quality in the situation)
5. "I thank life for ..."

The five sets: forgive myself for my mistakes (mistakes as opportunities for "learning discernment and patience"; thank life for chances to become "more wise and accepting"); forgive my parents (parents as "teachers and guides"); forgive those who hurt me (the hurt as an opportunity to learn compassion; a name may be substituted); let go of my pain and suffering (pain as "places where my heart is open and alive"; thanks for "a sensitive, open heart"); let go of the limitations of my past (past as "what needed to happen for me to become me"). Note: you may write your own affirmations in this form as needed; narrow the conditional quality with each statement, so that by the third you have let go of what held you back; in the fourth name a positive quality; in the fifth give thanks, since it may have been "a blessing in disguise."

### The Enneagram of Letting Go [pp.373-376]

**Origin [p.373].** After years of reflection the authors noticed they spontaneously followed a particular sequence whenever they successfully observed and let go of a defensive reaction or limiting pattern. Letting go cannot occur through intention or willpower alone, yet habits sometimes drop away spontaneously; they wanted to find what made letting go easier. Because (via Gurdjieff) the Enneagram can be used as a **process model**, they organized the observations around the symbol: **The Enneagram of Letting Go**, a practice usable at any time, proceeding through nine steps corresponding to the nine points on the circumference, "although these steps are not directly related to the personality types." (First four steps start with "s," the second four with "r.")

**Rules of use [p.373].** The process **always begins at point Nine, Presence**; without some degree of Presence we cannot take even the first step (Presence shows us we are in a state of identification). Each point must be completed before moving on; the process is **cumulative** (qualities of previous steps carry forward). With practice letting go accelerates through the first few points.

| Point | Step | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | **Presence** | Starting and closing point; enough Presence to recognize we are identified with some negative or unwanted state. |
| 1 | **See It** [p.374] | With Presence, see that we are identified with something (a view, reaction, need to be right, pleasant daydream, painful feeling, posture). Recognize we are stuck in a mechanism of personality and have been in a trance. This is "catching ourselves in the act"; it feels like waking up and "coming to our senses." |
| 2 | **Say It** [p.374] | Consciously name the state ("I'm angry," "I'm irritable," "I'm hungry," "I'm bored," "I'm fed up with so-and-so," "I don't like this"), simply and honestly, without analyzing or judging. |
| 3 | **Sense It** [p.374] | The process shifts from mind to body. Every intense emotional or mental state causes some physical tension (examples: clenched jaw and tense shoulders when angry at a spouse; burning in the belly; squinting while talking to oneself; fear feeling "electrified," curling toes, holding the breath). Sense the tension; do not think about or visualize it. |
| 4 | **Stay With It** [p.374] | Stay with the sensation of the tension or energy located in the body. Temptation: "I'm angry and my jaw is clenched; I get the point!" If we do not stay, the state is not released. Staying may bring underlying emotional pain or anxiety, requiring compassion for ourselves. It takes time before we are interested in the simplicity of this; we want growth to be more dramatic, but without this, extraordinary experiences have little real effect on how we live. Epigraph: "The only way out is through" (a saying of the Twelve-Step programs). |
| 5 | **Relax** [pp.374-375] | If the first four were done, something opens and tensions drop. We do not force relaxation; staying with sensation allows it. Relaxation is not numbness or limpness: we know we are relaxing when we experience body and feelings more vividly and deeply. Deeper layers and anxiety may arise and cause re-tensing; allowing both relaxation and the anxiety lets the gripping states continue to let go. Bringing both tensions and emotional patterns into the light of awareness "dissolves them." |
| 6 | **Respire** [p.375] | Remember to breathe; not huffing and puffing (not Lamaze), just awareness of breath. Allow relaxation from point 5 to "touch" the breathing. The more engaged with personality concerns, the more constricted and shallow the breath (e.g., driving, work pressure). Breathing grounds and releases blocked emotional energy; breathe through whatever emotion comes up; we may feel ourselves expanding, more "real," more centered. |
| 7 | **Reconnect** [p.375] | Reconnect with a fuller sense of ourselves and the world: let other sensory impressions in (sunlight on a wall, temperature and quality of the air, texture and color of clothing). Open to whatever part of experience we were not allowing in. Real connection drops the usual associations, goals, agendas and internal scripts; we see, hear and sense with greater clarity. If the problem was another person we do not react as habit compels; in personality's trance we believe we know what the other is "always like," but reconnecting shows how much we do not know; we respect the mystery of their Being because we are more connected to our own; allowing ourselves to "not know" what they will do or say enables a more real, immediate relationship. |
| 8 | **Reframe** [p.376] | Reframe the situation we believed was causing our problems. We see it more objectively, from balance and clarity, and find a more effective way to handle it. Examples: if angry at someone, we may see their hurt and fear and speak with compassion and acceptance; if overwhelmed, reconnecting reveals we are up to the task, or that we bit off more than we can chew and need to ask for help. Reframing places ourselves and our problems in a much broader perspective. |
| 9 | **Presence** (return) [p.376] | Open to more Presence and increased awareness; from this greater capacity it is easier to go through the nine steps again if needed. |

**Practice notes [p.376].** We may notice we get stuck at the same point repeatedly (e.g., see it, say it, and go no further; or notice tension but get sidetracked before staying with it long enough); noticing where we abandon the process and giving added attention to that point is "extremely helpful." The practice gathers momentum around the circle, becoming easier and quicker; the further along, the harder it is to separate steps; the first part takes more struggle but once moving toward Presence, Presence increasingly supports the activity. Result: more relaxed, alive and connected with our own Being and surroundings and open to grace; personality's "dross" turned into "gold" by cooperating with something beyond ourselves.

## CHAPTER 17: THE SPIRITUAL JOURNEY: ALWAYS NOW [pp.377-392]

### Is the work real? Measuring progress [pp.377-378]

After working with the material for a while we may be more at peace, grounded, forgiving; still we may wonder whether progress is illusion, self-deception or wishful thinking.

- **Levels of Development as a measure** [p.377]. If we no longer show behaviors or attitudes we formerly did and behave consistently with a higher Level, we are probably moving in the right direction. Examples: a **Four** formerly withdrawn, negatively self-conscious, hypersensitive to criticism, temperamental (all Level 5 behaviors) who is now consistently outgoing, not taking things so personally, revealing self to others, and more energetic, creative and outwardly focused (all Level 3 behaviors) has shifted center of gravity. A **Seven** less scattered and impulsive, more focused and in touch with own experience, finding life more enjoyable through being more selective, has made real progress.
- **Subtler worry:** we might only be better at dissociating and "spiritualizing" our experience [p.377].
- **Answer: observe spontaneous reactions** [p.378]. Real progress is indicated if people and situations that once brought out the worst in us no longer do; if we no longer lose patience or compassion with a particular person or circumstance; if life is easier, more expansive and zestful, "an unending adventure" instead of something to "get through"; if we are grounded and open-hearted and can bring the full force of our Being to the day's tasks "with the involvement of a curious child and the nonattachment of a disinterested witness."
- **The Level 1 qualities as markers** [p.378]. The high-functioning qualities (virtues) at Level 1 of each type "are the keys that open doors on the spiritual path"; having any is sufficient, having access to all is access to Essence at every moment. Markers:

| Type | Level 1 quality as marker of progress |
|---|---|
| 1 | Accepting of our limitations and the limitations of others |
| 2 | Self-nurturing and unconditionally affirmative of the value of everything |
| 3 | Authentically being ourselves with honesty and humility |
| 4 | Renewing ourselves and enhancing the quality of life for ourselves and others |
| 5 | Seeing the deeper meaning and context of all our thoughts and actions |
| 6 | Solidly grounded in reality and able to courageously handle whatever arises |
| 7 | Joyous and grateful in the face of death, loss and change |
| 8 | Large of heart and forgiving |
| 9 | All-embracing and solidly at peace no matter what life holds |

### Giving up our suffering [pp.378-379]

Gurdjieff's paradox: the last thing human beings will let go of is their suffering. Two reasons: (1) our suffering is familiar and feels safer than the unknown; we may fear a worse form will replace it. (2) The more important: much of **identity comes from holding on to suffering**: complaints, tensions, conflicts, blaming, drama, rationalizations, projections, justifications and the "energy" they allow; "it is the root of our personality." If nothing were wrong with us we would face the fear of standing alone in the present, take responsibility, make and complete choices, with no blame and no stories about past or schemes about future; we would "simply become a living human being facing the vast mystery of existence" [p.379].

- Until full self-realization personality will keep shutting us down to some degree; expecting this prevents discouragement. If we persist and keep showing up, knowing we will repeatedly fall asleep to ourselves, Essence arises more frequently and with each awakening something new is revealed [p.379].
- **Gurdjieff's salt image:** adding salt to a glass of water; nothing seems to happen for a long while until a saturation point is reached and a new crystal grows [p.379].
- If we refuse to be passive to the mechanisms of personality we open to Divine grace "yearning to be active in us"; the degree to which we release attachments and attendant suffering is the degree to which we free our capacity for joy and life. The journey then feels less a struggle than being in love; the Sufis describe it as a return to the **Beloved**. Nothing fulfills us if we have not opened the heart to our true nature; if we have, everything does [p.379].

**Life supports us [pp.379, 381].** "99 percent of the time" life is benign and supportive; the ego fixates on the 1 percent that is painful, dark or tragic (and even then tragic perhaps only "to us"); the mind imagines worst cases like car crashes, but most of life is not made of these. Seen objectively, reality is "highly supportive," the universe more generous than acknowledged, so it "simply makes sense to awaken and open ourselves to this generosity." Traditions cited as teaching we are not alone: the Christian "communion of saints" interceding for those on earth; Hindus seeing God's manifestations in trees, lakes, mountains, storms and volcanoes; Buddhists seeing infinite forms of Buddha-nature; statues of saints and bodhisattvas as reminders. **Illustration [p.381]:** the Sanjusangen-do (Hall of Thirty-Three Bays) temple in Japan, dedicated to Kannon, the Buddha of Divine Compassion, with **1,001 gilded statues of Kannon** ten rows deep over two football fields' length, evoking countless helpers and waves of grace. When we open to the present moment everything becomes our teacher; the Enneagram shows how we say no to life; what we seek outside is available here and now.

### Subconscious fears of dropping the personality (boxed, by Triad) [p.380]

Reason many fear becoming present: it means becoming less attached to ego agendas. Each Triad has a characteristic false belief that its ego projects must continue and a subconscious fear of what happens if they stop; these beliefs recur as obstacles to Presence.

| Triad | Subconscious fear |
|---|---|
| **Instinctive** (8, 9, 1) | "If I let down my guard and relax into the flow of life, I will disappear. The familiar 'I' will cease to exist. I cannot protect my sense of self if I am truly open. If I really let the world in... I will be overwhelmed and lose my freedom and independence. I will be annihilated." |
| **Feeling** (2, 3, 4) | "If I stop identifying with this image of myself, my worthlessness will be revealed and I will lose the possibility of experiencing love. Deep down, I suspect that I am a horrible, unlovable person, so only by maintaining this ego project do I have any hope of being welcomed into the world or of feeling good about myself." |
| **Thinking** (5, 6, 7) | "If I stop this strategy, if I stop figuring out what I need to do, the 'ground' will not be there to support me. The world cannot be trusted; without my mental activity I will be left vulnerable. Everything will fall to pieces. If my mind does not keep 'swimming,' I will sink." |

### Excavation and recovery of the true self: the nine strata [pp.381-387]

**Origin [pp.381-382].** On a late-night flight to California to give a training session, the authors reflected on their own stages of inner work, whether they would ever see "the light at the end of the tunnel," and whether the process of unpeeling "the onion" of the psyche was unique to them or generalizable. They sketched the model during the flight and have refined it since. Their answer: "excavating our true self" accurately describes transformation; digging through layers of pain and negativity and "old accumulated psychic junk" uncovers the Essential Being, the "core of gold" that had been waiting and urging us on. Nine strata, worked layer by layer. **They do not correspond to the nine types or to the nine Levels of Development**; think of them as different "worlds" met going deeper, like layers of an onion. Parts have been discovered by other traditions. The map brings together insights everyone faces confronting universal barriers to Inner Work [p.382].

| Stratum | Name | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Our Habitual Self-image** [pp.382-383] | Ideas and images of who we would like to be and how we automatically see ourselves, usually with grandiosity and illusion (never lie, never late, always think first of others), or habitually negative views (unattractive, unintelligent, unathletic). In the trance we seldom question these and react strongly when others question or fail to support the illusory view. Person is in the average-to-unhealthy range (Level 4 or lower). Without some outside means of waking up, there is little hope of change. **If we have misidentified our type** (e.g., a Nine who believes she is a Five) we are automatically operating in the habitual self-image and meaningful transformational work with the Enneagram is almost impossible; hence the importance of getting the type right. |
| 2 | **Our Actual Behavior** [p.383] | With self-observation we notice behaviors inconsistent with the self-image and begin to "catch ourselves in the act" (self-image: always truthful; reality: white lies to avoid confrontation or to please). We have spontaneous wake-up moments; to expand them we must value them enough to seek support (books, practices, friends, therapists or teachers). Staying here, much less going deeper, requires cultivating presence: "The deeper we go, the more presence we will need." |
| 3 | **Our Internal Attitudes and Motivations** [pp.383-384] | The attitudes and motives behind behavior (seeking attention, anger at mothers, discharging pain or shame). Psychoanalysis and most therapy aim at this layer. Deeper inquiry makes answers more ambiguous because it is often impossible to say precisely what "causes" a behavior. We see the depth of learned habits stretching back generations in family and culture. The type's motivational core, especially Basic Fear and Basic Desire, keeps automatic habits in place; motivations reveal what we think we lack and always seek, and give a glimpse of what the soul truly yearns for. |
| 4 | **Our Underlying Affects and Tensions** [p.384] | Felt experience in the moment. Worked example at a party: stratum 2, we are pretending to be interested in a conversation; stratum 3, we want to leave; stratum 4, we feel restless agitation in the stomach or tension in the shoulders and neck. We become aware of subtle muscular and energetic tensions and of areas where energy is blocked or absent. Relaxation and breathing become more important. Requires considerably more ability to stay present to bodily sensations. |
| 5 | **Our Rage, Shame, and Fear and the Libidinal Energies** [p.384] | More primitive and disturbing states: the three "master emotions" of the ego, **anger, shame and fear**, governing the **Instinctive, Feeling and Thinking Triads** respectively. Also the raw primitive instinctual energies (basis of the Instinctual Variants): the drive for **self-preservation**, for **social connection**, and the **sexual** drive; primal affects of attachment, frustration and rejection. Very uncomfortable; need relaxation techniques and above all a nonjudgmental attitude. "Traditional psychotherapy tends to end at this stratum." |
| 6 | **Our Grief, Remorse, and Ego Deficiency** [p.385] | Not guilt or everyday sadness: "heartrending sorrow and natural remorse" arising from clearly perceiving how deeply and completely we have been separated from Essential nature. Considerable "conscious suffering" willingly allowed for truth; purgative, burning away the last illusions of the ego. "There are no good guys and bad guys," no one to blame; experienced as profound sorrow for the human condition, an intense burning especially in the heart. Associated in spiritual traditions with the **Dark Night of the Soul**. (Ram Dass epigraph: a grief occurs when who you thought you were starts to disappear.) |
| 7 | **Emptiness, the Void** [p.385] | Described in Eastern traditions, especially Buddhism. We fully realize personality is a temporary fabrication, a story we told ourselves; leaving ego identity feels like stepping into nothing, "like walking off the edge of the world," requiring faith to counteract terror and despair. Personality experiences this as its death. With support and faith, what appears as "nothing" proves to be everything, the "shining Void" (**Sunyata** in Zen) from which everything emanates, empty yet full of potentiality, our freedom and the source of life; observer and observed, experience and experiencer are one. (Simone Weil epigraph on grace entering a void.) |
| 8 | **True Personal Being** [pp.385-386] | Within emptiness we still experience ourselves as personal beings functioning effectively, but identity is centered in Essence and actions are guided by Divine awareness rather than personality's projects. A sense of personal, individual awareness remains, with an outpouring of personal love, gratitude, awe and exaltation toward Being. The state of "I am"; in **Sufism** identification with the personal **Pearl**, the Essential Self as a personal expression of the Divine; in **Christianity** the beginning of the **Beatific Vision**. |
| 9 | **Nonpersonal Universal Being** [p.386] | Little can be said; all phenomena arise from it. Mystical union with God (the Supreme, the Absolute): complete nondual awareness, total merging of individual consciousness with God, only God-consciousness; beyond any sense of individual existence; "limitless Being from which the manifest universe blossoms." The ultimate destination of the mystical traditions; to attain it in any lasting way is "extremely rare" (only some extraordinary mystics and saints), but most of us can have a taste, "and often that is enough"; once we know the unity of existence as real experience we can never again regard people, ourselves or life the same way. (Abu Yazid Al-Bistami epigraph: sloughing off the self as a snake sloughs its skin.) |

**The Continuum of Consciousness [pp.386-387].** The nine strata form a continuum from the imaginary, with little connection to reality, through the purely psychological, into the spiritual:
- Strata **1-3**: primarily **psychological**.
- Strata **4-6**: **psychospiritual**: elements from depth psychology and from the spiritual; need an integrated approach using both.
- Strata **7-9**: mainly the realms of the **spirit**.

**Where the Enneagram helps [pp.386-387].** Primarily strata 1-5, most powerful in 1-3. Strata 1-3 help move into the healthy range of the Levels of Development. Strata 4-6 consolidate a healthy personality and begin transferring the sense of identity from personality to Essence. Strata 7-9 involve realization and maturation of the Essential self and deal with issues at Level 1 and beyond.

### Beyond personality [pp.387-392]

**Essence is under our noses [p.387].** Experiencing Essence is not as difficult as usually believed; one of ego's main defenses is the belief that spirituality is rarefied, impractical and far away. We need not go anywhere or accomplish anything; we must stop running away from ourselves. Seeing ourselves as we are (truth and falseness) begins unlearning the habit of abandoning ourselves. "You are already here": Essence already exists entirely and perfectly; once we see the reasons we leave the moment (understanding one's type reveals these "reasons") we run out of reasons to do so. We "observe and let go," stop interfering with our unfolding, stop defending a self-definition. Spiritual work is **subtraction**, letting go, not adding; challenging because patterns are deeply ingrained, but the whole universe supports the Work. Closing line of the section: "we cannot do it by ourselves, but without us, it cannot be done" [pp.387-388].

**Moments that live [p.388].** Buddhist saying: "There are no holy people or holy places, only holy moments." Essential moments have a different quality, even in memory: more vivid and real because impact of life penetrated the dullness of consciousness. We cannot produce them at will but can create conditions by letting go of fear, resistance and self-image. They need no extraordinary event; they occur quietly at the breakfast table, on the commuter train, walking, talking with a friend; the authors report some of their most fulfilling spiritual experiences "looking at a doorknob, or really seeing the face of an acquaintance." "It is thus not what we do that makes the difference, but the quality of awareness that we bring to the moment." Face-to-face presence with another person is among life's most extraordinary things and reminds us we are always in the presence of the Divine.

**Boxed journal exercise: Moments That Live [p.389].** Write for thirty minutes about the moments of your life that had the most reality for you. What were they like? What were you like at such moments? Important or ordinary events? How are they different from other memories?

**Toward spiritual maturity [pp.388-389].** Early stages often seek dazzling experiences, evidence of what we hoped for; with sincere practice we directly know compassion, joy, inner peace, strength and will, may grasp what Buddhists mean by emptiness, what Sufi poets mean by the Beloved, Christ's resurrection in a new personal way. Unless integrated into daily life they remain vague memories, conversation fodder or ways to impress friends with being "evolved." With continued practice we realize these states are not extraordinary nor signs of being "special"; we are simply glimpsing reality, as fundamental as sky and sea. Because we experience our love, value, wisdom and strength directly, we no longer strive after them nor attach to specific possessions or outcomes; we retire ego projects with gratitude and can live as mature human beings acting responsibly and compassionately: the meaning of "to be in the world but not of it" [p.389].

**Russ's window-washing story [pp.389-390].** During a work period on a spiritual retreat (like the one Don described at the book's beginning), Russ was assigned to wash windows on the second floor of a dormitory. By then inner reluctance was no longer the main problem; he washed slowly and mindfully, free to watch personality "run wild" (wondering if he was doing a good job, hoping his teacher would notice, pondering the moment's significance). He noticed something deeper: a part of him felt it had to "keep track" of everything, recording events, remembering observations for later, maintaining a watchful orientation that felt familiar and necessary; "I was this orientation." He saw he did not need to maintain it; he relaxed, and the windows still got washed. Experience became immediate and unmediated: he was simply there as Presence; window-washing occurred, body moved and breathed, leaves moved outside, no sense of separateness, the world including him "a single, magnificently beautiful flowering or unfolding" within a vast peaceful stillness. The everyday world was real but like sunlight on the surface of the ocean while he knew himself to be at the depth. Afterward the connection stayed and deepened; he felt no need to impress anyone since it was not an "achievement" but an experience of the world's true nature and everyone else was an aspect of the same nature. Conclusion: one can be aware of oneself as a profound depth of Being and function normally (eating, conversing, working, resting); respecting and loving others comes naturally; realizing true nature frees us from the cravings and illusions of personality, giving simplicity, grace and "unshakable inner peace," and ends the endless inner restlessness, leaving "the unfathomable mystery of our Being" [p.390].

**The heroism of the work [pp.390-392].**
- Exploring habits, reactions and inner voices reveals how many are inherited from parents (their psychological issues and "solutions"), who inherited from their parents, back through generations [pp.390-391].
- Bringing awareness to habitual personality heals not just our own problems but destructive patterns operating in the bloodline for generations, possibly centuries; working on ourselves "redeems" the struggles of ancestors, whose lineage produced people able to be free (compared to descendants of slaves who realize their freedom gives dignity to earlier generations' struggles) [p.391].
- A further reason: **prevent destructive patterns from being passed to the next generation** (unconscious habits about the environment or racism have reached a critical point; many young parents try to embody new socially and environmentally aware values). **Parenting** is a call to awaken; raising a child is "as close as most people get to being in a spiritual school," because it brings up all one's childhood issues, which are passed on either by repeating or by reacting to them unless we use the opportunity to work on ourselves [p.391].
- Releasing the habits of the past is "a heroic endeavor" requiring courage to face hurts, losses, anger and frustrations, and compassion not to flee suffering; transformation has far-reaching consequences; working on ourselves is taking part in "the evolution of human consciousness" [p.391].
- **The authors' view of the era [pp.391-392].** Something momentous is happening in the world; it may reflect "the awakening of our collective consciousness." The species cannot continue as it has; "rampant egoism, heedless consumption, and grasping individuality is over." They suggest the Enneagram "has been given to mankind in our era as a tool for accelerating the transformation of the individual ego self," possibly linked to the shift in consciousness spiritual teachers speak of. "If even a few hundred individuals awakened and began to live fully conscious lives, the history of the world undoubtedly would change."
- **Closing lines [p.392]:** transformation happens when our ordinary perspective shifts and we attain a new understanding of who we really are, and awareness of who we really are happens "only always now. When all is said and done, this is the wisdom of the Enneagram."

### The Stages of the Work (closing chain) [p.393]

A chain of conditionals, each result becoming the next premise (compressed, in order):
observe ourselves, then become aware of tensions and habits; let go and relax; be aware of sensations; receive impressions; awaken to the moment; experience reality; see that we are not our personality; remember ourselves; let go of fear and attachments; be touched by God; seek union with God; will what God wills; be transformed; the world would be transformed; all would return to God.
