## Overview of this range

Palmer's book is a companion to her 1988 *The Enneagram*, applying the nine-type system to intimate and working relationships. Its method is the **oral tradition**: types are understood from the self-reports of panelists who live them, so each chapter is built from first-person statements ("the thoughts say... the feelings say...") rather than from outside description. Each type is framed by a **passion** (emotional bias, fixed idea) that creates a "veil of illusion," by a **focus of attention** (what the person systematically notices and omits), and by a **subtype** (self-survival, social, sexual/one-to-one) where the passion is acted out. Each chapter then runs the same sequence: worldview and spiritual path; the "inside view" of how anyone adopts that mind-set; subtype focus; focal issues; dynamics of change under security and risk (moving against and with the arrows); the type in love (what to know when living with them, intimacy, the signals the type sends, positive, negative, mixed and inner); and the type at work (workplace, leadership style, conflicts, employee participation, team building). Her stance throughout is that no type is better, that each is incomplete rather than wrong, and that the types describe normal, high-functioning people, with an overlay of spiritual language (essence, higher centers, grace) that links personality to higher consciousness. Pair-by-pair material is deferred to the Directory in Part III (later parts of this extraction).

## Recurring features of Palmer's template (quick key)

| Feature | What it is in this book |
|---|---|
| **Personality bias / essence qualities** box | Each type has a *mental* bias and an *emotional* bias (the passion) with a paired higher quality: e.g., One resentment and anger / perfection and serenity [p.49] |
| **Subtype focus** | Passion expressed in the **sexual** (one-to-one), **social**, and **self-survival** arenas; each type has a named focus for each arena |
| **Worldview** | A one-line first-person statement of the type's view (e.g., Six: "The world is a threatening place. I question authority.") |
| **"We all adopt the X mind-set when..."** | Palmer's device for each type's "personality bias" section: any person enters the type's mind-set in a specific situation (One when values are questioned; Two when seeing potential in others; Three on a juicy project; Five in scarcity; Six when endangered) |
| **"Grow by / have a choice when / helped by"** | Three-sentence close of each inside-view: what the type grows by, what the moment of choice is, and what kind of significant helps |
| **Security and Risk** | Under security the type moves *against* the arrow to the point behind; under stress *with* the arrow to the point ahead; she insists this is "the preoccupations of the other point acted out from the perspective of" the home type, not a turn into that type |
| **Signals** | Positive, negative, mixed messages, inner signals: what the type sends and what the type misses in itself |

## Front matter and Introduction (Chapters 1 to 3)

### Front matter [pp.1-16]
- Cover copy: the Enneagram is "a centuries-old psychological system with roots in sacred tradition"; the book introduces nine types by the author's names (Perfectionist, Giver, Performer, Romantic, Observer, Trooper, Epicure, Boss, Mediator), their strengths, weaknesses, potentials, "telltale indicators," and views each type in relationship to the others [p.2].
- Dedication to her husband Christopher and son Josh [p.8]. Copyright 1995 HarperSanFrancisco. Contents list: Part I Introduction (ch. 1 Personality Typing, ch. 2 Synopsis of the Nine Types, ch. 3 The Passions and Dynamics of the Types); Part II The Personality Types in Love & Work (one chapter per type, One to Nine); Part III The Directory of Relationships (every type paired with every type, including each "Double"); Epilogue [pp.9-10]. [note: this part covers the Introduction and the start of Part II; the Directory is in later parts.]
- **Foreword by David N. Daniels, M.D.** (Stanford psychiatry) [pp.13-15]: credits Palmer with developing the Enneagram **oral tradition**: representatives of each type present their own stories, often in **panel** format, building on Claudio Naranjo's earlier interview exploration of type. Her second distinctive contribution is each type's "recurrent way of organizing attention"; attention "defines what we can perceive and what information gets left out," so we are "simply incomplete rather than right or wrong." Third, the relationship between original **essence** and each type's system of awareness: personality can become "a friend, instead of an enemy, of essence." The book shows how each type's energy appears in **one-to-one, social, and self-preservation** behaviors.
- A disclaimer page states that Arica Institute Press publishes Oscar Ichazo's "enneagons," that Palmer's theories are "different and distinct" and that neither she nor the publisher is affiliated with or endorsed by Arica [p.16].

### Chapter 1. Personality Typing [pp.19-24]
- Opens with Freud's reputed answer that living well means "to love and to work"; the book describes "nine different ways of relating in intimacy and business." Each viewpoint is determined by certain mental and emotional concerns. The types describe **normal and high-functioning people**, not pathology; "No type is better than another." [p.19]
- *Ennea* = nine (Greek), *gram* = model. Companion to her 1988 book *The Enneagram: Understanding Yourself and the Others in Your Life* (self-descriptions of the types in a spiritual framework); this book adds how types interact, plus a **directory of relationships** [pp.19-20].
- **Teaching method:** the best way to recognize your type is to listen to people who share your point of view; panels of speakers representing types; "better than any teacher's opinion, or any book." The teacher's role is to steer the conversation to show "key behavior patterns and the inner intentions of type" [p.20].
- Against the prejudice that self-observation is for broken people: successful people who already love and work well are drawn to the Enneagram because it "anchors type in spiritual life," linking personality type to higher consciousness [pp.20-21].
- **"The Soup of the Soup of the Soup"** [pp.21-22]: she quotes personals ads (including "Enneagram Six, 44, seeks Enneagram Nine woman") and worries about trivialization. She tells the Sufi tale of Nasrudin and the duck: guest after guest, "friend of the friend of the friend," finally served hot water, "the soup of the soup of the soup of the duck." Her reading: dilution is a classic way secret oral teachings survive; the few trace the teaching to its source and renew it; teachings go dormant like seed in drought and return when the human climate is right [pp.21-22].
- **Essence** = "the permanent, as contrasted to the accidental, elements of being"; also called higher consciousness or spiritual attainment. The "higher aspects of type" are spiritual qualities, not talents or psychological maturity; they appear when awareness shifts beyond thought and feeling [p.23].
- **Abhidhamma parallel** [p.23]: three Buddhist types correspond to the central triangle: Greed type like Three (motivated by gain), Hate type like Six (life as a battle), Delusional type like Nine (functioning without paying attention); counteracted by Nonattachment, Compassion, Mindfulness.
- **The "Veil of Illusion":** sacred psychology sees personality as a **false-self system**; the true self is spiritual, overshadowed in early life when attention turned to survival; we identified with our type until we "became" it. The nine types are "nine illusions about life," the natural starting place for growth [pp.23-24]. Epigraph from Juan de Borja (1581): eyeglasses color everything, as passions color what the soul sees [p.24].

### Chapter 3. The Passions and Dynamics of the Types [pp.35-45]
- **Passion** vs. ordinary emotion: an older approach centers each type on one of the emotional passions of sacred tradition; "Unlike ordinary emotions, which constantly change, the passion is the crux of an illusion, a compulsion, the linchpin that keeps the personality in place." Passion plus **fixed ideas** creates the **"veil of illusion"**; we think we see 360 degrees but see only our bias. Examples: Pride type Two sees people in need of help; Lust type Eight sees things out of control; Envy type Four sees something missing. "We're skillful about dealing with our own sector of reality. The bad news is that we're boxed in." [p.35]
- Seven biases = Christianity's seven capital vices, plus two **generic tendencies** common to all types (Deceit, Fear) = nine. Gurdjieff (1872-1949) called the bias **Chief Feature**; he is quoted: it "tips the scales," like a bias in bowling; it makes us go off at a tangent; arises from the sins "but chiefly from self-love and vanity"; discovering it brings an increase of consciousness. Knowing your bias and others' lets you see loved ones "as they are to themselves, without the bias of your own projections"; "the harm that people do is largely motivated by their own pain" [p.36].
- Gurdjieff introduced the nine-pointed star with its internal flow pattern, saying he learned it from Sufi sources [p.36]. Rohr, Chaucer (Parson's Tale: remedy virtue for each sin) and Dante (seven terraces of Purgatorio) are cited as precedents for pairing passion and higher opposite [pp.36-38].

**Passion table (Dante vs. Ichazo, as printed [p.37]):**

| Type | Dante: vice / virtue | Ichazo (Arica, 1970): passion / higher opposite |
|---|---|---|
| One | Anger / Meekness | Anger / Serenity |
| Two | Pride / Humility | Pride / Humility |
| Three | (none) | Deceit / Truthfulness |
| Four | Envy / Charity | Envy / Equanimity |
| Five | Avarice / Poverty | Avarice / Detachment |
| Six | (none) | Fear / Courage |
| Seven | Gluttony / Abstinence | Gluttony / Sobriety |
| Eight | Lust / Chastity | Excess / Innocence (she writes "Lust" for Eight in the prose) |
| Nine | Sloth / Zeal | Laziness / Action |

- **Deceit and Fear** sit at the "shock points" of the inner triangle (Three and Six) and are "generic" [p.38]. **Deceit** = identifying with the contents of thoughts and feelings; identification is necessary for forming personality but we became "convinced that our personality is our nature." Those who see role and image as their chief feature place themselves at Three [p.39]. **Fear**: children are born with physical trust; security invaded by distress made us guarded. People whose chief feature is fearfulness place themselves at Six; "anxiety arises first in oneself and is then projected outward"; they scan the environment to explain an inner sense of threat. **Projection** is the major defense for Six (paranoid style) and plays a part in all biased perception. The universal **fear of change** (stalling, vacillating, doubting, forgetting, calling the problem trivial) is the generic effect of fear; change threatens identity [p.39].
- The passions, though named as vices, are "the raw material, the compost" for liberation; the Enneagram names only nine facets of higher being obtainable by transforming negative emotional energy (joy, emptiness, bliss are not listed) [p.40].
- **The arrows (security and risk)** [p.40]: a secure situation relaxes defenses; risk mobilizes. Under risk/stress one tends to move **with the arrow** and adopt characteristics of the type ahead; in security one moves **against the arrow** into the type behind. [note: the book's later type chapters give each type's specific security and risk points.]
- **Three centers** (Gurdjieff's "three brains": mental, emotional, body-based): only three input channels (thought, feeling, sensation) though their expressions are myriad; the passion is emotional and acts with thought and sensation. Quieting thought and emotion shifts awareness to a **higher mental center** (vision and knowing) and **higher emotional center** (feeling and knowing), receptive to **grace** from "objective reality, or essence"; "Grace works on nature." Balanced physical energies produce an intelligent awareness in the belly: **Hara** (Zen), **Kath** (Sufism), **Tan Tien** (Taoism). The consolidated abdominal center is "the power pack" feeding higher centers [pp.24-26]. She apologizes for the word "higher" as sounding elitist; all are affected by essence whether or not they meditate [p.26].
- **Subtypes (the three instinctual arenas)** [p.29]: passions are acted out in three areas: **self-survival** (self-preservation), **social** (groups and others), and **sexual / one-to-one**. "Like the nine passions, the behaviors called 'Enneagram subtypes' act as a hidden focus of personality type"; a subtype names "a mental preoccupation in which the energies of the physical body (instincts) and the emotional energies of the passions are joined." She finds them "crucial pivots" in transforming passions to their higher opposites. Ichazo assigned Gurdjieff's three vital energies to these domains. Each type chapter in this book opens with a box: **Personality bias / Essence qualities** (mental and emotional) and **Subtype focus** for sexual, social and self-survival.

## PART II. THE PERSONALITY TYPES IN LOVE & WORK

Each type chapter follows the same template: **Personality bias / Essence qualities** box and **Subtype focus** box; **The [Type] Bias** (Worldview, Spiritual Path, Concerns); **Personality Bias** (inside view); **Subtype Focus**; **Focal Issues**; **Security and Risk** (dynamics of change); **[Type] in Love** (Living with..., Intimacy, Signals sent); **[Type] at Work** (workplace, leadership style, conflicts, conflict resolution, employee participation, team building).

### Chapter 1. One: The Perfectionist [pp.49-76]

**Box [p.49]:** mental bias *resentment* / essence quality *perfection*; emotional bias *anger* / essence quality *serenity*. Subtype focus: **jealousy** (sexual), **nonadaptability** (social), **anxiety/worry** (self-survival). Worldview: "The world is an imperfect place. I work toward perfection."

**Spiritual path [pp.49-50]:** preoccupation with error points to a search for perfection. The child felt anger at being separated from the perfect environment of essence; anger focuses on violations of standards; resentment grows from seeing how much better life could be. The perfectionist strategy is a child's attempt to build a perfect outer life to cope with a critical world and protect "the vulnerable sense of perfection"; it "mimics the perfection of higher being."

**Concerns (summary) [pp.50-51]:** avoid error and evil; self-denial causes anger, unmet needs breed resentment; anger finds an outlet in correcting error and championing causes; monitors own actions; "think right, do right, be right" (practical virtues: work, thrift, honesty, effort); severe mental critic (harsh superego); guilt over standards; work blocks pleasure and anger; "I'm energetic today, not mad"; self-criticism reverses in self-defense into criticism of others. Gifts: ethical behavior, superb critical powers, effective organizer and analyst, leads by example, upholds standards.

**Personality bias (inside view) [pp.51-52]:** everyone adopts the One mind-set when values are questioned. A life dedicated to perfection takes heroic effort; you notice standards slipping when no one else feels guilty ("Have they no shame?"); conscience goes wild when error is overlooked ("I saw. I knew. I'm guilty."); you don't recognize rising anger, tension feels appropriate and means you're trying hard; the task keeps enlarging, then fury spikes ("the jagged edge in your voice"); anger locks attention on the right way to fix things and "fuels your conviction." Ones grow by "knowing what they want instead of what would be right," by relaxing and letting pleasure in. They are helped by partners who accept differences of opinion, soften the one right way, and are open to pleasure [p.52].

**Subtype focus [pp.52-54]:**

| Arena | Form of the One's anger | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual / one-to-one: **Jealousy (heat)** | acted out angrily and possessively, "white-hot rage" in the guts when a relationship is threatened | "How dare you take what's rightfully mine?" Because Ones can't allow wants, a threat to gratification feels like losing a lifeline; compared with a rival, "If you had been perfect, there would be no contest." Obsessive checking, wanting names and dates, confrontation. Extends beyond sex: jealous of those promoted or popular at work. "I deserve recognition" feels safer than "I want"; "This has to be stopped" replaces the forbidden "I want to have." [pp.52-53] |
| Social: **Nonadaptability** | anger acted through correct causes and ideals; religious fervor and political conviction as outlets | Position in a group defined by uncompromising opinions; friends who share beliefs; rigidity removes gray areas and loopholes where error might creep in; hard to absorb new information once a position is taken ("one error blows the whole decision apart"); examples: the right-winger ranting in the gay district, the left-winger selling bumper stickers who makes buyers feel guilty. [p.53] |
| Survival: **Anxiety (worry)** | conflict between what you want and what's right produces worry about survival | Wants an exciting career but balks at risk of error; either/or (safe career vs. finding out what you want); "selling out for safety" angers them unconsciously; stingy about what they have and earn; belief that people do not willingly share, love and support must be earned; "What's yours is yours, and what's mine is mine"; resentful of those who don't have to struggle ("Life isn't fair"); procrastination, "What if I pick the wrong profession?"; "A slave to circumstance." [pp.53-54] |

**Focal issues [pp.54-61]:**
- **Righteous anger** [pp.54-55]: signs of building anger: physically rigid and superpolite. Anger comes with "total grievance recall"; forgiveness feels like pretending it never happened and risks repetition. Advice to partners: admit the mistake again and put it in perspective ("That was then. This is now. But yes, I do remember."). The **grudge list** is a safe outlet for anger that can't be expressed. Inner report: "I'm like a bottle with a cork in my mouth." Practice: name physical sensations ("Belly tight. Jaw clenched. Going blank.").
- **Emotional control** [p.55]: Ones weren't allowed "bad" emotions, so fear going out of control; suppressed feelings magnify; clamping down loses emotional information (can't tell a flash of anger from fury, can't know what they want). Relaxation exercises plus attention practice help.
- **Compulsive tendencies (doing things right)** [pp.55-56]: free time makes them anxious; activity fills the time so needs don't emerge. A Los Angeles contractor describes stress as compulsion: rechecking bolt sizes and code, testing whether a screw is tight enough ("It's a relief to see that I did it right."). "When Ones get focused, they're unstoppable"; the trick is telling compulsive work that blocks anxiety from work done for pleasure.
- **The inner critic** [pp.56-57]: loud, intrusive "superego run wild," insulting and punishing; Ones judge others in self-defense to equalize the pressure. Known as **Judging Mind (Comparing Mind)** in Buddhist practice, insidious because "it masquerades as giving good advice." Leads to constant comparison ("I wish my Sara was as smart as his Susie"); comparing signals insecurity; check backward to what triggered it.
- **Black-or-white thinking** [p.57]: error crowds out whole-picture thinking; "A 10 percent error fills 100 percent of the mind." Relationship is perfect or wrong; job faultless or an embarrassment.
- **One right way** [p.57]: yes/no decisions attractive; ambiguity "signals dead stop"; dislikes quick decisions amid crosscurrents; doesn't want to reopen a made decision.
- **Decision making (What if it's not right?)** [p.58]: perfect job would be educational, fulfilling, constructive, financially rewarding; ideal mate approved by mother and friends; speeds up with "good enough" and deadlines; factor feelings in.
- **Procrastination** [p.58]: reworking data "to be totally sure" while action is on hold; others feel controlled, meeting after meeting.
- **Ones as saints** [pp.58-59]: Ones who are established in the right are not self-critical, don't get depressed, and feel favorable to others; students call them "saints," perfectionists who've "gotten the internal critic off their backs." Many are genuine, their projects last, they are loved. Others feel morally superior through lifestyle, family, religion or career ("Good things happen to good people").
- **New Age Ones** [p.59]: earlier Ones were prim and superpolite; a newer kind educated non-repressively now hears that people "should" be sexual, "ought" to relax, are "supposed" to be creative, that it's "right" to schedule pleasure.
- **Trapdoor Ones** [pp.59-60]: "a way to release emotional pressure by acting on forbidden needs"; "trapdoor" rather than "going out the back door" (cheating); a double life, each satisfying different aspects; usually discovered in the twenties. Quote: "If one department of my life doesn't know about the other, then I am obedient to both." Story: a woman who posed nude for a photographer who chose imperfect bodies; she went from stiff terror to giggly flirtation once "I drowned the critic in a few glasses of wine"; "sticking my tongue out at the world."
- **A One away from home** [p.60]: freed from responsibility, a One "can go out and play"; relationships flourish on scheduled vacations; too much to do at home.
- **Pleasure** [pp.60-61]: paradoxically makes Ones anxious; shoulds interfere; "the backlash of pleasure is the Wrath of God. Lightning may strike"; bargaining ("I'll only take an hour off"); helps if a partner initiates time off or models relaxation, and to voice worries and reality-check.

**Security and risk [pp.61-64]:**

| | Direction | Healthy side | Low side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security | to **Seven** | guilt-free self; easier to know what you want and do it; wishes rather than shoulds; multiple options less threatening ("Let's try something new"); acts as "seeing the good in different points of view" | wanting everything, anger about years of self-deprivation, narcissistic entitlement ("the better people deserve the very best"), Seven's gluttony acted as entitlement |
| Risk (stress) | to **Four** | realize buried needs must factor in: "What do I want, rather than what should I do?"; many Ones find their true work and nature during loss | grief, depression, abandonment fears about flaws, self-absorption ("Only I have suffered so"), "Nothing has meaning," envy as feelings of worthlessness compared to successful happy people |

She stresses the One never "turns into" a Seven or Four; the preoccupations of those points are "acted out from the perspective of a One" [p.62]. Early warning signs of stress: free time provokes anxiety, work compulsive, pleasure dropped, critic punitive, emotional control tightens, right-or-wrong thinking intensifies; One is "a classic Type A health risk" [p.63]. The developmental task is integrating security and risk reactions, so a developed One "embodies the unique direction found at Four, coupled with the ease and experimentation of Seven and the rigor of One" [p.62]. Following the anger trail reveals buried needs [p.63].

**One in love [pp.64-70]:**

*Living with Ones (her list):* remember details (being on time, names, proper introductions); speak respectfully, ask permission; compliment thrift, effort, dependability (don't expect it back); cultivate character, don't flaunt achievements; admit error immediately to prevent resentment; bring novelty and fun (Ones repeat the known); avoid power struggles ("There are at least two right ways"); keep your own interests (Ones work long hours alone); humor helps. Ones perfect relationships: "What are our responsibilities?" "What does right relating mean?" Ones have a **scorched-earth policy** (consider calling the whole thing off when a negative appears), but once committed and convinced they dig in, are extremely loyal, value family. Guilt: pleasure signals anxiety [pp.64-65].

*Intimacy [pp.65-67]:* the Enneagram's critics "shatter under criticism"; "What will the neighbors say?"; they read implied criticism into innocent remarks ("You look great tonight" could mean the last outfit was bad); catch-phrases like "Why didn't you finish?" feel like traps. Partners should preface criticism with praise and follow with reassurance. Living with a One feels like living with two people (the critic is the second). Ones criticize in self-defense; need reassurance but feel guilty asking; by not asking they are immunized against rejection while the partner can't guess. Ones must learn that a spouse can have good and bad points; "If I have any bad feelings, then I'm either in the wrong relationship or guilty." Ones "suffer for the faults of others" and feel guilty for being angry. Case of a young husband: anger means "I don't love her anymore," so he pushes anger back and gets depressed (One moving to Four), each of his wife's faults gives "Shock" (baggy pants), a trouble spot blankets the whole relationship; he would "win twice" by getting angry about the pants and learning he is loved even when angry [pp.66-68].

*Signals Ones send [pp.68-70]:*

| Positive | Negative | Mixed messages / inner signals |
|---|---|---|
| challenge to correct action and effort; keep promises, "the buck stops there"; virtue its own reward; joy of a job well done; ethical idealism; practical areas: health, honesty, right livelihood, good fun | criticality and superior stance make you feel inferior, rejected, alienated; criticism as a kind of compliment ("proof of caring"); emotions controlled; unspoken criticism; partner is "wrong when you don't do your best and wrong if you're better" | when most committed to corrective action they may be focused on should, not want; self-serving acts become the objectively right position (the cyclist: cars are bad, bicycles the one right way); hard to read own anger, replacing it with a false "solution" ("I think I'll bring roses home"); misreading anger as energy; "Nothing is wrong," "I never get angry" in a clenched polite voice; the emotional rush of being "unequivocally right" may be the signal of bias |

**One at work [pp.70-76]:**
- *In the workplace:* wants specific guidelines and schedules ("loopholes are traumatic"); practical, turns abstractions into step-by-step procedures; accountability; tracks detail (energy may divert from product); looks for ethical character (discipline, manners, appearance, respect); prefers doing over feeling; sees critical points but struggles to propose broad solutions; secure in a formal role, respects hierarchy; "Good people have a good history"; works hard for the right cause, good leader, competent team; compares effort ("If they work, I work"); keeps score; may mask personal entitlement by working for a good cause; wants rewards but will not ask, may displace resentment onto details; hard to delegate; holds a loner's stance until the source of error is assigned; shifts blame ("It wasn't my fault"); avoids risk; advocate for disadvantaged and those who improve by effort [pp.70-71].
- *Leadership style:* "quality control. Quality and control go hand in hand"; a perfect plan, designated responsibility, stage-by-stage implementation; key question "Who is responsible?"; likes to work alone for hours with lists and diagrams; implements mechanically; once the plan starts it is hard to shift attention; effective for planning and structure, ineffective for on-the-spot decisions. To influence a rigid One, restate the standing plan and insert a simple accommodation without opening options; they need good **point men** and a trusted associate to ease decisions. Projects spread, take longer and cost more (patio example growing into a foundation ditch "the size of the Suez Canal") [pp.71-73].
- *Conflicts:* accountability's subtext is control; people feel stifled by meetings and reports; rules that bind people to the system; peer conflicts over being right, "It wasn't my fault," believing "support to others disempowers oneself" [p.73]. Conflict resolution cross-referenced to Part III [p.74].
- *Employee participation:* wants evaluation on skills, uncomfortable with "a good personality" criteria; critiques a position more easily than creating one but is innovative within guidelines; if told how, they can be flexible (designate options, give examples, state conditions); goal-oriented rather than process-oriented; a mistake signals "full stop, full review"; can blank out under pressure, helped by reminders of past success. Process speeds up when someone else asks questions and models "We're working out the steps as we go along"; Ones teach well, find it easier to ask questions on others' behalf; direct public questioning is threatening; "go home and study the manual all night"; announce procedure changes in advance [pp.74-75].
- *Team building:* needs to be right, traumatized by public contradiction, but thrives on controversy and debate; put them in charge of a tight sphere of influence; they hold back if a self-promoter is on the team or the team is weak (matching others' effort); prefer not to be the lone critical voice; discount you as a lightweight if you can't foresee flaws in your own sector; at best with motivated, well-trained peers [pp.75-76].

### Chapter 2. Two: The Giver [pp.77-97]

**Box [p.77]:** mental bias *flattery* / essence quality *will (freedom)*; emotional bias *pride* / *humility*. Subtype focus: **aggression/seduction** (sexual), **ambition** (social), **me first / privilege** (self-survival). Worldview: "People depend on my help. I am needed."

**Spiritual path [p.77]:** the preoccupation with pleasing points to a search for **will**. The promptings of higher will were subverted when the child's attention turned to flattery, which serves others' will. Pride is "an inflated sense of self-worth that masks dependency on approval"; strategic giving "mimics the action of higher will."

**Concerns (summary) [p.78]:** wants approval, avoids rejection; pride in being needed, central, indispensable; "I have no needs"; represses feelings to be loved; manipulates to get what is needed; boundary issues ("I'm not taking over; I'm responding to a need"); many selves, each attuned to someone ("Which one is really me?"); sees potential in people and is inspired to own best by them; own needs met through others; craves freedom, feels confined by supporting others.

**Personality bias (inside view) [pp.78-79]:** everyone adopts the Two view when they see potential in people; a Two's "window on life looks out on other people." Growing up when survival depended on pleasing, Twos gave to get needs met; as adults they move toward people and form associations in which they become indispensable; people feel "chosen and flattered," and relating then depends on what Twos will or won't give. Pride "is that rush of elation we feel when someone special adores us." If pleasing becomes habit, self-observation stops; rejection "feels like annihilation"; you position yourself with the right people, adopt their interests, become a good source of information; "you've lost yourself and become indispensable." Twos grow by discovering what they want and by being alone; they are helped by significants not seduced by adaptation, who love them apart from what they give and see them through the crisis of standing alone [p.79].

**Subtype focus [pp.79-81]:**

| Arena | Pride as acted out | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual: **Aggression/seduction** | seductive adaptation | become attractive by adjusting focus, adopting a partner's interests and tastes; talent for pleasing notoriously difficult people; pride in being "the favorite, the trusted confidant"; the aggressive stance is **pursuit** and shouldering difficulty, which spotlights the partner and deflects attention from self; accomplished Givers fine-tune the attention they get; "different tactics for parents than for studs and party girls"; not gender related (seductive males, aggressive females). [p.80] |
| Social: **Ambition** | pride of place by social positioning | who you know and where you're seen, did your guests get impressed; public image, professional letters; hosts social events; influence indirectly by arranging meetings and putting people in touch; "back a winner," attracted to people on the way up; aligns professional interests with a mentor or public figure; inner-circle membership is "the hallmark of ambition. I pat your back and you pat mine"; devotees, fans, hubs of the family. [pp.80-81] |
| Survival: **Privileged self-survival** ("me first") | pride in own independence while believing others depend on them | feeling like a selfless Giver masks need for approval and protection; privilege unveiled when they help someone succeed and are angry at no reward; power exerted indirectly through others; "If they win, you win," so expect preferential treatment (box seats at the inaugural, special attention at the victory ball). [p.81] |

**Focal issues [pp.81-85]:**
- **Other referencing** [pp.81-82]: Twos define themselves through others' eyes, finding identity in relationships as Threes do through work; attention is habitually split between others' feelings and their own: empathy, or confusion ("Trying to be yourself when you're at a party with all your different friends feels like being in a salad spinner").
- **Many selves** [p.82]: sense of self alters with each person, so relationships are not seen as emotionally fixed; "Should I be a soft person, an aggressive person, lighthearted, or dead serious?"; "not a matter of faking it or putting on a mask"; torn between attractions, "Which stirring is authentic?"
- **Living in feeling** [pp.82-83]: attraction is immediate "juice"; imagines scenarios; the open heart wants to talk to old people or hold a stranger's baby; decisions from feeling and facts ("I'll see how I feel when the time comes").
- **Chicken soup** [p.83]: noticing people's likes (colors that flatter, favorite sandwiches); giving can be manipulative ("If I give them something that they want, then I'll get what I want in return") but motives are usually hidden until resentment appears. "The chicken soup syndrome": nourish people and they'll be happy and appreciative.
- **Boundaries** [p.83]: Givers see what people need ("Soft people need to be hardened, aggressive people need to soften up, extroverts need social playgrounds, introverts need social help"); when heavily fixated they become immersed in the loved one's life, intrusive, "railroaded" others; underappreciated Twos can be invasive; in extreme cases they believe everything the partner owns is theirs and the family's accomplishments came from their care.
- **Giving to get** [p.84]: the helping strategy is to be attracted to a potential need, see what's needed, move in; the blind spot is that attention shifted from own needs; "I'm doing this for you" actually means working for the relationship with repressed expectations; the cry "Look what I've done for you."
- **Being alone** [p.84]: if not in a relationship "nobody's home"; "It feels like a black hole opens when I look inside. There's nothing there." Mature Twos have had long periods alone to find "the core of themselves that is steady and unchanging."
- **An unchanging self** [pp.84-85]: self anchored in how they relate, "like athletes relating to their game"; in training the game "gets inside you." As Twos mature they become impatient with being central to so many; "Which one is me?"; "Having needs feels like a fight. 'Who do I serve, you or myself?'"; working directly for own agenda is "new territory."
- **Cos** [p.85]: codependency question; she says she does not see addictions as type-related; Twos and Nines habitually merge; type differences affect motivations and healing routes (rousing anger for Nines versus anger management for Eights); "Yes. Twos can be codependent, and so can every other type."

**Security and risk [pp.86-87]:**

| | Direction | Healthy side | Low side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security | to **Four** | safe to know what you need; clear boundaries, not compelled to please; "What are my personal likes and dislikes?... authentic feelings, rather than feelings adapted to gain approval?"; forms a relationship with self; "separate at will from their emotions" | depression on seeing that being for others is easier than for yourself; feeling used and sold out; self-pity ("Poor me," "Why did I wait so long?"); grief and envy to explore: sad about what, envious of what, leading to repressed needs and "the realm of one's true emotions" |
| Risk | to **Eight** | where fighting real emotional tyranny, move to Eight can be liberating; many Twos "found themselves under adversity," knew what they needed because it was being taken away, "learned not to fuzz-out" | relentless; "righting for freedom" and "take the case to court"; punishing people for ingratitude; or shadowboxing with their own repression, resenting serving others' will while still focused on their needs |

Early stress: increased preoccupation with partner's needs and repression of own; a "hysterical emotional edge," short bursts of irritability, increasingly superficial emotional connection. Paradox: a secure relationship can precipitate anxiety for Twos, "because in order to live with someone, you have to be yourself" [p.87].

**Two in love [pp.88-93]:**

*Living with Twos (list):* wants to be the central figure ("I don't need you, but you depend on me"); learn to recognize manipulation tactics (complaints, guilt); control while appearing subservient; vulnerable to rejection; encourage authenticity; expect big emotions (anger and rising hysteria are signals of unmet needs; Twos may not know what they want but can get hysterical if they don't get it); short bursts of superficial feeling (hysterical laughter, hyperactivity, flirtation) cover insecurity; sex or affection seem equal to love; likely inexperience with real intimacy ("I can please you, but what do I really feel for you?"); allay the belief that personal will must lead to abandonment; **attracted to relationships with obstacles** (forestalls the confusion of an available intimate relationship); **triangulate**, attracted to a "great man" or inspiring woman ("muse") while in a more available romance, to hedge commitment; don't be surprised when the Two fights for freedom ("I've served your needs, what about mine?" "Go take care of yourself!"); anger when emerging real needs differ from pleasing behavior [pp.88-89].

*Intimacy [pp.89-91]:* "Appreciation is like oxygen"; buoyant, energetic when they feel needed; help is selective and specific, toward physical beauty, potentially successful people, those abused by life ("Appealing people who require help are Two bait"); "a small shift from helping to manipulating" (you feel guilty for disliking something "for your own benefit"). Anger is a hysterical, accusatory outburst about lack of appreciation, passes quickly ("No grudge. No remorse.") ; partner should fend off accusations and find the buried agenda. Outgoing, affectionate style can look seductive but is usually just social style; they may broadcast intentions to the room, causing jealousy. As a relationship deepens, neglected aspects of self reappear and "the well of need feels bottomless"; terrifying for those proud of being needed; "paramount need is to get their needs met without knowing that they're needy"; "Having needs equals rejection"; some get bored after first attraction. Successful relationships survive a period of **rebellion** in which the Two fights against restriction even by a permissive partner, learning they can follow their own will without abandonment. Young Twos are popular across circles (student leaders, rebels, jocks, nerds); rejection "feels like losing your identity" ("Who will I be without you?") [pp.90-91].

*Signals Twos send [pp.91-93]:*

| Positive | Negative | Mixed messages / Inner signals |
|---|---|---|
| make you feel special, worth the time; romantic meetings, sensitive care, "storybook flavor"; represent your interests, interface with people you need to know; exuberant aliveness; do for others what others normally do for themselves | dependent, want a lot of time; demand for emotional excitement drains; expected to be inspiring and confide; nudged into their agenda, "who calls the shots"; once wonderful, now less intriguing; life "subtly rearranged" | if your interests run counter, information is "finessed," support withdrawn ("Didn't you get the memo?"), you are moved from in group to out group, message "come to heel or make it on your own." Inner: pride hides as helpfulness; new Twos "go blank" on pride; thoughts: "I could suggest," "I could arrange"; "Christmas-morning feeling"; they think of others' needs but rarely "I will take"; helps to ask "What's in it for me?"; humbling if they love you anyway; devastating when love you could have earned goes to someone else |

**Two at work [pp.93-97]:**
- *Workplace:* identity from supportive authorities (right-hand man, the secretary who knows the secrets, "the power behind the throne"); responsive to approval, crushed by disapproval; keeps tabs on interactions (information pipeline, party coordinator); associates with "worthwhile" people; backs favorites; unrecognized conflict between ambition to be first and wanting to please; works for respect of the power elite; safety lies in pleasing authority, fears opposing power alone; may choose work valued by a loved one [p.93].
- *Leadership style:* effective, many as power behind the throne; attracted to promising directions and talent on the way up; rely on developing key people rather than meticulous structure; vision of "a flourishing, well-knit community that prospers with good care"; **in group and out group**, decisions weighted to favorites ("You know that you're being groomed for in-group membership when you get access to the leader"); image crucial, align with power; highly competitive but the image of choice is helpfulness over aggression; focus on identifying and filling client needs ("wooing the client rather than warring with competitors"); minimize reports and procedures; decisions in concert with trusted advisers; the "chamberlain role" lets them observe, advise and test the waters; advice determines what needs get met [pp.93-95].
- *Conflicts:* temperamental, thrive on recognition, irritable at implied disrespect; difficulty being kept waiting; fights at home brought to work; staff "handle" the boss and disappear on cue; "Twos not only forgive, they forget" [p.95].
- *Employee participation:* happy Twos are "a workplace treasure," represent you well, back you from behind the scenes, mobilize support, stay on top of shifting alliances; lose interest without positive feedback; the road to advancement begins with co-teaching or co-facilitating; may get prideful about contributions (an ambitious Giver: "I personally have no interest in appearances, but I spend at least a third of my free time shopping so that I'll represent the firm well"); need strong support at the beginning of a venture, "space-out" when first alone in the spotlight [pp.95-96].
- *Team building:* a team of equals is a bind (liked vs. special attention); more often competitive with peers, seeking special attention from someone higher; the real hook is "emotional juice," whether someone of significance cares about the outcome; best work when positioned to "suggest, prod, cajole, and extend a project that is of great concern to others" [pp.96-97]. Conflict resolution cross-referenced to Part III [p.95].

### Chapter 3. Three: The Performer [pp.99-121]

**Box [p.99]:** mental bias *vanity* / essence quality *hope*; emotional bias *deceit* / *honesty*. Subtype focus: **masculine/feminine image** (sexual), **prestige** (social), **security** (self-survival). Worldview: "The world values a champion. I must avoid failure."

**Spiritual path [pp.99-100]:** the preoccupation with performance points to a search for **hope**. Threes place hope in their own efforts rather than in work aligned with universal principles; vanity elevates achievement; the achiever's image mimics honest effort carried by universal energies; deceit means maintaining an image of success in others' eyes. Hope and honest effort are "both a psychological growth edge ... and a potential access point to higher awareness."

**Concerns [p.100]:** loved for performance rather than self; has to be first; efficiency, product, goals, results; competes only when winning is assured, "leaves before the loss"; self-presentation alters to enhance effectiveness; self-deception (takes on feelings that match the public image); heart is in work; chameleon; confuses real identity and job identity; trouble accessing deep emotion; free time brings anxiety (vacations packed with activity); looks to image, status, objects for happiness; "the instant expert," overvalues own ability; **polyphasic thinking** (many tasks at once); tunnel vision and anger when interrupted; effective leaders, packagers, promoters, captains of winning teams.

**Personality bias (inside view) [pp.100-102]:** each type focuses on a special sector of information; Threes on high-profile achievement; everyone shifts to performance mode with a juicy project. Threes "were loved for their achievements rather than for what they felt"; learned to compete, handle several jobs, promote themselves; "If the way to love is being a winner, you learn to project a winning facade." Image is tailored to results rather than needs; without "the rudder of a strong emotional life" they take cues from others. When automatic, self-observation stops and they can't distinguish their feelings from those that come with a role. Threes grow by seeing themselves as separate from their image; helped by people who reframe the goal "from emotional appearance to emotional depth," are patient with slipping away from feeling, and "offer loyalty to the person without buying into a convincing facade" [pp.101-102].

**Subtype focus [pp.102-104]:**

| Arena | Deceit as acted out | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual: **Masculine/feminine image** | becoming the prototype of what a mate finds pleasing | masters of appearance; "A top producer. A strong contender. An attentive lover. The ideal mate."; emphasis on form and surface; research the perfect romantic dinner spots; makes conquest a task, "put out hooks and see if they take"; role-model of whatever a partner finds appealing and deceive themselves that the feelings are genuine; confusion when the trend doesn't fit (wearing spandex when not a runner; John Wayne macho vs. New Age soft male). [p.102] |
| Social: **Prestige** | shifting self-presentation to attract social attention | anonymity makes them anxious; "confusion between being the best and being the best known"; wants public visibility, titles, honors, influential connections ("How many letters come after your name?"); says what the group wants to hear; "like an actor beginning to live out a part"; different sets of clothes for different groups (symphony or driving a truck). [p.103] |
| Survival: **Security** | security that money can buy | many skills to ensure job security; even those from wealth are terrified of being incapacitated; emotional survival attached to earnings; self-worth equals material worth; projecting affluence (costly home, travel, designer clothes); losing assets feels life-threatening; unbooked time produces high anxiety; "You'll relax after the next deal, the next promotion, the next raise." [pp.103-104] |

**Focal issues [pp.104-108]:**
- **Chameleon** [p.104]: they know what others want them to look like and become it; can change identity "in midsentence"; take "the protective coloration of an image that the group respects."
- **Polyphasic activity** [pp.104-105]: several tasks at once (the trip from kitchen to cellar); efficiency is energizing; spend little time on details; "Doing is a form of control."
- **Competition** [p.105]: play to win; a good opponent galvanizes; strength is the job gets done, liability is "a faulty feedback system"; people reduced to what they can do for the project; "Don't mess with me when I'm working," "I'll call you when I'm done." Negative feedback makes them work harder; positive makes them speed up.
- **Personal marketability** [pp.105-106]: "North American culture is largely Three" (rewards youth, vitality, competitive marketing, media propaganda); other-directed Threes market themselves like a product; not lies about facts but resume spin; follow a partner's interests and become "a living expert."
- **Can do** [p.106]: need a clear course of action and goal; fury at interruption covers deep anxiety about losing control; "I know the answer. No problem. Can do."; attention to final result; "Steps one through nine are blurred when you're already at ten"; "Moving into fast-forward, Threes outrun their anxiety."
- **Fraudulence** [pp.106-107]: image is deceptive and protective; shields from rejection; Threes look ahead and give little attention to past regrets; self-aware Threes can tell a leader from a bluffer; the moment of truth is discovering they live through a persona ("a sense of being a phony"); it is "a significant moment when Performers become themselves in public"; they can spot a phony because they see the impact of surface appeal.
- **Efficiency** [p.107]: security in doing; goals and lists; "choreographed rolling up to the stop sign"; hard to sit and talk; lunch as a sandwich in the car; life is passing by.
- **The hurry habit** [pp.107-108]: when a project picks speed, attention narrows and doubt vanishes; the project can dominate priorities and decide who stays in your life; mind split; "the key word is stop."

**Security and risk [pp.108-110]:**

| | Direction | Healthy side | Low side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security | to **Six** | discovery of real feelings, empathy, emotional loyalty; learns to reconsider, ponder, wait; forgoes quick-fix mentality for authentic creative ideas | doubt of feelings ("Maybe they're not genuine," "Is this what I'm supposed to feel?"); dependent on partner to boost image; wants adoration but suspicious of it; fear of being unmasked |
| Risk | to **Nine** | being unable to function at usual pitch, Threes pin hopes on someone else; reconsideration of the childhood conclusion that only winners are worthy of love; many report becoming fully committed to the relationship during a business crisis; "being loved for one's being rather than one's doing, can become a lived reality in stress" | spacing out by throwing energy at trivia (trashy novels, TV, endless chores); looks numb; "Who am I if I can't produce?"; most vulnerable in morning and night; blames the fates ("This is not my doing") |

Paradox: Threes become more tentative and afraid in a secure relationship; they tend to add a relationship to their overtime; the shift to security occurs at a partner's behest, after tragedy, or when convinced emotional life is worthwhile [pp.108-109]. Early stress: attention narrows to the task, then "hyperdrive" ("I get home at midnight and leave by seven"); in failure they switch to any direction that keeps momentum; if no exit, they go from big projects to small tasks [p.109].

**Three in love [pp.110-115]:**

*Living with Threes (list):* feel loved for achievement; frame the relationship as an "important task" to be built; expect appreciation for a winning image and style; tendency to "do" feelings (activity replaces affect; the perfect lover with a script of endearing things to say); help them slow down long enough to be affected by intimate feelings; intolerant of darker emotions ("Let's stay energetic and happy," "Let's have fun"); if taking responsibility for your negative feelings ("What should I do to make you happy?"), hold out that there is no quick solution to pain; confuse ideas about emotions with the real thing ("Tell me what I should feel"); anxious when activity is suspended; need assurance of being loved for themselves, not as "the prototype of the perfect mate"; heart is in work, need a strong push to take time off [pp.110-111].

*Intimacy [pp.111-113]:* "Threes have a tendency to do feelings": watching how faces change in sorrow or love and behaving that way while the mind runs lists; doing is less threatening than "turning attention inward and finding an empty place where authentic feelings are supposed to bubble up." They promote "upbeat, active love" convincingly: "Love is doing things together, love is productive and happy. Love is not supposed to be overwhelming or evocative or painful." Negative emotion triggers action ("Let's not wallow"); voluntary suffering is "a total mystery"; frightened when a partner surrenders to sadness ("Nothing good can come of this"). Dilemma: "afraid of rejection if you don't produce, and rejected when you do" (come home late and tired, unappreciated). Helpful: schedule intimacy ("made to order for Threes"), hanging out with no agenda, a walk with no running shoes; "How do you do being?" Intimate life takes a "picture-book quality" (cute couple, ideal family, activities schedule). Significants assume complete access to feelings; when real feelings develop, "a great turning point," things get worse before better; "Who am I without my roles?" Role also protects (a breakup rejects the role); private self feels fraudulent [pp.111-113].

*Signals Threes send [pp.113-115]:*

| Positive | Negative | Mixed messages / Inner signals |
|---|---|---|
| optimistic worldview ("We can make it happen"), enthusiastic and encouraging; "hope in action"; practical, dedicated; responsible, stay with it, willing to do the most | seem not to care about your feelings; artificial or superficial; things and goals matter more than people; difficulty relaxing and on vacation; cut corners on relationship time, softening the truth with partial truths; let personal friends disappear while work friends stay | most productive when least self-aware, "achieve robotically"; happiness equated with recognition and owning what others want; confusion between pleasure of things and emotional satisfaction; moving on before absorbing pleasure; helps if the relationship is framed as an important task with goals and results. Inner: "I know what I'm doing. Fill me in as we go along"; disagreement sounds like interference ("That's negative thinking"); criticism makes them push harder ("These people are jealous"); energy tied to an image makes the image seem worthwhile regardless of merit; self-aware Threes ask "Will this goal serve me, or does it serve my image?" |

**Three at work [pp.115-121]:**
- *Workplace:* assumes ability ("instant expert"); "I am what I do"; prototype of the profession; saves time even by cutting corners ("Details later"); expansionist until opposed, then parlays options; rage when tasks are interrupted (task-specific); product over process ("How much did I produce?"); respected for ability more than liked; "machinelike achiever," expects the same of others; high-profile image (credentials, "who's who"); competes for leadership; intolerant of ambiguous returns; selective attention to positive feedback, intolerant of criticism, places responsibility elsewhere if failure occurs; avoids failure by switching tracks; trouble telling admiration as leader from being liked [pp.115-116].
- *Leadership style:* leadership is the preferred position ("Let's go. I can do it. Why are you tired?"); "The typical American Three does business like a football quarterback"; like chameleons they adapt to any milieu: a Japanese Three will espouse process-oriented Deming-style facilitation; "If the people want a fighter, a Three climbs into the ring." Tunnel vision; "You can't afford to doubt yourself at high speed"; any risk worth it "if you get there fast and first"; instead of slowing under pressure they "expand and franchise"; "Just Do It!"; delegate assuming everyone will cut corners; quantity over quality; experiment beautifully with known ideas but are not usually original innovators; "apply known ideas to new situations and package them very well" [pp.116-117].
- *Classic conflicts:* expect everyone equally motivated; interruption threatens them so they want to fire the troublemaker or leave; bad-news format: reaffirm the goal and encapsulate the problem in few words ("We're on schedule on every front except for one sector"); power struggles over a single leadership post; design cooperative leadership structure from the start; "A power vacuum in leadership invites warfare" [pp.117-118].
- *Employee participation:* classic rewards (bonus, titles); aware of status differences; happiest with stated goals, earned reward, advancement; "greyhounds straining on the leash"; nonrecognition incites competition ("I should be up there doing that lecture"); may manipulate the system when advancement is blocked; learn best when material serves a function ("What can I do with this?"), learn on their feet, raise a hand before knowing what to say; may close down options prematurely; succeed at long-term projects staged in rewarded chunks (awards dinner, newsletter article) [pp.118-119].
- *Team building:* take over a team when spheres of influence are not designated; volunteer, set up brainstorming, work late; "the activity flurry is more about recognition than about the project"; helpless when others take charge; enroll by showing how the goal furthers their interests; specialists rather than generalists; surpass themselves in prestigious company; not detail oriented, pair with conscientious people; "can amplify the value of results without noticing a subtle exaggeration"; "shoot for the middle of whatever their group values"; sell and promote well, "change hats several times on the same idea"; impatient with people who need time to speculate; rarely absent due to illness; feelings expressed through team spirit; "when trouble hits, they dig in and work harder; and when the team wins, they organize the victory dinner" [pp.119-121].

### Chapter 4. Four: The Romantic [pp.123-142]

**Box [p.123]:** mental bias *melancholy* / essence quality *original source*; emotional bias *envy* / *equanimity (balance)*. Subtype focus: **competition** (sexual), **shame** (social), **dauntless (reckless)** (self-survival). Worldview: "Something is missing. Others have it. I have been abandoned."

**Spiritual path [pp.123-124]:** melancholy is "a sweet sadness based on the perception of loss"; the child lost connection with essence when attention turned to survival and became acutely sensitive to human abandonment; longing for authentic bonds swamps equanimity in "the pitch and roll of dramatic moods"; envy reminds that others seem to enjoy happiness denied; the search for authentic connection "mimics an ongoing awareness of essence"; "We would not be seeking if we were complete."

**Concerns [p.124-125]:** attracted to what's missing (distant, unavailable, hard to get); envy = belief that others possess the missing element ("They are happy," "They feel loved"); melancholy is "sweet and evocative"; abandonment supports low self-esteem ("I would not have been abandoned if I were worthier"); mood, manners, luxury, good taste as supports; "feels different from other people, which shifts to feeling unique"; longs for the absent lover, distant friend, communion with God; impatient with mediocrity, "flat" ordinary feelings; "amps up" through loss, fantasy, artistic connection, dramatic acts; push-pull relationships ("When can we have the spark again?"); gifts: sensitivity, depth, supporting people in crisis and pain.

**Personality bias (inside view) [pp.125-126]:** Romantics are absorbed in an emotional world; "You connect through pleasure and pain." **Envy** is "the knife's twist in the heart when others enjoy the happiness that you long for": not jealousy, "the sight of happiness reminds you that you're not happy"; fuels the search for objects and status (money, lifestyle, recognition, mates); acted out as a cycle of **desire, acquisition, disappointment, rejection**; relationships that passed to the stage of rejection begin to seem attractive again. Selective focus on the positive aspects of distant relationships; current relationships seem pale beside an absent figure. Fours grow by seeing "a half-full glass," by feeling they have enough; helped by significants who stay calm during push-pull, see the good in the here and now, "stand fast during intense emotional tides."

### Chapter 5. Five: The Observer [pp.143-166]

**Box [p.143]:** mental bias *stinginess* / essence quality *omniscience*; emotional bias *avarice (greed)* / *nonattachment*. Subtype focus: **confidence** (sexual), **totems** (social), **castle (home)** (self-survival). Worldview: "The world is invasive. I need privacy to think and to refuel my energies."

**Spiritual path [pp.143-144]:** omniscience is "an accurate knowing that appears before any data is analyzed," not mediated by intellect; a Five's lifelong attachment to information and private study mimics it. Stinginess toward self and others guarantees independence ("when one has fewer needs, one feels less pressure to reach out"); avarice develops for the resources of private survival (knowledge, money, energy, time). The grasp of reality by intellect is a child's attempt to detach from painful emotion and preserve a fragile connection to the nonattachment of mind that leads back to essence.

**Concerns [pp.144-145]:** craves privacy to recharge; likes boundaries, precise agreement, defined time/agenda/responsibilities; "knowledge is power"; minimalist; greedy for the few necessities of mental survival (private space, knowledge, bare necessities); compartmentalizes (friends from one interest may never meet those of another); drained by open-ended events and people's expectations and emotions; likes noninvolvement ("troublesome, since both love and hate require involvement"); unhooks from emotion, confusable with spiritual nonattachment; emotions best felt in privacy; outside-observer point of view.

**Personality bias (inside view) [pp.145-146]:** "We all adopt the Five worldview during times of scarcity": we shrink expectations; "Less produces more: more time, more energy, and more autonomy"; "silent abundance of mind." "An Observer's home is like a castle. Low visibility, controlled contact, and uninterrupted private time." The mind is a companion and a refuge that cannot be invaded. If a necessity is short, Fives develop "a tenacious desire to acquire that commodity"; because they dislike having needs, "that fury feeds their desire"; "Avarice is an angry desire to possess, a greed that overrides detachment. You get so hungry that you have to reach out." Caught between emotional emptiness and fear of being engulfed. Fives grow by joining mind with emotion and finding passion; helped by significants who relate without inserting their own emotional agenda, respect time, privacy and space, point out over-intellectualization, and make self-disclosure safe [p.146].

**Subtype focus [pp.146-148]:**

| Arena | Avarice as acted out | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual: **Confidence** | confidential understandings as private bonds, kept separate from each other | lust for "intense, brief, highly meaningful encounters"; key disclosures and emotional ties that endure separation; confidants are the few with an "understanding" (private adviser, the personal moment, the secret love affair); bonds are "mental treasures" re-created in imagination, so "a love enshrined in memory does not fade"; drawn to sexual expression as the antithesis of intellectualism; loneliness a by-product ("You can read and think and imagine just so long, and then you crave experience"); devastating when a confidence goes public. [pp.146-147] |
| Social: **Totems** | totems of a tribe as links between vast forces and the limited human mind; "power information" | attracted to ideas and people who influence the culture, systems that explain human behavior, "the pivotal formula that governs a field"; gather in chess clubs, math departments, Yoga centers, musical events; will not jockey for place, want to be called to the inner circle, refine thinking with "people who know" without small talk; drawn to big-picture systems (political prediction, stock market analysis, psychoanalysis, the Enneagram); "Knowledge is power and forewarned is forearmed." [pp.147-148] |
| Survival: **Castle (home)** | reduce contacts and possessions | minimalists; small luxuries seem extravagant (dessert may lead to gluttony: "Just one sip of piping hot coffee"); pride in doing with little; the home as sanctuary from prying eyes; most store critical information rather than money; "Economy equals independence"; stingy with themselves even in plenty; take pleasure in abstinence, since more means entanglement. [pp.148-149] |

**Focal issues [pp.149-152]:**
- **Commitment** [p.149]: "Quitting is easy"; they measure depth of commitment by "I can still do without this"; "The hardest decision is deciding to stay. Staying means opening yourself to pain"; when Fives commit to love "they commit themselves to struggle" and "the certainty of loss"; "love has less to do with romance than with deciding that a certain person is worth the price of pain."
- **Renunciation** [pp.149-150]: needs appear as ideas, passions are conceptual rather than felt; living in the mind produces an illusion of nonattachment; "true renunciation implies giving up a tangible investment."
- **Secret lives** [p.150]: happy to conform to expected roles but cherish the idea of doing things no one would imagine; compartmentalize into sectors that don't overlap ("you have a piece of yourself invested in every sector, but nobody gets it all"); ways to preserve privacy: withhold feelings, censor information, separate thinking from emotion, confidential bonding, secret lives; "hardly ever anything illicit about the secret."
- **Loneliness** [pp.150-151]: no loneliness when solitude is voluntary; "privacy feels good and safe as long as there's someone on the other side of the door"; when nobody knocks, "privacy can feel like a prison"; to reengage you must initiate and disclose.

- **Detachment** [pp.151-152]: Fives can go public and still be distant; "You can be far away even when you're in front a crowd"; "a split in awareness"; self-descriptions: "It's like seeing myself in a three-way mirror," "A part of myself isn't attached to me"; detachment elevated to a virtue, "Lust is for lesser beings... Detach before you get hooked."

- **Avarice** [pp.152-153]: panelists "look blank" at the avarice passion ("Me? Not me! I can do with very little"). **Five at work [pp.160-166]:**
- *Workplace:* sense of limited energy reserves; works for privacy and autonomy ("Works to buy autonomy"); needs predictability (minutes of last meeting, names of next attendees); finds it hard to concentrate with others around; freezes when questioned unexpectedly; strictly avoids conflict ("a wall of memos and secretaries"); values unemotional decision making, sees through flattery and charismatic leadership; very productive in a decision role protected from frontline interactions [pp.160-161].

### Chapter 6. Six: The Trooper [pp.167-184, in progress; chapter continues in the next part]

**Box [p.167]:** mental bias *cowardice/doubt* / essence quality *faith*; emotional bias *fear* / *courage*. Subtype focus: **strength/beauty** (sexual), **duty** (social), **affection (warmth)** (self-survival). Worldview: "The world is a threatening place. I question authority."

**Spiritual path [pp.167-168]:** doubt involves a loss of faith; the child reacted with fear to losing the security of essence; fear creates dependency on rules and protective authority, which "mimics the certainty of faith." The Fear type parallels the **Doubting Mind** of Buddhist practice (attention shifts from belief to intense internal questioning); cowardice and doubt are the natural starting place to develop faith.

**Concerns [p.168]:** thinking replaces doing, procrastination ("image of the frozen rabbit"); inaction increases anxiety, imagining the worst, fight or flight: **counterphobic** Six (confrontational, daring) charges, **phobic** Six (furtive, frightened) flees; fear manifests as strength/beauty, duty, affection; high goals with history of incompletion; anxiety peaks with success (exposure to hostile forces); amnesia about success and pleasure; often successful in an underdog cause ("the loyal companion in adversity, the business turnaround"); ambivalent about authority (seeks protection from or rebels against); questions motives; leads the opposition party; afraid of own and others' anger; asks hard questions; "Yes, but..."; excellent troubleshooter; scans for signs of safety and threat ("personal radar"). Gift: recognizing others' motives, needs and inner worlds.

**Personality bias (inside view) [p.169]:** "We all adopt the Six perspective when we feel endangered": war zone, dark streets, a cliff on the mountain road; adrenaline forces fight or flight; phobic Sixes hide and submit when cornered; counterphobics, "equally afraid," challenge fear aggressively. Six children learned to question authority and "look for what you really mean"; they search hidden intentions "as intently as Threes seek image and success. Sixes doubt authority, while Threes take on leadership roles." Success is frightening: "Visibility equals attack." Sixes grow by regaining faith in people and learning to trust; they have a choice when they can separate mental fears from real danger and watch hope rise then turn to doubt; helped by significants who offer reassurance, "remain steadfast when the future looks doubtful," and "are faithful to their word."

**Subtype focus [pp.169-172]:**

| Arena | Fear as acted out | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual: **Strength and beauty** | a show of power as a mask over inner doubt, to attract and command allegiance | caring makes you feel helpless ("What if they don't love me?"); craving reassurance feels humiliating; doubting people's sincerity ("They were being kind"); respects power of all kinds (ideas that command allegiance, aesthetics that capture minds); a beautiful, wise environment gives reassuring feedback; acted out by cultivating strength or beauty, a string of lovers, or dominating intellectualism ("A formidable intellect. A fierce opponent."); the motive for being strong or beautiful is "anxiety management"; "Fear evaporates when significants look up to us"; intertwined with counterphobic risk-taking; more obvious in phobic Sixes against a timid style. [pp.169-170] |
| Social: **Duty** | contain fear through mutual obligation | needs of the group govern behavior; self-doubt lessens when opinions are backed by collective authority; devoted to family, underdog causes, "islands of sanity" (communes, political leagues, self-help groups, the church); compulsive form is ritual obligatory duty spurred by guilt and fear of abandonment ("Following the rules ensures your place in the group"); capable of enormous self-sacrifice; most effective before a situation becomes fully successful ("us against them"); heroic in a turnaround; "It's easy to act for the cause, but a personal success can be alarming." [pp.170-171] |
| Survival: **Affection (warmth)** | fear disappears in the company of friends | safe with people who like them; ongoing request for reassurance ("Do you still love me?", "What are you thinking now?"); silence encourages doubt ("Why didn't I get a call?"), imagination becomes fact, "Sixes don't realize that they're scaring themselves," then shocked by the cheery call; warm Sixes devoted to friendship: disarm anger, stick up for friends ("We're in it together"); a change in affection is "like being thrown back into a hostile world"; **difference from Two:** both move toward people to feel safe, "Twos gain identity by pleasing. **Focal issues (in range) [pp.172-176]:**
- **Projection** [pp.172-173]: vigilance; faces and gestures seem to answer "Do you like me? Am I safe?"; "All nine types project to some extent... but projection is almost synonymous with the fearful tendency." Anecdote: a Six engineer, wife (a One) asleep in the car while teen daughter drives fast; he fumes at her imagined silent judgment, accuses "Why do you always have to be critical?" and she answers "But Henry, I've been sleeping since we left the party."
- **Certainty** [pp.173-174]: doubts soften if someone else goes first (being on the receiving end is safer); consistency essential ("Untruth triggers suspicion and is very hard to forgive"); suspicious of moodiness and mixed intentions; a partner's change of feeling is taken personally ("Why are you mad at me?"), the Six pushes for reassurance exactly when a stressed partner is least able to give it. "The unknown is paralyzing"; reassure by getting the basics in order: voice each fear, then locate money, car, a place to stay; finally imagine the best-case outcomes aloud.
- **Doubt** [pp.174-175]: inner questioning, searching under face value ("A lovely face, striking clothes, a polished manner and what?"); easily stereotyped as negative thinking; Sixes "bump into telephone poles because they're preoccupied" ("An earthquake? Where? I missed it. But look at that interesting face!"); helps to get a reality check: are these perceptions false ideas, intuitions, projections or doubts?
- **The devil's advocate** [p.175]: doubt yields discernment; "paranoid attention" joke: "Hello. How are you?" examined for secret meaning ("Why do you want to know?"); "Doubt is usually an inquiry about intentions"; mental precision, impressive troubleshooters, see holes in arguments and the context; "Most of all they see intention."
- **Action** [pp.175-176]: doubt creates depression ("It wouldn't work anyway, so why try?") that, unlike biological depression, lifts with a call to action; mobilize without being an oppressive authority ("The best medicine is a good example. Carry out your part and leave the Sixes' part undone"); Sixes are rarely proactive, going first into success feels like walking into a trap, encouragement can feel like a setup, compliments can mean being set up as an authority; reduce areas of uncertainty ("Reaction is much easier than proaction").
- **Authority** [p.176]: Troopers remember negative authority figures, overestimate others' power and doubt their own; need to account to authority, ask permission; gravitate to protective marriages and institutions (army, church); "submission leads to rebellion"; both phobic (submissive) and counterphobic (rebellious) styles overvalue others' power; Sixes need to "stand on their own" and deal with fear of aggression.

**Security and risk [pp.177-179]:**

| | Direction | Healthy side | Low side |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security | to **Nine** | mind lets go, thought gets quiet, body awareness returns ("like returning to your senses"); opens to people and environment; "feels the goodness of people, not as an idea but as a tangible reality"; sexuality, pleasure, companionship more available; sports help distinguish body from mind | "losing your edge": feeling afloat, spacy; loss of initiative, less adrenaline and interest, "It looks peaceful on the outside. Inside there's not much happening"; fear of accommodating to peacefulness and losing motivation; a radiologist who believed his skill improved when slightly paranoid |
| Risk | to **Three** | counterphobic can't stand waiting and acts fast, energizing if attention goes to the task (a test, a child to be born, a challenge); "relaxing into the energy instead of tensing up" | dread without basis in fact; head-down, "walking in small circles"; want to be rescued; fight or flight at critical tension; adrenaline charges a worst-case scenario; stuttering start-stop pattern between mustering up and caving in, ending in a crash; running scared |

Fear "isn't nearly as attractive as some of the other passions. There's no exciting ego inflation," so for Six self-development brings immediate relief [p.177].

**Six in love [pp.179-182]:**

*Living with Sixes (list):* question your intentions and positive regard; loyal ally, "us against the world," devoted supporter; want reassurance ("Will you always love me?": "There's no right answer for this one"); project personal dissatisfaction (deny their own wandering eye by "seeing" that you are attracted to someone else); identify with problem areas of relationship; want to affect you rather than be affected (through warmth, dutiful alliance, or sexual power); frightening to have their own desires aroused; show strength by assisting others, capable of self-sacrifice; can't always locate the source of tension in intimacy; search for clues in your behavior ("How do you act toward other people?") [pp.179-180].

*Intimacy [pp.180-182]:* strong in ideas and imagination, weak in follow-through; a romance becomes doubtful; "If you go ahead, you're going to be hurt. If you don't, you'll miss out"; "It's safer not to want when you couldn't have it anyway"; look ambivalent from outside; can be fully committed (physically, financially, emotionally) and still doubt; take pride in seeing through image and glamour. "Periodic doubt does not mean that a Six is leaving"; **doubt can be "a way to stay in,"** a way to coast without anxiety. Concerns must be aired or "they become fact in the mind of the Six." "Will you always love me?" can mean "I love you now. Is it safe?" or "Have you changed your mind since this morning?" Partner as "the couple's memory" during a doubt attack: restate the exact terms of commitment; "Don't exaggerate. Don't plead. Don't coax. In a crisis of faith, Sixes need a consistent story." The result is many stable long-term marriages; reassured Sixes carry more than their share, are low-maintenance, unusually loyal ("Someone who's like family," "One of ours"); attached to an individual, not an image or status; sacrifice. A scared Six: fight or flight; fighting Sixes are angry because they were fooled ("You see? I should never have trusted you"); anger fuels "nervous grandiosity," can be bullies, bait people ("How angry will you get?"); the partner's best move: reaffirm quietly ("I loved you last week. Nothing has changed from my side..."), because the Six "went full out. They got angry, and the worst didn't happen" [pp.181-182].

**Six at work (partial; range ends mid-section, PDF p.184) [pp.184-185]:** In the workplace (list begins): strong analytic powers, questioning the opposite position; **overvalues authority's power** and feels weakened by comparison; reacts to own weakness by seeking protection from authority (**loyalist**) or trying to bring it down (**rebel**): "At your feet or at your throat"; tries for superhero status to compensate for anxiety, "Toughing it out. Braced against fear"; able to go full out against the odds, defends the underdog, business turnaround; tests an argument, "the loyal opposition," "Let's consider the other side"; **action paralysis** as success materializes and when there is no opposition; tends to diminish a powerful success (blowing it, losing time, losing the critical file in a computer crash) from the sense of endangerment in the exposed successful stance; difficulty locating the source of tension connected to success ("Why don't I feel the pleasure of a win?").
