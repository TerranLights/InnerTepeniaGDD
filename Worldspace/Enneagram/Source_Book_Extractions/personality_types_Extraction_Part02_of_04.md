## Overview of this range

Each type chapter in the book follows the same template: a one-page **profile** (Healthy / Average / Unhealthy summary, Key Motivations, Examples), an **Overview** (the type's place in its center, its core problem, its childhood "parental orientation," its characteristic problem with hostility/anxiety), the **nine Levels of Development** grouped as Healthy (1 to 3), Average (4 to 6) and Unhealthy (7 to 9), **The Dynamics** (direction of disintegration and direction of integration), **The Major Subtypes** (the two wings), and **Some Final Thoughts**. The authors treat the levels as a continuum along which a person slides down or climbs up, with each level's behavior growing out of fear of losing what the level above provided. Four is the last-but-one of the Feeling Triad (Two, Three, Four), whose shared problem is hostility and a rejected "real self"; Five and Six (with Seven) belong to the Thinking Triad, whose shared problem is anxiety and insecurity (the Thinking-Triad theory is developed in the Five and Six chapters; see below). [s.16, s.17, s.18]

Ethical note from the text: the authors stress throughout that the levels describe tendencies, not moral rankings, and that unhealthy descriptions are warnings of where the pattern leads, not sentences on any person. [note: paraphrase of the book's framing across the chapters.]

# CHAPTER 6 — TYPE FOUR: THE INDIVIDUALIST [s.16]

## The Four in profile (the book's summary page) [s.16]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Self-aware, introspective, engaged in a "search for self," aware of feelings and inner impulses. Sensitive and intuitive to self and others: gentle, tactful, compassionate. Highly personal, individualistic, true to feelings; self-revealing, emotionally honest, humane. Ironic view of self and life; can be serious and funny, vulnerable and emotionally strong. **At their best:** profoundly creative, expressing the personal and the universal, possibly in a work of art; inspired, self-renewing, regenerating; able to transform all experience into something valuable: redemptive and self-creative. |
| Average | Artistic, romantic orientation; build a beautiful, aesthetic environment to cultivate and prolong feelings. Heighten reality through fantasy, passionate feeling, imagination; long for the idealized partner. To stay in touch with feelings they interiorize and personalize things: self-absorbed, hypersensitive, shy, self-conscious, temperamental, moody; play "hard to get" yet feel like outsiders. Feel different and therefore exempt from living as everyone else until their emotional needs are met; melancholy dreamers, disdainful, decadent, sensual; self-pity and envy lead to self-indulgence; increasingly impractical, unproductive, pretentious, waiting for a rescuer. |
| Unhealthy | When dreams fail: self-inhibiting, angry at self, depressed, alienated, blocked, emotionally paralyzed, ashamed, fatigued, unable to function; withdrawn to protect self-image and buy time. Tormented by delusional self-contempt, self-reproach, self-hatred, morbid thoughts; blame others and drive away helpers. Despair, hopelessness, self-destruction (alcohol, drugs); in the extreme, emotional breakdown or suicide. |

**Key Motivations:** want to be themselves, to express themselves in something beautiful, to find the ideal partner, to withdraw to protect their feelings, to take care of emotional needs before anything else. [s.16]

**Examples** (the book's list, abridged): Tennessee Williams, Rudolf Nureyev, Ingmar Bergman, J. D. Salinger, Bob Dylan, Joni Mitchell, Martha Graham, Maria Callas, Edgar Allan Poe, Judy Garland, Michael Jackson, Yukio Mishima, Marcel Proust, Virginia Woolf; fictional "Blanche DuBois" and "Laura Wingfield." [s.16]

## Overview of the Four [s.16]

- **Opening epigraph (Winnicott via Storr):** the artist has two co-existing urges, the urgent need to communicate and the still more urgent need not to be found; creative work lets the inner world be accepted as significant and is the "personal style" of the maker. The authors take communication and concealment as the two motives particularly suited to the Four, "the artistic temperament among the personality types," though anyone of any type may become an artist. Fours take an artistic or aesthetic orientation to life to find out who they are and what about them is significant. [s.16]
- **In the Feeling Triad.** Four emphasizes the subjective world of feelings: creativity and individualism, introversion and self-absorption, self-torment and self-hatred. Fours are "the most self-aware of the types," which is the basis of both their best and worst. The constant conflict: the need to be aware of themselves in order to find themselves versus the need to move beyond self-awareness so as not to be trapped in self-consciousness. Creativity resolves the tension: in the creative moment a healthy Four harnesses emotion without getting lost in it, and is "both most themselves and most liberated from themselves." Inspiration is hard to sustain because it requires first transcending the self, which threatens the self-image. Only by learning not to look for themselves will they find and renew themselves. [s.16]
- **Average Fours** try to understand themselves by introspecting on feelings, become so self-conscious that subjective emotional states become the dominant reality, and communicate feelings *indirectly and symbolically* rather than directly. Overall direction is inward, toward self-absorption; they withdraw from life to trace "the labyrinth of their emotions," and so have noticeable difficulty coping. Because they identify with feelings they look for intensity in everything ("the more intensely they feel something, the more real they feel"): they use imagination to "stir up" emotional life, dwell for hours on a transitory encounter to extract its "emotional juice," and find moods hard to sustain amid real interaction or practical needs. They eventually interact only with the few who support their identity, cutting themselves off from "the wellspring of their feelings and their creativity—participation in the world." [s.16]
- **Healthy Fours** are "the bridge between the spiritual and the animal in human nature": aware of the depths humans can descend to and the heights they can be swept up to ("spiritual animals occupying an uneasy place between two orders of existence"). By delving into their own depths they create things that move others, transcend self and fuse personal conflicts and divergent feelings into art. [s.16]
- **Unhealthy Fours** have closed themselves off from other influences; alienated even from themselves, they see their search for self has led into "useless fantasies and illusions," and turn to hate and torment themselves, destroying what they have become. [s.16]

### Problems with Identity [s.16]
- Fours cannot transcend self-consciousness because they want more consciousness of states and feelings to arrive at a firm identity; but more self-consciousness draws them into unresolved, contradictory, irrational feelings they want to sort out *before* expressing.
- Their sense of self is never strong enough to sustain identity, particularly when they need to assert themselves; feelings change readily so identity is "not solid, dependable, in their own hands"; they feel like "a gathering cloud which may produce something of great power or merely dissipate in the next breeze." Something is missing in the self.
- Dilemma: they may not know what they feel until they have expressed it; but expressing all risks exposure to shame or punishment; not expressing leads to endless self-absorption ("aware of being aware of themselves"), ending in illusions, regrets, a wasted life.
- **Identity built from taste and negation.** Likes, dislikes and emotional reactions become building blocks ("I only wear black." "I listen to classical music, never country and western."). Most such traits work by negation: they may not know who they are but are sure of who they are *not*. Over time this paints them into a corner ("I'm a poet. I can't work in an office.").
- Identity is held by a continuous inner dialogue and reference to emotional reactions. They want validation, but are less dependent on others' affirmation than Twos or Threes; much of their identity is tied to feelings about *not having* affirmation. Feeling different and misunderstood is as central to the Four's self-image as being only good and loving is to the Two's or being a totally competent "winner" is to the Three's. [s.16]

### Parental Orientation [s.16]
- Fours are **disconnected from both parents** ("I am not like my mother; I am not like my father"). Childhood may have been unhappy or solitary (marital problems, divorce, illness, personality conflict) or relatively normal; either way they did not see themselves reflected in either parent; advice seemed to address a generic child. Lacking role models, they turned inward to feelings and imagination as the source of information for building an identity.
- They felt essentially alone and felt rejected or ignored, so concluded something was deeply wrong with them, defective because they lacked nurturing attention. Self-knowledge became the goal and means of self-esteem. But self-consciousness alienates them, arouses aggression toward self and others (parents especially); feeling powerless, they withdraw and turn aggression against themselves.
- Because the formative relationship was disconnection, ego identity is based on **difference**: they inventory the ways they are unlike others; "being unique" becomes one of the only stable building blocks, so being "ordinary" is frightening. They cling to differentness while envying and resenting those with a more normal existence.
- Their disconnection produces a longing for the **"good parent,"** usually experienced as longing for an ideal mate who will see them as they truly are and validate the constructed self. They project the role onto new acquaintances, idealize, fantasize; on getting to know the person they become disenchanted (the other's "blemishes" become the focus), lose interest, and go back to the search with more desperation. [s.16]

### Problems with Hostility and Despair [s.16]
- Like Twos and Threes, Fours have a hostility problem, directed at themselves, because they have rejected the real self for an idealized one; self-awareness makes them forever conscious of the gap, and they disdain real qualities as barriers to the imagined self. They inhibit and punish themselves.
- Toward others: rage if the self-image or emotional states are questioned or dismissed; expressed by "dropping" people suddenly and without explanation, or by sarcastic, withering remarks from their creativity; also hostility toward idealized people who fail the "good parent" role, replaying the original pain and sometimes dramatically expressing rage they could not express to parents, then withdrawing quickly. More often they "simmer and seethe in silence."
- Unconscious deep hostility toward parents (not welcomed into the world, out of place, unwanted) is too deep to express; they fear their own anger and withhold it. They dwell on old hurts and, subtly, do not want to let go of pain lest others (especially parents) not realize how much they were hurt.
- As this wears them out, average to unhealthy Fours sink into self-doubt, depression and despair; hopelessness is "the current against which they must constantly swim"; if the undertow is too strong: breakdown or suicide.
- **Direct teaching:** withdrawing from life to search for self is going in the wrong direction; the direct search for self is a temptation that leads to despair. Healthy Fours are not free of emotional turbulence; they have learned to ride it and to sustain identity without exclusive reference to feelings. Then they can bring something beautiful and good into existence: "bringing good out of evil, hope from hopelessness, meaning from absurdity, and saving what appeared to be lost." [s.16]

## Analyzing the Healthy Four [s.16]

### Level 1: The Inspired Creator
- Very healthy Fours are most in touch with unconscious impulses, listening to inner voices while remaining open to the environment; they act without self-consciousness and, given talent and training, give unconscious impulses objective form in a work of art.
- Having transcended self-consciousness they are creative in the root sense of bringing something new into the world; such moments come and go but at their best they sustain it. Image: "like oysters," transforming all experience, even painful, into something beautiful; they become "conduits through which the sublime passes into the world."
- Paradox: they express the personal universally, with resonance beyond their intention; they cannot explain where creativity comes from; their knowledge of self and others arrives as inspiration, spontaneous and beyond conscious control.
- Creativity is not limited to artists. The most important form is **self-creation**: renewing and redeeming the self by transcending the ego, turning all experience, good and bad, into growth. (Epigraph: William James, "Be the kind of person on whom nothing is lost.") The authors cite Otto Rank (via Reuben Fine): the creative individual, not the artist as such, is the ideal, and the artist is still seeking in art a refuge that would be better given up for real life.
- Acting in the moment of inspiration, "not primarily a moment of feeling," they create and discover themselves in what they bring forth; they are told who they are not by their parents but by what they discover in creativity and the richness of daily life. They embrace life, stop restricting their experiences, say "yes" to life, experience themselves freshly each moment; constantly renewing the self is the highest creativity, a kind of **"soul making."** [s.16]

### Level 2: The Self-Aware Intuitive
- Stepping back from the creative moment to reflect on or enjoy it loses the unselfconsciousness needed to sustain it; the self is "more like a process than like an object," but Fours fear they cannot find themselves in shifting feelings and begin to self-reflect rather than let experience flow. They become self-aware and introspective.
- Motivation: "Who am I? What is my life all about?" They turn to inner feelings and responses to establish identity (feelings are the most changeable part of the psyche, so this path cannot give what they want). At this Level they still have objectivity about feelings and emotional equilibrium, but the subtle shift is the seed of decline: "They have stopped having their feelings and have begun to be their feelings."
- Awareness distances even healthy Fours from the environment: life becomes theater, they are spectators and actors; the distance helps them frame and understand themselves but makes self-assertion and sustained practical activity difficult. They cannot hide from disquieting realities about self, others, life; but they are not afraid of what their feelings say.
- **Intuition** is how they understand others' thoughts and feelings: not "sideshow telepathy" but perceiving reality via the unconscious, "like receiving a message in a bottle which has washed up on the shore of consciousness." Self-awareness is its psychological basis. (Stendhal epigraph: "I note the echo that each thing produces as it strikes my soul.")
- **Jungian link:** Fours correspond to Jung's *introverted intuitive type* (quoted from *Psychological Types*: introverted intuition is directed to the inner object, the contents of the unconscious; external objects only release something within; unconscious images acquire "the dignity of things").
- Because their richest conscious life is outside their control, intuitions "come and go like ghosts"; they can be unsettling and are hard to express rationally; it takes time and courage to identify and accept them. [s.16]

### Level 3: The Self-Revealing Individual
- Healthy Fours need to express what they feel to know what their intuitions are telling them; "the most personal of the personality types." No masks, no self-deception about unflattering feelings; they willingly reveal flaws and irrationalities as part of their truth; genuineness, willingness to be touched even at the cost of pain.
- True to themselves as individuals even at the risk of censure by those who value convention; their emotional honesty may antagonize or embarrass; their contribution to society is the example of their humanity: everyone is valuable as an individual.
- Want others to be true to themselves too (Hamlet's "This above all: to thine own self be true"); respectful of others' individuality, feelings, privacy; allow others to find their own way without control, which makes them good parents, friends, listeners, therapists; others are "other," not functions or objects.
- Not thrown by others' revelations; supportive and compassionate listeners because they have explored their own emotional terrain; others see in them a quiet, emotional strength that feels safe and well grounded. Astute, eloquent; art by those with talent lets others recognize feelings they could not articulate.
- Acutely aware of themselves as individuals: not lonely but aware of being alone as an individual consciousness; "not merely individualists but existentialists."
- Rich sense of humor: poignant absurdity of human behavior in light of larger questions; "double vision" on human nature, devil and angel, sordid and noble, especially in themselves; the incongruities amuse and touch them. [s.16]

## Analyzing the Average Four [s.16]

### Level 4: The Imaginative Aesthete
- Fear: they will not sustain the flow of feelings, impressions and inspirations that underpins identity and creativity, so they use imagination to stir up feelings and hold certain moods that "express who they really are." This yields fertile fantasy life but marks a major shift away from interaction with life.
- Creativity becomes self-conscious, less universal: healthy Fours are artistic, average Fours *think of themselves as* artistic; more energy goes into creating moods from which inspiration is expected; output is sporadic and moves into imagination. Art and beauty become a substitute for themselves, expression "through a kind of proxy."
- Professional artists must find the medium that suits and learn their craft; non-artists treat jobs as support while the real interest is elsewhere. Given a "magic wish," most would become painters, singers, ballet dancers, poets, novelists, sculptors, filmmakers, designers.
- Without artistic ability they beautify their environment: tasteful decoration, art collecting, dressing well. Beauty (in people or things) stimulates feeling, reinforces self, and symbolizes the perfection and wholeness they lack; they are romantics, idealizing beauty.
- **Atmosphere** supports moods: mystery and romance, specific music, lighting, color schemes, emotionally charged objects, clothing "tastefully different"; nothing is casually selected; it all says quietly "This is who I am."
- Imagination shifts attention from reality: grand passions, lyrical longings, stormy emotions; preoccupations with nature, God, the self, the idealized other; portents and meanings; fascination with death and the passing of all things; the imagination becomes "an endless source of solace and gratification."
- Relationships: they relate to people as aesthetic objects to be contemplated; easily infatuated; hold long imaginary conversations with lovers and friends; scenes of love, longing, courtship, sexual ecstasy and bitter letting go play out inwardly. From here on they long for a deep relationship with someone who will "recognize" them and validate their identity as parents did not; they constantly look for love and replay brief meetings and glances for meaning; they like to see themselves as loners but are constantly looking for a relationship. Most relationships occur solely in imagination, which heightens impact while sparing self-exposure and rejection, but others inevitably turn out different from what Fours imagined. Intuition has deteriorated into using imagination to make up for experiences not had. [s.16]

### Level 5: The Self-Absorbed Romantic
- They believe too much interaction will make the fragile self-image fall apart; they control access, fearing shame or exposure of the gap between image and reality (e.g., imagining themselves great artists while rarely creating).
- Reserved, shy, extremely private, melancholy outsiders, painfully self-conscious; they have developed a persona with little connection to their background (the book's example: a person from a small Midwestern town affecting a British accent). Unlike Threes, who try to impress with an idealized self, Fours withhold the idealized self and most authentic thoughts from all but a few confidants, rationalizing others as incapable of subtlety ("throwing pearls before swine").
- They avoid many people, seek "kindred souls," and pour out their hearts in long late-night conversations when someone seems to understand; the ardor shows how deeply they long to be understood and rescued, to find the "good parent." To keep a rescuer's attention they must keep having problems, so they are "high maintenance" while seeing themselves as loners, and gauge their importance by how much others tolerate their ups and downs. They play "hard to get," but not so much as to drive the other away.
- They insist others respect the delicacy of their feelings; moodiness makes others "walk on eggshells"; vulnerability is also used to get attention and control; they demand tolerance for their peccadilloes while being impatient with others' habits.
- Relationships become long discussions of the Four's feelings; they are uninterested in anything not touching their feelings and quest for identity, since they are worn out by self-doubt and lack emotional resources. Socially inept, uncomfortable with small talk, work with others, meeting people; they secretly want to be sought out, projecting silence and aloofness; the "mysterious" air is a protective haze over vulnerability. Withdrawal after a hurt is "as aggressive an act as average Fours allow themselves," and it annoys them if the offender does not notice.
- **They take everything personally** and interiorize all experience: a curt cab driver can ruin a day, a perceptive criticism festers for months; they hear negative reviews even in compliments (congratulated on weight loss, they hear that they had been fat). Everything gathers private associations; healthy Fours use this richness as inspiration, but average Fours lose touch with emotions and feel "confused, amorphous, unanchored."
- Envy and secret resentment grow; "Why do I feel this way?" becomes "What is wrong with me?" Healthy Fours enjoy solitude; average Fours feel lonely, believing they are at best tolerated.
- **Moods set preconditions for action:** they put off letters, shopping, job-hunting until in the right mood; since the moods are usually unpleasant, things go undone or are done against resistance. They dwell on shortcomings, resentments toward "crude and insensitive" people, rehash conversations, imagine revenge, and fritter away days in fantasy. [s.16]

### Level 6: The Self-Indulgent "Exception"
- Having withdrawn into private fantasies they actually have become different; they feel entitled to compensate through indulgence, "exceptions to the rule, exempt from expectations, totally free to 'be themselves'." Undisciplined, they luxuriate in whatever emotional and material pleasures they can afford.
- The negation-built self-image is now so narrow they reject normal life: not holding a regular job or even looking, not cooking or cleaning, not joining social or community affairs. They defend identity doubts by contempt for "the rabble," using aesthetics as a weapon to insult those who do not appreciate what they do.
- Disdain is paired with **envy and resentment**: they see others' simple happiness in ordinary jobs, marriages, friendships and sense their own unhappiness; others' joy and spontaneity "is like a slap in the face." The authors say that seeing their "depth" as pretension and illusion could open a way out.
- They antagonize others by being perversely willful: no sense of social responsibility, cannot be counted on, resist obligations, petulant if forced ("I do what I want to do when I want to do it"). Everything is allowed because of emotional needs; they resist jobs, discipline and cooperation if something else will feel better; self-indulgence does not satisfy real needs, only transient desires, and weakens them. They often depend on others' support to keep this lifestyle and hide its extent.
- Precious, impractical, effete disdain for reality; affectations and mannerisms substitute for expression ("prima donna" quality); self-referential art; no serious work, eroticism and languorous overwrought fantasies ("Brilliant poetry... pour from their imaginations—as long as they never try to write them down").
- They feel sorry for themselves; minor hypochondria; **self-pity** rationalizes anything and says life owes them something; wallowing occupies time and keeps the sense of self alive "even as the life is being drained out of it."
- To make up for lack of achievement they turn to **sensuality** to deaden the too-sensitive self: sexual licentiousness or erotic daydreaming, frequent masturbation (symbol of the ingrown life), obsession with imagined beloveds, oversleeping, food, drugs, alcohol. Their emotions are overripe, "rare orchids kept in the hothouse of self-absorption," decadent to others; they have abandoned the search for self and substituted self-gratification. [s.16]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Four [s.16]

### Level 7: The Alienated Depressive
- Self-indulgent Fours fear losing hopes of self-actualization; if something makes the dream feel lost they "spiral in" to a core of themselves in shock. They are angry at themselves, ashamed of wasted time, missed opportunities, falling behind others; they envy others' happiness and success; they feel like failures.
- They unconsciously inhibit all meaningful desires so as not to be hurt again, causing sudden total blockage of feeling: fatigue, apathy, alienation, emotional paralysis. Exertion is extraordinarily difficult (cannot sit at an easel or typewriter, call friends, see a movie, look for work or a therapist); they stay in bed; they cannot even be self-indulgent.
- They fear expressing anger lest it worsen things; if angry at a disappointing romantic interest they cannot stand being in the same room, yet others see they look desolate, sigh, are near tears.
- They feel everyone has let them down; their only uniqueness is the degree of suffering (a humiliating shock in therapy to learn others have suffered as much). The authors add that this does not discount that they truly are in great pain. They know they are on the verge of going under: "An inner light is going out." [s.16]

### Level 8: The Emotionally Tormented Person
- Disappointment intensifies into consuming **self-hatred**; withering self-contempt; they excoriate mistakes, wasted time, unworthiness of love; obsessively negative thoughts become delusional, no ray of hope intrudes.
- Morbid fantasies: outcasts, sacrificial victims suffering for what parents did and what they did to themselves; guilty for existing; self-hatred like "an electron accelerator whipping incidents of virtually no significance into formidable forces." They believe others regard them as contemptuously as they regard themselves. A "black hole" of inner darkness; hours alone barely breathing yet violently tormented; sudden sobbing, then silence.
- They sabotage remaining opportunities, hurl irrational accusations at friends and supporters, keep a fading hope that the "good parent" may appear (itself becoming self-torture); drug and alcohol abuse escalates; fantasies become death-obsessed.
- Everything mocks them (unfinished work, failure in love, family, job). Some self-accusations have a factual basis; self-hatred does not expiate guilt but destroys remaining inner resources; "the only way out is to do away with their tormented consciousness altogether." [s.16]

### Level 9: The Self-Destructive Person
- If conditions do not change, despair leads neurotic Fours to destroy themselves directly (suicide) or indirectly (drugs, alcohol, other means). Others find it hard to understand that everything positive, beautiful and good has become a rebuke; they must "rid themselves of themselves."
- Suicide attempts as a plea for help are possible, but many intend it to work; death is a hoped-for annihilation of painful self-consciousness; suicide is also a rebuke to others for not helping or understanding, "the ultimate act of withdrawal, an aggressive act" that inflicts suffering without being aggressive or guilty. Fours are also capable of murdering those they blame (crime of passion, then suicide, after a disappointment in love). Contemplating suicide is the one thing they still feel they control (the power to say no to life) and thought of it brings comfort; having rehearsed it many times, they may act without warning. [s.16]

## The Dynamics of the Four [s.16]

(Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material: Four disintegrates to Two, integrates to One.)

### Direction of disintegration: the Four goes to Two
Starting at Level 4, under stress Fours show qualities of average to unhealthy Twos. The move is an "ironic, unintended acknowledgment" that average Fours still need people; they hope to be loved so they can eventually love themselves. By level:

| Level | Four at Two |
|---|---|
| 4 | Focused on imagination more than people; stressed when others engage directly; slightly forced effusiveness; relationships a major stress; like Twos, seek reassurance by repeatedly saying how much they care and how meaningful the relationship is. |
| 5 | Self-absorption makes others avoid them, producing fears of abandonment; they hover and intrude like average Twos; become clinging and possessive of those who support their fantasies; find ways to "be needed"; keep loved ones in sight. |
| 6 | Need financial support to continue the "artistic" lifestyle; anxiety about "slipping through the cracks" makes them exaggerate their importance in others' lives; magnify small gestures and demand acknowledgment for services rendered or for the "unique pleasure" of their company; want to be respected as undiscovered talents while dissipating in idle sensuality. |
| 7 | Too depressed to hold a job; dependent on others' goodwill but resentful and angry; terrified of losing the "supply lines"; manipulate by guilt and undermining others' confidence; often stuck with the families they want to get away from; want a wonderful relationship but cannot sustain a genuine one. |
| 8 | At the limit they break through self-inhibition at Two, acting out repressed rage and sexual impulses; feel entitled after long suffering; binges of drugs, alcohol, compulsive sexuality; may stalk a sexual obsession, take advantage of those more helpless, or physically attack persons blamed for their misery. |
| 9 | Possible nervous breakdown; indirectly coerce someone (almost certainly not the imagined beloved) into caring; pity substitutes for love; parasitic existence; feel exempt from any expectation, even of getting well. |

At Two they come to hate the person they depend on (a constant reminder of defects), alternate aggression at self and other, destroy the relationship; without professional help, "they may well eventually go mad, commit suicide, or both." [s.16]

### Direction of integration: the Four goes to One
- Healthy Fours actualize by focusing on something objective beyond feelings and imagination. Going to One means moving from subjectivity to objectivity, from self-absorption to **principled action**: controlled by convictions, not moods; mobilizing energy in service of something beyond personal interest.
- They accept values to submit to; become self-disciplined and work consistently at their potentials; freedom comes from wanting to do what they need to do instead of whatever they please; by being part of the world they find a context to discover themselves; feelings stay but are tied to what is happening in reality.
- No longer self-indulgent or "different"; they submit to reality and conscience, accept limits, drop the sense of exemption; like healthy Ones, exceptional teachers (intuition reinforced by discernment, insight by clarity).
- What they create is objective, so they can contemplate it (a work of art, an act of kindness, a successful relationship), learn who they are and find genuine self-esteem: "to the extent that their creation is good, the person who created it must also be good." [s.16]

## The Major Subtypes of the Four (wings) [s.16]

### Four with a Three-wing: "The Aristocrat" [s.16]
- Emotionally volatile and contradictory combination; the two types seem opposites (Four introverted, fears exposure, even "fear of success"; Three extroverted, self-display, competitive) but share concern with self-image and self-esteem. Fours' image is private (trying to *become* it through imagination and feeling); Threes' is public, validated through achievement. (Also true for Three with a Four-wing.)
- Examples named: Tennessee Williams, Maria Callas, Rudolf Nureyev, Judy Garland, Michael Jackson, Prince, Chopin, Jeremy Irons, Proust, Martha Graham, Edith Piaf, Paul Simon, Harold Pinter, Walt Whitman, Camus, E. M. Forster, Mahler, Tchaikovsky, "Blanche DuBois."
- **Healthy:** sociable, ambitious, accomplished (especially in the arts), creative and driven to "make their mark"; more goal-oriented, sociable and aware of interpersonal "politics" than the Five-wing; want to be successful but distinctive and will do the work; create with an audience in mind; adaptable, sensitive, good humor.
- **Average:** concern about what others think may help out of self-absorption; they can project a favorable image and conceal their real emotional condition; competitive but fear success, exposure, humiliation; strong need for attention and admiration; heightened sense of drama to the point of exhibitionism; narcissistic needs and desires for triumph feed fantasies and disappointments; more practical but more emotionally and financially extravagant than the other wing; surround themselves with refinement and culture; "good taste" a prerequisite for closeness; see themselves as more sophisticated and "high class" than those around them, especially their families; more responsive to suggestion and criticism but more resentful from suppression of deeper responses.
- **Unhealthy:** aggression still turned mainly on self; also moments of unhealthy Three: hostile, malicious, envy reinforced by jealousy, exploitativeness, opportunism, duplicity (which add shame and guilt); the Three's desire to ruin others is often contemplated, rarely acted on; if acted on they punish themselves more severely; crimes of passion and suicide possible. [s.16]

### Four with a Five-wing: "The Bohemian" [s.16]
- Traits reinforce each other; both are "withdrawn" types (Fours withdraw to protect feelings, Fives their security); more reclusive and less ambitious; more observant of the environment and of people; intellectual depth and intensity but social insecurity; more insightful and original, less likely to do consistent concrete work.
- Examples named: Virginia Woolf, Edgar Allan Poe, Anne Rice, Ingmar Bergman, D. H. Lawrence, Yukio Mishima, J. D. Salinger, Johnny Depp, Bob Dylan, Joni Mitchell, Kierkegaard, Hermann Hesse, William Blake, "Laura Wingfield."
- **Healthy:** "probably the most profoundly creative of all the types": intuition with insight, sensitivity with comprehension, often stunningly original or prophetic; burn lighter but risk burning out faster; drawn to arts and social sciences; care less about others' opinions, follow the muse; highly personal, idiosyncratic self-expression; create more for themselves than an audience.
- **Average:** self-absorption plus philosophical and religious speculation; the emotional world dominates with an intellectual cast; loners with less social connectedness; art more completely substitutes for the person; brilliant flashes of insight, trouble sustaining effort; ethereal, extremely independent, unconventional to the point of eccentricity; secretive, purposely enigmatic; ideas unusual, mysterious, surreal; attracted to the exotic and symbolic; bohemian or casual presentation; little interest in communicating with those who cannot understand; simpler lifestyle (the Five's minimizing of needs), high-strung nervous energy; less healthy ones become reclusive, daydreaming with baroque fantasies, tormented by self-doubt and unable to act.
- **Unhealthy:** barren, terrifying inner world; self-denying, even life-denying resistance to everything outside the self; understanding of their problems but lost in turmoil (fatigue, depression, hopelessness); pondering torment goes in circles, heightening self-contempt and nihilism; resist being helped; project fears into the environment (distorted thinking, hallucinations, phobias); pessimism about meaninglessness; potentially "the most isolated from themselves and from reality of all types"; prone to the depressive forms of schizophrenia. [s.16]

## Some Final Thoughts (Four) [s.16]
- Neurotic Fours make the worst and most crippling aspects of the self-image into the whole truth of who they are; the idealized image grows so out of touch they reject positive qualities they have. "The pity is not that they always have been as damaged as they feel they are, but that they have become profoundly damaged because they hate themselves": a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- A key mistake: equating themselves with their feelings; believing they must work through feelings (especially negative ones) before acting. The self is not the same as its feelings, and negative feelings do not preclude good in oneself; as they deteriorate "the bad drives out the good."
- **Practical guidance:** a leap of faith that, despite no clear sense of self, they will find themselves most surely by acting positively toward others; they must love others even if they do not feel adequately loved; self-discovery and self-esteem follow; the fact that they can love shows they learned to love somewhere: "What they have been given has perhaps been enough after all." [s.16]

# CHAPTER 7 — TYPE FIVE: THE INVESTIGATOR [s.17]

## The Five in profile [s.17]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Observe everything with extraordinary perceptiveness and insight; mentally alert, curious, searching intelligence; foresight and prediction; able to concentrate and become engrossed. Skillful mastery of what interests them; excited by knowledge, often expert; innovative, inventive, producing valuable original works; highly independent, idiosyncratic, whimsical. **At their best:** visionaries, comprehending the world broadly while penetrating it profoundly; open-minded, taking things in whole and in context; pioneering discoveries and entirely new ways of doing and perceiving. |
| Average | Conceptualize everything before acting (model building, preparing, practicing, gathering resources); studious, acquiring technique; specialized and "intellectual" (research, scholarship, theory building). Increasingly detached amid complicated ideas or imaginary worlds; preoccupied with visions and interpretations rather than reality; fascinated by offbeat, esoteric subjects, even dark and disturbing; a "disembodied mind," though high-strung and intense. Antagonistic to anything interfering with inner world and personal vision; provocative, abrasive, intentionally extreme and radical views; cynical, argumentative. |
| Unhealthy | Reclusive, isolated from reality, eccentric and nihilistic; highly unstable, fearful of aggressions; reject and repulse others and all social attachments. Obsessed with yet frightened by threatening ideas: horrified, delirious, prey to gross distortions and phobias. Seeking oblivion, may commit suicide or have a psychotic break; deranged, explosively self-destructive, schizophrenic overtones. |

**Key Motivations:** want to be capable and competent, to master a body of knowledge and skill, to explore reality, to remain undisturbed by others, to reduce their needs. [s.17]

**Examples** (abridged): Einstein, Stephen Hawking, Nietzsche, Stanley Kubrick, Georgia O'Keeffe, Emily Dickinson, Simone Weil, Bill Gates, Sartre, James Joyce, Gary Larson, David Lynch, Stephen King, Tim Burton, Clive Barker, Laurie Anderson, John Cage, Glenn Gould, Charles Ives, Bobby Fischer, van Gogh. [s.17]

## Overview of the Five [s.17]

- **Genius and madness.** The opening passage: genius and madness are opposite poles, not neighbors. The genius fuses knowledge with insight and sees *correctly, within context*; the genius perceives patterns actually present, the madman **imposes** patterns and projects erroneous perceptions. The Five is the type that most exemplifies both extremes: the genius and the madman, the innovator and intellectual, the mildly eccentric crackpot and the deeply disturbed delusional schizoid. "To understand how these widely diverse states are part of the same personality type is to understand the Five." [s.17]
- **In the Thinking Triad.** Fives emphasize thinking over doing; their mental world becomes all-engrossing; they are more at home in their minds, viewing the world from a detached vantage point, than in action. All three members (Five, Six, Seven) *focus attention on the world outside themselves*. This is not a contradiction: Fives' material comes via sense perceptions whose accuracy they cannot be sure of; the only thing known with certainty is their own thoughts; so they look outward but identify with their *thoughts about* the environment. Many problems come from needing to learn how their perceptions square with reality so they can act confidently. [s.17]
- **Problems with Security and Anxiety.** Like other Thinking-Triad types, average Fives feel the environment is unpredictable, potentially threatening, and they feel powerless to defend themselves; their number one priority is acquiring skills and knowledge to function. **Basic Fear:** being helpless and incapable. They believe their resources are limited, so they respond to anxiety by **downscaling activities and needs**: the more anxious, the more they minimize needs (some end up in extremely primitive conditions). They are overwhelmed by others' needs and avoid situations that expect more than they can give; "shrink away" from the world and from connections. [s.17]
- Healthy Fives observe reality as it is and grasp complex phenomena at a glance because they participate in life and test their perceptions. Average Fives' perceptions skew: thinking becomes convoluted, elaborate, fueled by anxiety; withdrawal heightens fears of not coping; even basic living requirements seem overwhelming. Unhealthy Fives cut themselves off from most human contact, develop eccentric ideas to absurd extremes, and are finally "terrified and trapped by the threatening visions which they have created in their own minds." [s.17]
- Their anxiety links to difficulty perceiving reality objectively: afraid to let anyone or anything influence their thoughts, they fear others' agendas will overwhelm them or that more powerful people will control or possess them. Yet even average Fives will be "possessed by an idea," so long as it originated with them. Relying only on untested ideas, they can become profoundly out of touch. Average to unhealthy Fives become unsure which perceptions are valid and "do not know what is real and what is the product of their minds"; they project anxiety-ridden thoughts and aggressive impulses into the environment and become convinced their dark interpretation is how things are. [s.17]
- **Jungian link:** Five corresponds to **Jung's introverted thinking type** (quoted: thinking oriented by the "subjective factor"; "Facts are collected as evidence for a theory, never for their own sake"). The authors prefer to call Fives a *subjective thinking type*, since the aim is often directed outward at the environment: thinking is how Fives both fit into the world and, "paradoxically, defend themselves against it." [s.17]
- Even healthy Fives are not deeply rooted in visceral experience: they get "a great deal of intellectual mileage out of very little experience," finding significance where others see little; this can lead to great discoveries. When they stop observing and focus on their interpretations, they lose touch with reality and with constructive action, where their self-confidence would grow; they play with ideas or alternative realities of no practical impact, feeling more fearful and vulnerable. [s.17]

### Parental Orientation [s.17]
- **Ambivalent to both parents.** Like Twos and Eights, Fives sought a niche in the family system, a role that would earn protection and nurturance; they perceived no place to fit and that nothing they could do was wanted or needed. They withdrew to find something to "bring to the table": something they do well enough to feel an equal. Because their fear is being helpless and incapable, they look for areas of expertise others have not explored; their agenda is search for and mastery of subjects and skills until confident to "reenter" the world.
- **Unstated bargain** carried into all relationships: *"Don't ask too much of me, and I won't ask too much of you."* Average Fives resent intrusions on space, time, persons; comfortable distance for other types can feel uncomfortable. Possible background: felt crowded out or intruded on; parents erratic, emotionally disturbed, alcoholic, in a loveless marriage, not dependable sources of love. They become ambivalent also toward the world.
- They resolve the ambivalence by identifying with nothing but their thoughts: thoughts are "good" (correct, safe to identify with), outside reality "bad" (watched vigilantly, to be repulsed at a moment's notice). In average to unhealthy Fives, feeling crowded may produce feeling unsafe in the body: they become detached, indifferent to physical comfort, extremely cerebral, willing to jettison comforts and even needs to protect space and time for their interests.
- Cognitive style: strict **dualism** of self and world: inner/outer, subjects/objects, known/unknown, dangerous/safe, with tremendous ramifications. [s.17]

### Problems with Detachment and Phobia [s.17]
- Healthy Fives do not need to detach; observations are accurate because they interact. Down the Levels, perception focuses on what seems threatening, the mind fills with anxiety; ironically, the more fearful, the more compelled to investigate what terrifies them.
- They make terrifying projections their only reality, "literally scaring themselves out of their minds"; so phobic and fragile in capability that it is extraordinarily hard to function or ask help; yet only reaching out gets them back in touch with reality.
- If this persists their thoughts become delusional and terrifying enough that they separate from their own thoughts: neurotic Fives become **schizoid**, unconsciously splitting off from their teeming minds; reality becomes hellish; they retreat "into emptiness—and yet more horror." [s.17]

## Analyzing the Healthy Five [s.17]

### Level 1: The Pioneering Visionary
- Paradoxical ability to penetrate reality profoundly while comprehending it broadly; take things in whole, perceive patterns where others see confusion; synthesize knowledge, connecting phenomena no one knew were related (the book's examples: time and space, DNA structure, brain chemistry and behavior). Artistically inclined Fives may develop new art forms or revolutionize existing ones, creating "the new platform from which others will learn and create."
- They do not cling to their own ideas of how the world works; they discover unanticipated truths "precisely because they are willing not to know the answers, keeping an open mind." Not imposing thoughts on reality, they discover its internal logic and interrelated patterns, give clear thoughts into obscure matters and predict events far ahead of others' ability to verify; foresight comes from seeing with clarity "like a weaver who knows the pattern of a tapestry before it is completed."
- They transcend rational thought toward the ineffable, where words, theories and symbols are left behind: "closer to contemplatives than thinkers"; the "quiet mind" of Buddhism and other traditions, a mind like a mirror that accurately reflects what it turns to. They allow reality in, understanding they are not separate from it.
- Gifted Fives describe reality so well that discoveries seem obvious in hindsight; the leap from known to unknown is a great achievement. Very healthy Fives are intellectual pioneers who open new domains; a sufficiently gifted individual may be a genius of historic dimensions.
- At Level 1 the brilliance is entirely unselfconscious; they feel at home and at peace in the world, are freed from the relentless pursuit of knowledge and skill because they have transcended the fear of incapability, no longer overwhelmed by others or challenges, and bring "hearts and minds together for the compassionate use of their knowledge and talents." [s.17]

### Level 2: The Perceptive Observer
- Extraordinarily conscious of the world's glories, horrors, incongruities, complexities; "the most mentally alert of the personality types"; thinking is enjoyed for its own sake; knowledge and understanding are exhilarating; fascinated by people, nature, life, the mind.
- They penetrate the superficial; brilliant insights from noticing the anomaly or hidden element that unlocks the whole, so they always have something worthwhile to say. **Seeing** is virtually a symbol of their orientation: if something can be apprehended by senses or mind, it can be understood; once understood, mastered; then they can act with confidence.
- They concentrate rather than observe passively, noticing patterns and meaning, perceiving detail "as if training a magnifying glass on the environment"; never bored; no matter how much they know they want more.
- They sustain concentration, are hard workers who patiently attack problems, labor in obscurity for years, are undeterred by others' indifference; process (exploring, learning, creating) is more enjoyable than the final goal; they tinker with familiar forms till almost unrecognizable (especially in the arts); good conceptualizers who ask the right fundamental questions and define intellectual boundaries; "They do not attempt to do the impossible, only to understand what they have not understood before."
- Most Fives, regardless of actual intelligence, consider themselves "smart" and perceptive as a defining characteristic; not all are intellectuals, but all focus on ideas, perceptions and ways of seeing; new insight gives confidence and drives deeper exploration.
- Others see them as "unusual" (intelligence, sensory acuity, idiosyncrasy, penetrating gaze); unlike Fours they are not interested in being "different"; they accept outsider status with a shrug, are unsentimental, follow curiosity wherever it leads and are relatively unconcerned with convention.
- Yet even here inquiry shifts them from participation toward outside observer; even healthy Fives have some anxiety about environments they do not understand (a conundrum, since they can understand only by dealing with it). The habit of observation reflects "not simply a dispassionate curiosity but a deep personal need." [s.17]

### Level 3: The Focused Innovator
- Having identified as intelligent and perceptive, they fear losing perceptiveness or being inaccurate, so they focus intensely on their most interesting areas to master them and secure a place in the world; they want to go beyond what has been explored ("push the envelope"), partly as a test of competence and partly to create a unique niche.
- Results may be inventions and technological marvels, startling discoveries or artworks, or ideas with no immediate use that later become the base of new knowledge (the book's examples: the physics behind television and radar; scraps of ideas that later yield a novel or film).
- Open-minded, not attached to viewpoints, curious about others' perspectives; patient explainers; want to communicate and be understood; clear explanations of problems and solutions; share knowledge since they learn from discussion; exciting teachers, colleagues and friends with infectious enthusiasm for cross-fertilizing expertise.
- Extremely independent: innovating, learning, creating are solitary adventures; they will be as unorthodox as inquiry requires and challenge existing dogma. Their innovations can overturn previous thinking; art, dance, cinema and music have been "scandalized" by Fives' creations that later became standards.
- **Whimsical, wry, taboo-breaking humor:** attuned to absurdities and ironies; slightly distort reality to expose an assumption with no logical basis; fascinated by strange, offbeat subjects; tinker with objects, images, words; attracted to subject matter others reject. Powerful imaginations visualize solutions or create alternative realities; many artistic Fives become **filmmakers, cartoonists, authors of genre fiction (science fiction, horror, black humor)**. Aesthetic runs to two extremes: **minimalist and spare**, or **surreal and fantastic**. They are not interested in simple "human interest" stories; they direct attention to more absolute truths, especially those hidden in the "ordinary world."
- Healthy Fives are usually **polymaths**, expert across many subjects, sharing knowledge to enrich society; confident because they are doing things with their insights (the book points to computers, antibiotics, communications media and technology as products). [s.17]

## Analyzing the Average Five [s.17]

### Level 4: The Studious Expert
- Essential difference from healthy Fives: they begin to fear they do not know enough to act or take their place in the world; they need to study, practice, acquire technique, run tests ("The more you know, the more you know you don't know"). They retreat to the area of strength, the mind. Rather than innovating they **conceptualize and prepare**: "healthy Fives use knowledge, whereas average Fives are in pursuit of it."
- From this Level down they avoid direct contact with the world; spend hours conceptualizing but hesitate to put ideas into concrete form; stuck in **"preparation mode"** (the refrain "I need a little more time"); a book or invention stays in imagination. Hesitation conceals a growing lack of faith in their ability to cope.
- They feel less prepared than others and so gather information, skills, resources to "build themselves up," disengaging from social activities. Their homes become reflections of their minds, storage for collections of books, tapes, videos, CDs, gadgets. Bookish: bookstores, libraries, intellectual coffeehouses; spend on tools for their interests but loath to spend on their own comfort because they identify with mind and imagination, not body.
- **Specialization:** they delve into bodies of knowledge most do not understand and take pleasure in "I know something that you don't know." Academic (genetic structures, mathematics of snowflake formation, bird migration) or non-academic (antiques, stamps, comic books, jazz). **Collecting is a metaphor for their approach to life** (gathering material and incorporating it into what they believe they know). Completeness matters (a video collection organized by director of every major horror or science fiction film from 1950 to 1990; the complete Beatles catalogue with bootlegs; all nine Beethoven symphonies in multiple recordings). It brings a temporary feeling of competency while they avoid activities that would build real confidence.
- Increasing identification with the mind: they begin to **miss things**, focusing intently on certain details and overlooking other relevant information; they "make a science of" whatever they like (history, linguistics, stereo equipment, jogging shoes, the sociology of ape families); beginning of abstraction from reality, narrowing perception to pursue depth.
- They approach new experiences by analyzing them: the shy person who learns a dance by watching and memorizing steps (the dance is over by the time they finish "figuring it out"). Upside: systematic learners who can explain how they reached conclusions; at Level 4 they enjoy sharing expertise at length, but are uncomfortable about much else; personal life, hopes, desires, disappointments and especially feelings become private; they prefer discussing their subjects and reaching deeper "truths" through intelligent conversation. [s.17]

### Level 5: The Intense Conceptualizer
- Retreating into the apparent safety of the mind heightens their insecurities; less time on anything outside narrowing interests and less willingness to try new things; they use internal and financial resources to gain confidence but misapply the energy, bogged down in what others see as trivia, unable to come to closure because uncertain and afraid to leave their cerebral constructs.
- They believe they have no inner resources to spare; fear others' emotional needs will overwhelm or sidetrack them; believe everything depends on acquiring a skill to survive in "a world without pity or mercy"; may deeply want to connect but not before they have confidence and mastery. Social interactions are intrusions upon time and space; they defend by intensifying mental focus: "If Fives began to create alternative realities in the healthier levels, they begin to inhabit them at Level 5."
- They distract themselves with anything that gives a sense of confidence; in their minds they feel capable and in control, compensating for fears of powerlessness. They plunge into complex intellectual puzzles and **labyrinthine systems**: obscure theories in astronomy, mathematics, philosophy, or esoterica (Kabbala, astrology, the occult); intellectual games (chess, computer simulations, Dungeons and Dragons) where study becomes game and game becomes study; affinity for **genre fiction, especially science fiction and horror**; exploring dark, fantastic realms gives a sense of mastery; interest in disturbing subject matter is both a search for "turf" unclaimed by others and a **counterphobic** reaction to helplessness.
- Thinking becomes **uncensored**: willing to entertain any thought, however horrible or taboo, in pursuit of truth (laudable and the source of discoveries in healthy Fives); but with fewer "reality checks," exploring unsettling matter fuels anxiety.
- **Fascination with power:** knowledge is power and security; drawn to studies of power in nature, politics, human behavior; ambivalent about power and suspicious of those with power over them, whose power might render them helpless.
- **Secretiveness and compartmentalization:** unwilling to discuss personal or emotional life (would give others power and plunge them into their own vulnerability); terse, cryptic, uncommunicative; control access by compartmentalizing relationships (one friend learns of the professional life, another of the fascination with insects, another of the romantic life, another of the late-night haunts), keeping friends from meeting to "share notes."
- They reduce needs: cut back on creature comforts, activities, relationships; anything compromising independence is expendable; Spartan, minimal existence; take employment far below capability to avoid entanglement. "Ironically, they are avoiding living their lives so they can devote time to preparing to live their lives."
- **Turning point:** they stop observing the world consistently and focus on ideas and imagination; they are preoccupied with their own interpretations, detached from environment and their own emotions; they perceive little of the world.
- Speculation without closure: "they lose the forest for the trees"; every conjecture hangs in a cloud of possibilities; expositions grow incomprehensible; they do not publish easily because they cannot conclude. All ideas seem equally plausible ("Anything thinkable seems possible. Anything thinkable seems real."); the problems of epistemology are not only fascinating but unwittingly lived out; speculation maintains the sense of self by keeping the mind active.
- Communication suffers: convoluted thought, too much detail, condensed ideas, elaborate monologues, tangents with unmarked leaps (a remark on Jackson Pollock's technique followed by environmental chemicals and biological systems); possibly breathtaking in sweep but exhausting to follow; they assume it is as interesting to others as to themselves.
- **Disembodied minds:** the body is merely the vehicle; forget to eat, sleep, change clothes; absent-minded professor (a missing shirt button, untied shoes). **High-strung**, nervous systems "tuned to a higher pitch"; note on contrast: Nines also become cerebral and imaginative at average levels, but Nines become placid and passive while Fives become agitated and intense. They seem unable to repress unconscious impulses that erupt into their minds; find it hard to do things casually; find close relationships taxing.
- **Ambivalence to people:** attracted yet suspicious; they analyze people ("What you just said was fascinating—you're incredibly angry at men, aren't you?"); avoid deep involvement because people are unpredictable and demanding; believe there must be a catch; cannot imagine why anyone would be interested in them; passions flood into their minds; most have strong sexual impulses so cannot avoid involvement altogether. Typically unmarried or stormy relationships; intimacy becomes so complex and exhausting that they become reclusive, which fuels helplessness and a bleaker view of reality.
- Theology note: they have great difficulty accepting a benevolent universe or God; the problem of evil is an enormous stumbling block; a God who allowed the world as it is "must be sadistic, an evil God, a God they refuse to become involved with." [s.17]

### Level 6: The Provocative Cynic
- The complexities they create cause more troubling problems; anxiety rises; they fear others will make them give up pursuits before ready; they defend their fragile niche. Through speech style, dress and subjects they say "Leave me alone!" and become more aggressive in scaring people away.
- On the surface intellectually arrogant, actually less certain; they alternate between aggressively defending ideas and finding them worthless; take extreme and unorthodox positions to extract confidence from ideas becoming meaningless, wielding radical views "like cutting tools." Their subconscious fears erupt; they live in growing terror and are infuriated that others seem content or oblivious; they undermine others' contentment by "sharing" provocative views (the example: telling someone going to the beach about an ozone-layer report and skin-cancer statistics). There is often truth in the content but the intention is no longer truth but unsettling others.
- Extremism in social style and viewpoint: radicals in politics and art, avant-garde; push ideas to extremes for shock value and to demolish popular opinion; nonconformists and dissenters rebelling against social conventions (feminism, politics, child rearing, sexual liberation); "Understanding has been abandoned for polemics."
- Lifestyle as statement and rebuke: marginal existence to avoid "selling out" (which may mean any regular employment or even a relationship); intentionally provocative clothing and grooming. The authors note healthy protest is vital in any culture, but with lower average Fives "the point is that there is no point. Life is futile. People are stupid. My own life is meaningless." They link this attitude to many "alternative" cultures of the late twentieth century (grunge, cyberpunk, heavy metal), while noting other types are part of that picture.
- **Reductionism:** oversimplifying reality and dismissing positive alternative explanations (the flower as "nothing but" mud; painting nothing but the desire to smear feces; God nothing but a projection of the father; humans nothing but biological machines); mixed legitimate insights and extreme interpretations, with no way to tell which is which.
- A perverse resistance to reality taints thought: healthy originality becomes quirky eccentricity; "the genius has become little more than a crank" (examples: arcane secrets in numerical codes from TV character names, or that all rational thought is meaningless). They dwell on outlandish ideas, defend them at all costs, are contentious and quarrelsome, worry about intellectual priority and threaten lawsuits over "stolen" theories.
- Underneath is cynicism and hopelessness about all their ideas; all viewpoints seem equally irrelevant; they can argue anything, even repugnant views, to reaffirm intelligence and prove futility.
- They look busy but spend time on inessentials (reading a book on ants, building a database of a record collection, studying chess strategy rather than the résumé, bills or work project); may not face a job interview or learn to drive but "conquer the world, survive a nuclear holocaust, or wield awesome occult powers in the world of their imaginations." Feeling helpless in a hostile reality, anxious, tormented by tempers and imaginations (insomnia common); their abrasive behavior makes lack of a place in the world real. Guidance: if they could reach out and acknowledge their suffering, they could turn their difficulties around; continuing to turn away risks plunging into darkness. [s.17]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Five [s.17]

### Level 7: The Isolated Nihilist
- Keeping others at a distance becomes antagonism toward anyone who seems to threaten; self-doubt makes almost everything threatening; they "go it alone," convinced they will never find a place in society; extreme isolation, eccentricity, nihilistic despair.
- Aggression arises when ideas are questioned or ridiculed (their self-confidence is wedded to their ideas); they go on offense: discrediting people, showing ideas worthless, solutions illusory, the world a "fool's paradise." They provoke rejection, then become cynical about relationships. They take pleasure in debunking the positive, proving human relationships near-impossible and human nature rotten; deflate "bourgeois illusions" others live by, which they have escaped through "greater intellectual honesty."
- Half-truth: others may live too comfortably or self-deceptively; families and relationships may be tainted by hypocrisy, jealousy and power struggles; but cynicism is not the best response: "throw out the baby with the bath water"; faith, hope, love, kindness, friendship are hard to believe; nihilism about all relationships and the value of humanity justifies isolation.
- The intensity of their minds repels everything, like a fire hose holding back a crowd. They "burn their bridges" (ending friendships, quitting jobs, emptying life of all but the barest necessities) so as not to be dependent: possibly living out of a car or squatting in a condemned building; neglecting appearance, poor eating, going unwashed. Alcoholism and substance abuse are common (drugs, including designer drugs and heroin, are particularly debilitating since they erode confidence).
- To avoid influence they maintain isolation: rants, long diatribes and denunciations, or glowering hateful silence (usually not violent). Isolation deepens; no "reality checks"; experiences confirm helplessness and meaninglessness; minor problems besiege; interactions are invasions. They are clearly unstable; the hint that they "need help" triggers fears of incompetence and drives them deeper; they can still reason well enough to argue away positive input.
- Dilemma: they want to act and discharge their teeming minds but are crippled by fear, avoid violent conflict, and so do nothing; "the intensity of their own minds begins to devour them." [s.17]

### Level 8: The Terrified "Alien"
- Retreat is down to a single room or a friend's basement; the only place left is deeper into their minds, which are the true source of terrors. They cannot distinguish sensory impressions from fearful thoughts; the world is an implacable, devouring force, a fever dream like a Hieronymus Bosch painting.
- Phobias incapacitate; inanimate objects turn sinister (ceiling collapsing, armchair swallowing, the television causing brain cancer); hallucinations (voices, distorted visuals); the body seems alien and turning against them; they must stay vigilant, cannot turn off their minds, are physically exhausted; they literally cannot sleep (fear of vulnerability and violent dreams); alcohol or drugs to shut down the mind add exhaustion; sleeplessness produces hallucinations; "the childhood monsters in the closet become real."
- Thoughts seem to have a life of their own (like Dr. Frankenstein, destroyed by what they brought to life). Pattern-finding goes haywire: unrelated things are absolutely related (the behavior of birds as a political trend; raisins in cereal predicting months to cataclysm); existence pointless and horrifying yet trivial phenomena assigned sinister meaning.
- Image: five hundred watts through a one-hundred-watt bulb. They resist all help (it would confirm uselessness) and flee those who reach out. They would like to destroy everything but cannot discharge rage; actions erratic, irrational, frightening yet only minor responses to inner chaos; life is unbearable, "as if their eyelids had been removed," though "their minds are devouring them." [s.17]

### Level 9: The Imploding Schizoid
- Detachment and compartmentalization no longer hold; they become schizoid, splitting off terrifying parts of the psyche; the world's hostile forces and the terrors of their minds collapse into one continuous pain and horror. They want everything to stop, in oblivion, by two main routes.
- **Suicide**, like unhealthy Fours but for different reasons (Fours out of self-hatred and silent accusation; Fives because life is meaningless and horrifying; Fives with a Four-wing and Fours with a Five-wing show a combination); like Hamlet, "not being" becomes "a consummation devoutly to be wished."
- Or they split consciousness in two and retreat to the safe part, regressing to an autistic-like, psychosis-resembling state: they identify with the emptiness remaining when they detach from remaining identifications, "detach themselves from themselves," like parents throwing away everything that recalls a dead child; the self becomes "a totally empty house." Probably a form of schizophrenia. All intellectual intensity gone; utterly isolated from environment, people and inner life (thinking, feeling, doing).
- Irony: they retreat to gain time to build confidence and ability, but destroy confidence, talent, even life; those who survive may live in helplessness, dependency or incarceration, the very thing feared; what they removed themselves from was not reality but the projection of their anxieties; unable to cry out from the void. [s.17]

## The Dynamics of the Five [s.17]

(Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material: Five disintegrates to Seven, integrates to Eight.)

### Direction of disintegration: the Five goes to Seven
Starting at Level 4, an unconscious reaction to the shrinking of the Five's world, in the scattered, hyperactive discharge of anxiety seen in average to unhealthy Sevens.

| Level | Five at Seven |
|---|---|
| 4 | Studying and preparing, plus restlessness and desire for variety; constantly acquiring information and collections; move from topic to topic seeking the satisfying project, none quite satisfying. |
| 5 | Starved for stimulation, take on varied experiences unrelated to central projects: video games, movies, science fiction and horror; free association, silliness, offbeat humor that surprises others; taste for nightlife (restaurants, bars, nightclubs), often as secretive voyeurs; rising anxiety raises desire for distraction. |
| 6 | Fearful, despairing of a niche; antagonistic and provocative defending their turf; high gear avoiding anxiety; insensitive and aggressive in pursuing what they want; Seven's jaded callousness reinforces cynicism; impatient, hardened; some find them wild and exciting, most put off by bristling energy; drugs or alcohol quell anxiety. |
| 7 | Isolated with no constructive outlet; discharge in escapist behaviors that dissipate them; lurch from isolation to wild activity; impulsive, irresponsible, poor judgment; abusive and infantile when questioned. |
| 8 | Cannot tell horrific images from reality; manic, reckless; totally out of control; some feared things may happen through erratic behavior; heedless of real dangers (run over by a truck, mistakenly eating something poisonous rather than "death rays" or the KGB); like a manic-depressive Seven. |
| 9 | Consumed with terror; may compulsively do permanent harm to self or another; reckless activities may have damaged health; debilitated, burned out, paralyzed; may do something irrevocable such as impulsively killing someone or suicide. |

### Direction of integration: the Five goes to Eight
- Fives typically feel they never know enough to act; they feel insecure until they master the real world, not just their own minds. Psychoanalytic note: the ego is too weak for the id, with aggressions and impulses overpowering the mind.
- Integrating Fives incorporate perceptions of the world by **identifying with the objects of their thoughts, not just the thoughts**; they overcome fear of the environment and learn to trust it; self-confidence grows after the manner of healthy Eights.
- At Eight they realize that what they know is still more than almost anyone else; they need not know everything before acting; they learn more by doing, solve problems as they arise, will know what they need when they need it. Confidence comes from real connection with presence in the world, not a vast memorized body of knowledge; they experience themselves "not as separate from the world, not as a helpless speck, but as a powerful, integral part of it."
- They act from genuine mastery, lead others with confidence, put ideas (and themselves) on the line, contribute something worthwhile; thoughts become action and possibly leadership; they show others how to do what only they know. [s.17]

## The Major Subtypes of the Five (wings) [s.17]

### Five with a Four-wing: "The Iconoclast" [s.17]
- Traits reinforce each other: both are withdrawn types turning to the imagination to defend the ego and reinforce the sense of self; both feel something essential must be found in themselves before living fully (Fives lack the confidence to act, Fours lack a stable identity). So they have difficulty connecting and staying grounded. More emotional and introverted than the Six-wing, though paradoxically more sociable; more interested in the personal and intrapsychic. Fives are cerebral, holding experience at arm's length; Fours internalize everything to intensify feeling. Together, "one of the richest subtypes," with possibilities for outstanding artistic as well as intellectual achievement.
- Examples named: Einstein, Heisenberg, Nietzsche, Georgia O'Keeffe, John Cage, John Lennon, k. d. lang, Laurie Anderson, Joyce, Dickinson, Kubrick, David Lynch, Buster Keaton, Gary Larson, Stephen King, Tim Burton, Clive Barker, Kafka, Umberto Eco, Sartre, Oriana Fallaci, Glenn Gould, Hannah Arendt, Kurt Cobain, van Gogh.
- **Healthy:** union of intuition and knowledge, sensitivity and insight, aesthetic and intellectual endowment; artists (writers, directors, designers, musicians, composers, choreographers). This subtype is "somewhat overlooked" because it does not fit the academic/scientific stereotype (the Six-wing). More synthetic, pulling things together; uses imagination more than analytic and systematic faculties; in science, drawn to areas emphasizing intuition and comprehensive vision over experimentation and data collection; sees beauty in a mathematical formula as an indication of truth (the order beauty represents confirms the rightness of an idea). The Four-wing adds desire for a unique personal vision and a propensity to "tinker" with familiar forms until almost unrecognizable, yielding startling innovations.
- **Average:** emotional depth but difficulty sustaining effort and working with others; more independent, resist imposed structures and deadlines; off-putting detachment (thoughts, introversion, emotional self-absorption); analytic powers used to keep people at arm's length; moody, hypersensitive to criticism of their work (it impinges on self-esteem); reclusive; highly creative, envisioning alternate realities in detail but lost in their cerebral landscapes; fantasy tends toward the surreal and fantastic rather than the romantic; impractical, specializing in trivia and intellectual games; attraction to dark, forbidden subject matter, to self-expression that disturbs others; some fascinated by the macabre and horrific; solace in self-indulgence (alcohol, drugs, sexual escapades).
- **Unhealthy:** debilitating depression plus disturbing aggressive impulses; envy mixed with contempt; desire to isolate mixed with regret; intellectual conflicts make emotional life hopeless and the reverse; one of the most alienated of all types (hopeless, nihilistic, self-inhibiting, isolated, self-hating); retreat into a bleak minimal existence cut off from all needs; Four's self-rejection plus Five's cynical nihilism = "relentlessly negative and terrifying" worldview; isolation, addiction, chronic depression common; suicide a real possibility. [s.17]

### Five with a Six-wing: "The Problem Solver" [s.17]
- The subtype most often associated with Fives: the intellectual interested in science, technology, facts and details; "analysts" and "cataloguers" of their environments; problem solvers who dissect components to see how things work. One of the most "difficult" types to contact intimately or sustain a relationship with. Both components are in the Thinking Triad; perhaps the most intellectual subtype, more disengaged from feelings than the Four-wing. Trust is hard (essential Five plus the Six-wing reinforcing anxiety, making risk-taking in relationships difficult). Inner tension: Fives find security by withdrawing, Sixes by working cooperatively with others; so relationships are erratic and not an important part of life.
- Examples named: Bill Gates, Stephen Hawking, Freud, Simone Weil, Jacob Bronowski, Darwin, E. O. Wilson, Marx, James Watson, Ursula K. Le Guin, Hitchcock, Doris Lessing, Bobby Fischer, B. F. Skinner, Isaac Asimov, Howard Hughes, Ezra Pound, Theodore Kaczynski.
- **Healthy:** more extroverted and focused on the external world than the Four-wing; not introspective, prefer to observe and understand; combine the Five's drive for mastery with the Six's quest for certainty: gift for drawing meaningful conclusions from disparate facts and predicting; technical subjects, engineering, science, philosophy, inventing, repair work; the Six-wing adds cooperation, discipline, persistence, aptitude for practical matters and sometimes business savvy; attention more on objects than people, but identify strongly with key people; deep feeling but restrained expression; intellectual playfulness, humor; once tested, others find deep capacity for friendship and commitment; endearing eagerness to be accepted though sometimes socially clumsy.
- **Average:** relationship problems; good organizational ability and endearing personal quality but added anxiety; do not know what to do with feelings; insensitive to their own and others' emotional needs; little awareness of how they come across ("the classic intellectual nerd, the socially inept oddball"); preoccupied, theoretical, absent-minded; avoid interpersonal conflict by burying themselves in work and by passive-aggressive techniques; rebellious and argumentative for no apparent reason (unconscious emotional associations). Contrast stated: Six-wing Fives cling to views and theories (**reductionism**) and antagonize those who disagree; Four-wing Fives reject all meaning (**nihilism**) and disturb the certainty of those who seem secure.
- **Unhealthy:** suspicious of people, counterphobic, contentious, volatile; extremely fearful of intimacy; unstable with paranoid tendencies; unconsciously seeking rescue yet fearfully rejecting and antagonizing supporters; Six-wing paranoia, inferiority feelings and persecution conviction reinforce isolation and mental distortion; extremely phobic, projecting dangers everywhere while retreating; may lash out at imagined enemies, sometimes with lethal results; psychotic breaks and madness possible. [s.17]

## Some Final Thoughts (Five) [s.17]
- The Five's overall struggle is between pairs of opposites: thinking and doing, fascination with the world and fear of it, identification with others and rejection, love and hate; an attraction-and-repulsion that began with ambivalence toward the parents. By defending against threats they also exclude the good, until there is nothing to identify with or believe in: "total nihilism."
- Like every type in the downward spiral, Fives bring about the thing they most fear (helpless, useless, incompetent): they became helpless and incompetent because they rejected attachment to everything; intensifying involvement with mental processes brought insecurity and powerlessness instead of security and power.
- Closing line: "If there is something perverse and dark—even demonic—about Fives, it is that to protect themselves they have relentlessly repulsed the world and other human beings. What then is left? Only a fascination with—and a terrifying attraction to—the darkness." [s.17]

# CHAPTER 8 — TYPE SIX: THE LOYALIST [s.18]

## The Six in profile [s.18]

| Band | Summary |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Able to engage others and identify with them; steadfast, earnest, affectionate. Trust is important: bonding, relationships, alliances. Dedicated to individuals and movements they deeply believe in; community builders: responsible, reliable, trustworthy; good foresight and organizational ability, natural troubleshooters; hardworking, persevering, sacrificing for others; create stability and security and bring a cooperative spirit. **At their best:** self-affirming, trusting of self and others, independent yet symbiotically interdependent and cooperative as an equal; belief in self leads to true courage, positive thinking, leadership, rich self-expression. |
| Average | Invest time and energy in whatever they believe is safe and stable; organize and structure, look to alliances and authorities for security and continuity; make many commitments hoping for reciprocity; constantly vigilant, anticipating problems; want clear guidelines, well-defined systems and procedures. To resist further demands, react passive-aggressively: evasive, indecisive, cautious, procrastinating, ambivalent; strong self-doubt and suspicion of others' motives; highly reactive, anxious, complaining, giving contradictory "mixed signals." To compensate for insecurity: belligerent, mean-spirited, sarcastic, blaming; highly partisan and defensive, dividing people into friends and enemies; authoritarian, prejudiced, fear-instilling to silence their own fears. |
| Unhealthy | Clingingly dependent and self-disparaging, acute inferiority feelings; see themselves as helpless and incompetent and seek a stronger authority or belief to resolve everything; submissive, masochistic. Feeling persecuted, "out to get them," they lash out irrationally and bring about what they fear; fanaticism, violence. Hysterical and seeking to escape punishment, they become self-destructive and suicidal: alcoholism, overdoses, "skid row," self-abasing behavior. |

**Key Motivations:** want to have security, to feel supported, to have the approval of others, to test the attitudes of others toward them, to defend their beliefs. [s.18]

**Examples** (abridged): Robert F. Kennedy, Malcolm X, George Bush, Tom Hanks, Bruce Springsteen, Gilda Radner, Princess Diana, Julia Roberts, Woody Allen, Marilyn Monroe, Oliver North, J. Edgar Hoover, Richard Nixon; fictional "George Costanza" and "Archie Bunker." [s.18]

## Overview of the Six [s.18]

- **Contradictions.** Sixes can be dependent yet value independence; want to trust and be trusted yet constantly test others; want authority's protection yet fear it; obedient and disobedient; fearful of aggression yet sometimes highly aggressive; seek security yet feel insecure; likable and endearing yet mean and hateful; reassured by traditional values yet may subvert them; want to escape punishment yet may bring it on themselves. Cause: anxiety makes them ricochet between psychological states, and they respond by looking to structures, beliefs, allies and authorities to quiet it. Epigraph (Michael Korda, *Power*): faith in a corporation, marriage, trade, religion or politics that offers rules and rewards obedience: "It's safer to be a domestic animal than a wild one." [s.18]
- Security comes from a "rock-of-ages allegiance" and investment in something outside themselves, something bigger and more powerful guiding them (IBM, the Communist party, the Republican party, the church); doctrines matter, but so does *having someone to trust and believe in*. [s.18]
- **In the Thinking Triad: the primary type.** Sixes are the most out of touch with the ability to decide and act on their own without reference to a trusted person, institution or belief system; they have difficulty trusting their own minds; once a reliable system is found, they must constantly evaluate any new ideas that contradict it. They look for guidelines or authority to give direction, tell them what they can and cannot do, give clarity, put limits on them: in a word, security. All types have some relationship with authority; whether supporting, rebelling against or fearing it, Sixes have the most issues in this area. [s.18]
- **Puzzling and reactive:** fluctuate quickly between near-opposite states (engaging, funny, then cranky and negative; decisive, then indecisive and self-doubting); seek approval of important people yet resist inferiority; obedient then openly disobedient. Others call them "easy to like but hard to get to know." [s.18]
- **Key: ambivalence.** Two sides oscillate between aggressive and dependent tendencies (strong/weak, dependent/independent, passive/aggressive, "sweet and sour"). They are also ambivalent toward themselves (like then disparage themselves, confidence then hopelessness, cowardice then sudden rage); a double set of dependent and aggressive impulses operates, since they react ambivalently both to external authority and to internal authority, their **superego**. At each Level they display a personality substantially different from what preceded and follows. [s.18]
- **Structure as stabilizer; the weakness.** They build structures for stability and continuity; given known rules and support they are steady and accomplish much. But they make **internal stability dependent on external stability**; when problems or uncertainty arise they are thrown into confusion and emotional reaction (so "many Sixes mistake themselves for Fours"). They cannot suppress one side (they cannot become independent by suppressing dependence); healthy, both sides work together; rising tension between the sides raises anxiety. [s.18]

### Problems with Anxiety and Insecurity [s.18]
- Sixes are the type **most conscious of anxiety** ("anxious that they are anxious"), whereas Fives displace it by distancing and Sevens repress it through constant activity.
- **Phobic vs. counterphobic coping:**

| | Phobic Six | Counterphobic Six |
|---|---|---|
| Stance | Dependent; turn quickly to others, especially authority figures; more self-doubting and emotionally vulnerable; can resemble Four | Questions or rebels against authority; quicker to confront; often more suspicious; determined to be independent; can resemble Eight |
| Strategy | Keep a low profile, avoid trouble, appease in conflict, stay within guidelines | Repress anxiety through action; react strongly and defensively if questioned; provoke others to find out where they stand |
| Fear | Being abandoned by those depended on | Being tricked or taken advantage of |
| Under conflict | Appeasement | Confrontational, even belligerent; the aggression is a reaction to the anxiety, not its direct expression |

  No Six is entirely either; the mix may differ by life area (aggressive with a spouse, dependent at work, or the reverse). Early lessons matter ("turn the other cheek" and walk away from bullies vs. be tough and fight back). The two responses tend to **alternate Level by Level**. [s.18]
- All Sixes protect themselves by **vigilance**: questioning, attention to details and problems, needing to know where others stand, and eventually paranoid tendencies. Ironically, Sixes must have "danger" on their minds to feel safe; the more paranoid, the more completely defended they try to be. [s.18]
- **Root of anxiety:** a continual feeling of being unsupported; they doubt they can support themselves and do not trust their ability to know what to do when security is at stake; they second-guess and look outside for reassurance (spouse, job, friends, the military, religion or belief system, therapist, spiritual practice, guru). They continually monitor support systems and "touch base" with allies to confirm they are "on the team"; average Sixes want to be liked but doubt they are, so they test others for evidence of approval or disapproval. In neurosis: paranoid, anxiety-ridden, unable to function. [s.18]
- **Jungian link:** Sixes correspond to Jung's **introverted feeling type**. Despite belonging to the Thinking Triad they are emotional because anxiety colors feeling. (Quote: it is "extremely difficult to give an intellectual account of the introverted feeling process.") The authors suggest thinking of Sixes as **"ambiverts."** The oscillation chain: affectionate, then fear of exploitation, then suspicion of the same person, then anxiety about the suspicion, then reassurance-seeking, then worry of being too ingratiating, then defensive overcompensation (acting as if they did not need the other); "If you have difficulty understanding someone who is a mass of contradictions, you are probably dealing with a Six." [s.18]
- They do not show emotions directly (unlike Twos), are seldom sentimental, take a less "rose-colored" view. Healthy to average Sixes are fairly clear about who they love or dislike; they are uncertain what they *think* and especially what to do, fearing the wrong action or signal. Mixed signals: "Sixes react to their feelings—particularly anxiety—and communicate their reactions rather than their feelings." [s.18]
- Independence matters: too compliant and self-esteem suffers; too aggressive and they fear alienating the people who provide security and being punished. Task: maintain both sides and reduce tension until they form "a reciprocal unit." [s.18]

### Parental Orientation [s.18]
- Sixes connected with their **protective-figure**: the adult who provided guidelines, structure and sometimes discipline (traditionally the patriarchal position: most often the father or a father figure such as a grandfather or teacher, but sometimes the mother or an older sibling). Children wanted its approval and felt anxious without it. As they grew, the connection shifted to substitutes: civil authorities, belief systems.
- Depth-psychology view: the protective-figure helps the child break away from the mother to function independently; Sixes unconsciously seek people or structures that will help them become independent; ironically the more unsure, the more dependent they become on them.
- They powerfully internalize the relationship, whatever its quality. **If the protective-figure was benevolent**, they look for similar guidance (spouse, job, therapist, mentor), try to please, follow rules, but feel deeply betrayed if trust is violated. **If abusive, unfair or controlling**, they feel at odds with those with power, fear being "in trouble" and unjustly punished, and adopt a defensive, rebellious attitude, projecting the cruel figure into many relationships; extremely dysfunctional childhoods may produce self-destructive, wasted lives enacting the figure's negative image.
- Like Threes (who abandon themselves for their nurturing-figures), Sixes abandon themselves to win the protective-figure's support, believing enough support will bring independence. They feel cut off from internal guidance and act out phobically (dependent) or counterphobically (assertive); either way they look outside for reassurance. As they deteriorate, dependence or hysterical reaction increases "until they actually destroy their own security."
- **Internal check-in:** taught not to trust inner guidance, they test their actions against the internalized standards of the protective-figure, their superego; like Ones they try to find the "right" action by imagining how mentors, allies and authorities would respond; for major decisions they can go around for days, "as though Sixes must regularly hold committee meetings in their imagination." Even blustering counterphobic Sixes need their support network; less healthy Sixes may undermine it through reactivity and paranoia, then work hard to reconnect with some other source of security. [s.18]

## Analyzing the Healthy Six [s.18]

### Level 1: The Valiant Hero
- Very healthy Sixes learn to **rely on themselves**: in touch with inner authority, trusting themselves without nagging self-doubt; secure, accepted, comfortable. Self-reliance is internal: staying connected with inner strength and inner knowing, which brings clarity about what to do from moment to moment and the fortitude and will to accomplish what life requires. It is "emphatically not the same thing as reacting aggressively out of fear"; it arises from realizing one's intrinsic capacity and value, a shift from seeing protection outside oneself to a sustaining faith in oneself and life that is not a belief but a lived experience. They are no longer reactive but mature; they meet major crises with "equanimity, determination, and grace."
- They give confidence and courage to others (positive thinking, reassuring presence, calm, decisiveness, tireless work for a greater good); face great danger to champion others' welfare or speak out against injustice (resembling healthy Eights or Ones); flexible, work well with others or alone.
- **Dynamic interdependence:** true reciprocity; no one dominates or is inferior; they support and are supported, love and are loved, work alone and together, are coequal partners who trust themselves and therefore can trust those worthy of trust.
- Deepest feelings emerge; they express themselves effectively in personal life and work; with talent and training they may become outstanding artists or leaders, especially effective as leaders because they know insecurity and can help create genuine security for others. Courage is a high achievement won "not merely against external difficulties but against chronic inner doubt." (La Rochefoucauld: "The only security is courage.")
- Caution: anxiety is part of the human condition and will never be entirely removed; self-reliant Sixes do not expect absolute security (an impossible goal) but harness the inevitable anxiety to higher goals. [s.18]

### Level 2: The Engaging Friend
- Relatively healthy Sixes begin to look for security outside themselves, fearing abandonment; they have lost touch with inner strength; they are "looking for someone or something to trust, because they have begun to lose faith in themselves" (a subtle change of direction). They scan for allies, supporters and security-enhancing situations and move out to connect or join projects, developing the capacity to **engage others emotionally**.
- An endearing, personable, hard-to-pin-down quality: they unconsciously arouse strong emotional responses; perhaps via genuine curiosity about others and desire for mutually beneficial connections, as if silently asking "Can we be friends? Can we work together?" Cues: eye contact, smiling, subliminal body language; not necessarily seductive or fawning. Like a child's trust, expectation and love toward parents, communicating "There is nothing to fear here"; winsome, playful, silly, childlike mischief; **droll, self-deprecating humor** and teasing; good-natured bantering is a sign of affection and security.
- Reliable and trustworthy: back promises with prompt, consistent action (can be mistaken for healthy Ones); steadiness, common sense, a solid down-to-earth quality distinct from healthy Fives and Sevens (the other Thinking types). They can **perceive threats before they become unmanageable**: vigilant alertness to hazards, taking measures to keep everyone safe.
- Relationships are mutually beneficial: friendship, trust, support asked "more by deed than by word" and offered in return; spontaneous nurturing and affection. Note: the desire to engage the other automatically puts the other person in the superior position, "something which will have significant consequences later on." [s.18]

### Level 3: The Committed Worker
- Once they find reliable people, ideas, situations, they fear losing those connections; engaging others raises at least a glimmer of anxiety; they strengthen alliances by **fully committing** to people, jobs and projects.
- Practical, responsible, disciplined, persistent, meticulous in detail and craft; proud of their work; effective at keeping organizations running (corporations, small businesses, family budgets); bills paid and taxes covered; thrifty, industrious. Often skilled at analysis and troubleshooting; vigilance becomes facility with details; give and take directions well; prefer structures with clear parameters, within which they feel safe to be creative and inventive (computer programmers, engineers, legal professionals, CPAs, musicians); highly developed craftsmanship; they often rise "like the tenacious tortoise who beats the faster, flashier hare."
- They bring an egalitarian spirit and sense of community welfare; collegial atmosphere (partners not superior and functionary); work cooperatively to maintain institutions; local politics (school board, improvement committees), public service; see themselves as **stewards** of work and environment.
- Not afraid to raise questions about impropriety or abuse of power; rally support against injustice and inform others of community problems. Contrast: average Sixes tend to lose their nerve here, use passive-aggressive techniques or become fear-instilling saboteurs. In healthy Sixes the willingness to question authority reflects interest in general welfare and "commitment to integrity and honesty."
- Personal bonds: devotion to special others and wanting reciprocity; family (even "a family of friends") is a symbol of emotional support and stability; closeness reduces fear of abandonment. The authors defend the virtues (perseverance, faithfulness, honesty, hard work, commitment to family, friends and group welfare) as needing "no apology" in a "Me first!" society. [s.18]

## Analyzing the Average Six [s.18]

### Level 4: The Dutiful Loyalist
- Committed Sixes fear "rocking the boat" and wrecking their hard-won stability; they worry about all that could go wrong and work harder to be accepted and approved by allies and authorities; take on greater commitments and obligations, loading themselves with responsibilities to "cover all the bases" ("Be prepared"); multiple jobs, maintaining the house, checking family finances; eager to fulfill duties, finding more work as soon as pressure lessens.
- They invest only in institutions with a **"track record"**: check credentials of doctor, therapist, lawyer; inspect the background of a car or home; want commitments to endure; a certain caution limits choices.
- Self-reliance fades; they become more attached to systems of thought that give answers, while doubting their own ideas, seeking people who "seem to know what they are talking about" to confirm beliefs. They look for precedents in documents, authoritative texts, rules and regulations, "scriptures" of one sort or another; average Sixes as leaders often form committees and govern by consensus. They second-guess even when following protocols and check with a drill sergeant, priest or the law to interpret rules; they want to know exactly what the rules are and how far they must stretch them before breaking them.
- Major decisions (career, family, job stability) bring knots of anxiety: conclude, then doubt or negate; yet resent others deciding for them; every important choice triggers "a storm of anxiety, consultation, and doubt."
- Not "spineless milquetoasts": true believers observing legitimate precepts with the weight of authority and tradition behind them; do not mind permission, rules and procedures (adept at making rules work for them). Many are traditionalists, organization people and team players, "the backbone of groups of all sorts" (fraternities, clubs, corporations, parties, universities, unions, religious affiliations); structure strengthens them; bonding yields close friendships.
- **Two drawbacks:** (1) they act from obligation; responsibility wears on them and they take on unwanted tasks; (2) identification with a group, view, tradition fosters **parochialism**: "them" versus "us"; they police who pulls their weight, and others' lesser commitment angers and threatens them.
- Loyalty almost without bounds ("Once a friend, always a friend"); love may turn to hatred but never to indifference; commitments are deep identifications, so they become slower to commit and ask who is worthy of loyalty. [s.18]

### Level 5: The Ambivalent Pessimist
- Overextended, they fear they cannot meet demands but also fear alienating alliances; they must decide whom to "let down"; they test others (including allies and authorities) about feelings toward them; anxious about themselves and others, reacting defensively or compliantly, sometimes both at once.
- They react strongly to their own thoughts, feelings and actions (an intensification of second-guessing): with calm they keep a cool head, but problems spin their inner order into nervousness, confusion and impulsive reactions. They may decide they are too obedient and react against authority at least occasionally for self-esteem, then suspect they are being taken advantage of; healthy Sixes are consistent, average Sixes evasive and unpredictable (sometimes by denying responsibility).
- Deep ambivalence: fluctuating compliance and aggression; "knowing how quickly they can turn on someone else, Sixes feel that others may well turn on them," so they become guarded and evasive.
- Overloaded, they resist new ideas and acquaintances; skeptical of new views; fear change as a threat to security; positional and entrenched thinking; sputtering assertions and specious arguments.
- Losing inner authority: difficulty acting or deciding alone or leading; dithering, procrastinating; cautious, timid decisions padded with legalisms and precedents; wait until the last moment, then work under great pressure.
- **Passive-aggressive ambivalence** (Theodore Millon, *Disorders of Personality*, quoted: the vacillating donkey who never settles on which bale of hay is best). "One foot on the accelerator and the other on the brake": tense, tired, complaining, grumpy, negative; dinner conversation as litany of complaints; "make mountains out of molehills," a reason why any idea will not work; drugs and alcohol to ease tension risk trading one dependency for another.
- Others find them frustrating: no straight answer ("yes means no and vice versa"; agreeing to dinner without a date); holding people with one hand while putting them off with the other; mixed signals force others to take responsibility; they complain loudly if things go amiss while avoiding blame.
- **Indirect aggression:** put someone on hold and "forget" to return; "forget" a deadline or lose materials; edgy sarcastic humor saying the opposite of what they mean ("Of course I respect you—I treat you with all the respect you deserve"); the subtext sinks in later.
- **Turning point:** for the first time they realize their attitudes toward themselves and others are confused; suspicion of others and of themselves; do not know their thoughts or feelings or what to do. [s.18]

### Level 6: The Authoritarian Rebel
- Rather than resolve doubts they react against them: fearing ambivalence costs support, they overcompensate with zeal and aggression to prove they are not anxious, indecisive or dependent ("pushed around"); they assert the aggressive side to repress the passive side. Whatever their basic type (phobic or counterphobic), they become **openly counterphobic**, battling what aroused the anxiety (innocent form: a child afraid of the dark entering a dark room); here they overcompensate, blaming and berating threats, rebellious, belligerent, harassing and obstructing.
- Aggression is not true strength but a way of feeling superior by frustrating or isolating others; Sixes who feared being cut off now isolate others and cut off their support; masters of **intrigue and office politics** knocking rivals "out of the race"; in power they become the arbitrary, unjust, vindictive authority they detest: "martinets and petty tyrants, full of bombast and bluster, dangerous but weak—and therefore especially dangerous."
- Extremely **partisan and authoritarian**: strict "for us" or "against us"; "My country (authority, leader, beliefs) right or wrong"; challenge to beliefs is an attack on their way of life; irrational fear and hatred of others as the manifestation of anxiety. Prejudiced and closed-minded, siege mentality, suspicious of outsiders as potential enemies; fearing conspiracies, they conspire; turn opinion against enemies and even insufficiently loyal members of their own group. Overcompensation makes them deviate from their own professed beliefs (believers in freedom become bigots denying civil rights; Christians hate; the law-and-order person breaks the law in the law's name).
- **Leaders at this stage** tap a group's fears, blame others, offer few realistic solutions; sought for aggressiveness in upholding "traditional values"; usually **demagogues** inciting insecurity to gain a mob; "Insecurity, not courage, is the impetus."
- Difficult to work *for* something; energized by being *against*: the classic revolutionaries who, once they overcome enemies, are uninterested in building a more just system. They **need enemies** to discharge anxiety (without someone to blame, guilt and fears escalate) and find a scapegoat if none exists, assigned the basest motives to justify treatment; the scapegoat may be an unpopular authority or the weaker and less supported (the book lists those they often hate: blacks, Jews, homosexuals, foreigners, "outsiders" of all sorts, who embody vulnerabilities Sixes fear in themselves); militancy and bigotry cut in many directions, and Sixes in minority groups too will blame and marginalize someone at Level 6.
- Their view: they have been handed a "raw deal," exploited by powerful forces; film *Network*: "I'm mad as hell, and I'm not going to take this any more!" They take anxieties out on outsiders and on family, pets, weaker coworkers or schoolmates, even strangers.
- Functioning at this Level usually indicates a very dysfunctional childhood; they project the unjust protective-figure into the world (bullies everywhere) and use the same aggressive tactics (the beaten-for-crying child who feels like striking out at a crying child). Their belligerent state is not permanent (it rests on a shifting psychic foundation) but can last long enough to do serious harm; persistence alienates allies and makes real enemies, leaving their position tenuous. [s.18]

## Analyzing the Unhealthy Six [s.18]

### Level 7: The Overreacting Dependent
- After aggressive or defiant episodes they fear ruined relationships, powerful enemies, punishment, abandonment; intense anxiety and reassurance-seeking ("Beneath the armor of the tough authoritarian is a frightened, insecure child"). Once blustering, they shift abruptly: tearful, obsequious, disgusted with themselves for not being tough enough to stand alone.
- Pattern of **self-disparagement and massive insecurity**: convinced they are incompetent; clingingly dependent on allies or authorities or, if deserted, on anyone who takes them in; loyalty has deteriorated into dependency; relieved to get through the day; no decisive constructive actions; lean on spouses, friends, family waiting to be directed; terrified of mistakes; avoid responsibility ("I was just doing what you told me to do"; "The priest said it was all right"; "But my friends were doing it, too"), which relieves anxiety but undermines independence.
- Feel worthless; whining and self-disparagement weaken confidence and drain others; they induce anxieties in others, driving people away, making themselves more "morbidly dependent" (Karen Horney). "Anyone who likes me must have something wrong with him." Self-disparagement says "You ought to love me because I am helpless and no good without you." They discount encouragement; they want a stronger, decisive "good" protective-figure to take over.
- Still suspicious; confused by true kindness; those who suffered extremely abusive authority figures respond best to similar treatment, and gentleness feels alien; they passive-aggressively push away helpers. They are drawn to the same sorts who hurt them ("bad company": those who deepen dependence, encourage paranoia, prey on insecurity), feel a misplaced camaraderie with disgruntled "outsiders," and choose abusive or indifferent romantic partners ("What does she really want from me?"): "Bullies, addicts, hysterics, and rogues they at least understand."
- Work: poor performance, anxiety, absences from psychosomatic problems; alcohol or other drugs to deaden anxiety. Depression: boiling anxiety unexpressed (fear of abandonment or losing control) becomes lethargy and incompetence; rage and paranoia grow beneath. They have a **vested interest in keeping their problems** (complaining draws reassurance); if the helper is as unhealthy as the Six, "the picture becomes frightening indeed." [s.18]

### Level 8: The Paranoid Hysteric
- Oscillation again: from self-disparagement to overreacting, hysterical anxiety: panicky about themselves, hysterical and paranoid about others; free-floating anxiety so great that reality is misperceived and everything is a crisis. They **project their own aggressions onto others** and become paranoid about what they find: a reversal from fearing themselves to fearing others. They are positive the verdict is negative (a gruff boss means firing; a landlord dispute means eviction or a hired hit man); they see conspiracies everywhere, feel persecuted especially by authority figures; terrible conflict: they need authority's reassurance more than ever yet believe authority wants to destroy them.
- Furious at those who let them down, they do not recognize their own hatred and project it; hypervigilance outward; "like a sentry that must stay on guard twenty-four hours a day." Coincidence becomes fact; innocent remarks confirm paranoia; a stranger is a policeman, spy or madman; suspiciousness becomes "insane paranoid delusions."
- Delusions of persecution may alternate with compensating **delusions of grandeur and reference** (watched by God or a childhood hero; the FBI pursuing them over a nuclear antigravity device); or paranoia alone (phone bugged, mail read by the CIA, food poisoned, friends plotting); they think themselves more realistic than anyone.
- They rant about enemies, individuals never met or whole groups as monsters; band together in conspiracies whose only mission is to attack others, or become loners developing paranoid theories. Bundles of irrational fears, absolutely certain something horrible is about to happen; impossible to reason with.
- **Danger:** they cannot restrain anxiety and may lash out at allies, strangers who symbolize their fears, or a hero who let them down. Mild forms: hysterical outburst, smashed furniture, screaming, physical violence with a friend or relative. Darkest forms: assassination of a political or popular figure, a random public shooting, the collective insanity of mob violence, gangs and hate groups. The authors note most Sixes do not get trapped at this Level and many have help; the unrelenting terror may still drive them one step further. [s.18]

### Level 9: The Self-Defeating Masochist
- If they have overreacted precipitously or cannot stop projecting, they hasten their own downfall to provoke attention and connection; sure punishment is inevitable, they punish themselves to expiate guilt and lessen the authority's wrath. A further reversal: instead of dreading disaster, they bring defeat and degradation on themselves first, turning on themselves the aggression once aimed at scapegoats. They do this not to end their relationship with authority but to **reestablish a protective one**: "they at least save themselves from being defeated by someone else"; self-punishment eases guilt and defuses condemnations that might push them to suicide.
- Methods are irrational and may kill: dropping out as vagrants in skid-row conditions; excessive alcohol and drugs, flirting with death; suicide attempts that work more effectively than planned.
- **Masochism explained:** not pleasure in suffering as such but hope that suffering will draw someone who will save them: "Punish me because I have been bad. Then you can love me again." (Wilhelm Reich via Hinsie and Campbell: the provocation defends against punishment and anxiety by substituting a milder punishment and putting the authority in the light of "See how badly you treat me," behind which lies disappointed excessive demand for love and fear of being left alone.)
- Even being punished retains a degree of control and reassurance of not being abandoned: "to be punished by the authority is still to be loved." This recalls the abused child who prefers a horrible parent to none. When even this is gone, they see little recourse but to end their lives. [s.18]

## The Dynamics of the Six [s.18]

(Also in the repository's existing Enneagram material: Six disintegrates to Three, integrates to Nine.)

### Direction of disintegration: the Six goes to Three
Starting at Level 4, stress brings average to unhealthy Three traits: compensating for insecurity by acting as they believe others expect and by trying to convince themselves and others of their value and capability.

| Level | Six at Three |
|---|---|
| 4 | Careers, recognition, "getting ahead" as buffers against loss of safety; competitiveness for limited prestige and positions; competition among systems that bolster identification ("Harvard is better than Yale"; "I use a Mac, not an IBM clone"; "My Cowboys won the Super Bowl again!"); pile on responsibilities and bury themselves in work to repress anxiety: when insecure, they work harder. |
| 5 | Pressured by obligations, ambivalent yet unwilling to alienate or reveal self-doubt: turn on the charm, more "friendly" or "professional" as needed; adaptation driven by fear of rejection; gauge responses; usually fails (others read uneasiness; cheerfulness seems rehearsed), which increases anxiety and self-doubt. |
| 6 | Defiant, aggressive; bully; arrogance and highhandedness; overestimate abilities and inflate accomplishments to compensate for inferiority; promote self, business, causes; belittle rivals; demand recognition and become hostile without it; may hype meager successes or sexual conquests; group competitiveness degenerates into hostility (fights over football teams, tearing down rival spiritual or political views): pride in affiliations becomes "a frenzied contest for supremacy." |
| 7 | Fear they have eroded their base of support; cover mistakes and deny involvement; keep offering help ("I'll get that done for you") but too dysfunctional to do it, so deceive by saying things are done; not maliciously but to escape punishment, hoping to repair damage if they placate others long enough; the fabrications become more pressure and panic. |
| 8 | Dangerous obsessions; fear their hatred and revenge plans will be uncovered; devious and duplicitous; the "nice, quiet person down the block" until paranoia erupts; may vanish from a job or relationship without explanation; false identities or presenting a different self to new acquaintances: terror of being seen without camouflage. |
| 9 | Instead of self-punishment, the lowest Level of Three: anxiety and rage explode in **psychopathic fury**, aggression turned on others to see them suffer; authoritarian bigotry returns in more dangerous form; strike out violently to overcome inferiority "once and for all"; vindictively hurt others who may not be the cause. Still fundamentally Sixes: the violence is another self-defeat that demands the strictest punishment (imprisonment, execution, or becoming the object of hatred and revenge). |

### Direction of integration: the Six goes to Nine
- Resolving ambivalence and anxiety about self and others is "precisely what happens when they go to Nine." Integrating Sixes are more emotionally open, receptive, sympathetic (emotional spectrum widens), emotionally stable, peaceful, self-possessed; they overcome dependence and become autonomous persons others can and do rely on; they reassure and support rather than seek reassurance and support.
- "A revolutionary change for the better": independent and yet closer to others than ever. Unexpected payoff: **more friends** than when they looked to others for protection; no longer reactive, they form stable unions; others seek them out. Playfulness and humor remain, with the Nine's sunniness, optimism and kindheartedness. They attain security and the ability to trust others, because they now trust themselves; transcend anxiety; feel grounded and at ease. [s.18]

## The Major Subtypes of the Six (wings) [s.18]

### Six with a Five-wing: "The Defender" [s.18]
- The traits conflict: Sixes orient toward affiliation, Fives toward detachment to avoid influence; both seek safety (Sixes through alliances and systems of thought, Fives by retreating and tinkering with or dismantling systems). Result: a subtype that sees itself fighting for the "little person" while drawn to systems, alliances and beliefs with strong authoritarian elements. Can seem like Ones (serious, self-controlled, committed to moral, ethical and political beliefs); like Eights, outspoken and passionate, with less concern about being liked than the Seven-wing.
- Examples named: Richard Nixon, Robert F. Kennedy, George Bush, Malcolm X, Robert Redford, Diane Keaton, Jessica Lange, Bruce Springsteen, Phil Donahue, Billy Graham, Walter Mondale, Joseph McCarthy, J. Edgar Hoover, Oliver North, Meir Kahane, John Hinckley, Jr.
- **Healthy:** the Six's organization and personal engagement with the Five's perceptiveness and curiosity; sometimes a strong intellectual streak; technical expertise, practical problem solvers, communicators, educators, pundits; medicine, law, engineering (master a system of knowledge in a field whose rules are established); political causes and community service; strong identification with the underdog (spokespeople or champions); greater concentration and focus, sometimes too narrow; shrewd observers who prize foresight and predicting reactions; more original perceptions than the Seven-wing but seem extremely competent and knowledgeable rather than intellectual.
- **Average:** more intense and independent; less likely to go to others for reassurance (one or two mentors, otherwise "gut out" problems alone); hardworking, loyal to systems and people, which fuels partisan stances and rival competitiveness; constricted emotional expression, detached, cerebral, pessimistic; the Five-wing adds secretiveness and compartmentalization that feed suspicion; see the world as dangerous, become reactive and aggressive, denounce or scapegoat perceived threats, fear conspiracies and may hatch plots.
- **Unhealthy:** increasingly paranoid and obsessed with security; extremely needy; alcohol or drugs for anxiety, paranoid delusions and inferiority; the Five-wing adds cynicism and nihilism, isolation, desperation, capacity for sociopathic action; rage outbreaks with breaks from reality; self-sabotage to atone for guilt (hidden from others because of reclusiveness); propensity for violence and sadomasochistic tendencies in sexuality; murder and suicide real possibilities. [s.18]

### Six with a Seven-wing: "The Buddy" [s.18]
- Traits reinforce each other: more extroverted, sociable, fun-seeking, less intensely focused on environment or self than the Five-wing; but a dynamic tension: the Six focuses on commitment, responsibility and sacrificing personal pursuits for security; the Seven on experience, satisfying personal need, keeping options open (can sometimes seem like Twos). Affable, supportive, strongly identified with others; more eager to be liked and more hesitant to speak out; the Seven-wing adds playfulness and enthusiasm, but the Six component is uneasy with it, so they monitor others' reactions to see if their behavior is acceptable.
- Examples named: Jay Leno, Tom Hanks, Johnny Carson, Sally Field, Candice Bergen, Gilda Radner, Princess Diana, Marilyn Monroe, Julia Roberts, Mikhail Baryshnikov, Patrick Swayze, Ted Kennedy, Andy Rooney, Rush Limbaugh, "Fred Mertz," "Archie Bunker," "the Cowardly Lion."
- **Healthy:** want to be accepted, secure *and happy*, especially materially; broad interests, hobbies; friendly, not solemn; performing arts (acting, popular music) or professions combining energy with discipline and craft (advertising, marketing, managing, law); self-deprecating, turning fears into reassurance, bonding or humor; extremely playful and funny, humor a salient coping means; more extroverted.
- **Average:** hardworking and loyal but procrastinate and struggle to initiate projects; depend more on others' reassurance, seek advice from several sources and are more indecisive with conflicting advice; handle pressure poorly: impulsive, grumpy, peevish; humor deflects, passive-aggression escapes unpleasant situations; complain and fret, turning the Seven's envisioning of options into envisioning everything that can go wrong; comfort through distractions (overeating, drinking, substances, nonproductive "hanging out"); highly opinionated and vocal about likes and dislikes but fewer strong political stands; displace anxieties about personal failings and relationships onto helpless "third parties" ("kicking the cat" syndrome).
- **Unhealthy:** more dependent, do not disguise emotional needs; stuck in abusive working conditions, dependent on people or substances; inferiority plus desire to escape themselves; few means to deal with anxiety; **flight from anxiety, tending to become manic rather than paranoid**; hysterical overreactions, unpredictable and reckless; alternate between clinging to destructive relationships and lashing out at supporters; debilitating panic attacks (anxiety, not aggression, has the upper hand); suicide attempts as a way of eliciting help likely. [s.18]

## Some Final Thoughts (Six) [s.18]
- Sixes have ruined their desire for security; unhealthy Sixes are "their own worst enemies"; by persisting in masochistic, self-defeating behavior they drive away everyone depended on, becoming abandoned and alone, the things most feared.
- **Practical guidance:** forming alliances is fine but Sixes must be aware of their allies' character, since allies influence them strongly: commitment to a good person helps Sixes be good; commitment to a person who brings out their aggressions and insecurities has very bad effects.
- Sixes fear being alone because without at least one other person they would be at the mercy of anxiety; others put boundaries on the fluctuation between anxiety and its response, aggression; but Sixes also need some tension in their psyches to maintain the sense of self, so they interact with people both to control anxiety and to stimulate it, and look to others to save them from the consequences of both. The "insoluble conundrum" is escaped by learning they need not react to or depend on people if they trust themselves, and they can trust themselves only by discovering "a quiet source of inner guidance within themselves"; by trusting it "they find the security, support, and direction they have been looking for." [s.18]

# Gaps and cautions

- **Coverage:** The whole range (sections 16 to 18, lines 1340 to 2308) was read. Types Four, Five and Six are complete. The book's lists of famous examples are reproduced only in abridged form; the book assigns each famous name to a type and wing (the abridgment is an extraction choice, not a verdict).
- **Compressed:** long epigraph quotations (Winnicott/Storr, William James, Rank/Fine, Stendhal, Jung's *Psychological Types* passages, Korda, Millon, Reich/Hinsie and Campbell, La Rochefoucauld, Hamlet) are summarized in one line each, not reproduced.
- **Source quirks:** the EPUB text contains stray characters and typos (for example "Seren Kierkegaard," "Heisenburg," "clanger of being overwhelmed," "mining their lives" for "ruining," "ground-edness," "areproblem solvers," "honor at the emptiness" where "horror" is meant, "lightness of an idea" where "rightness" is meant). These were read as the evident intended words; no passage was unreadable. A stray asterisk after the Five's Level 9 schizophrenia sentence in the source likely marks a footnote that is not present in this range. [s.17]
- **Practical-guidance coverage:** the book's "Some Final Thoughts" and in-text remarks are the practical guidance for these types; no separate exercise sections (such as "Wake-up Calls" or "Instinctual Variants") appear in this range. This edition of the book places the wing, arrow and level material per type chapter; centers/triads appear mainly in each chapter's Overview.
- **Repository overlap:** the Dynamics sections (Four to Two and One; Five to Seven and Eight; Six to Three and Nine) restate arrow directions that the repository's Enneagram README says are already held in `Enneagram_Dynamics.md`; this extraction records this book's own level-by-level formulation of them (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material, in direction only; the level-by-level detail above is this book's own).
- **Modern-day references:** the book (a 1990s text) cites contemporary public figures and subcultures (grunge, cyberpunk, heavy metal) as illustrations; these are dated examples, preserved here only where they carry the point.
- **Sensitive content:** the Four chapter describes suicide, sexual compulsion and self-harm patterns; the Six chapter describes bigotry, scapegoating and violence at Levels 6 and 8. These are summarized faithfully as the book frames them (as unhealthy patterns), not endorsed.
