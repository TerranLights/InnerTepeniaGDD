## Overview of this range

This is the final third of the book, titled **Part III: Inner Dimensions**, followed by two closing essays, notes, an index and summary tables. Part II (the nine type chapters, not in this range) has already described each type. Part III **systematizes** those descriptions and then adds new machinery: (1) the **pitfalls and invitations**, **root sins** and **fruits of the spirit** diagrams, (2) the **27 subtypes** (three per type, by sexual / social / self-preserving drive), (3) **idealization and self-image** ("I'm good when I..."), (4) **temptation, avoidance, defense mechanism** per type, (5) the **triple continuum** (immature, "normal," mature), (6) the **wings** (both neighbors, in all three conditions), (7) the **arrows**, called **false and true consolation**, with full discussion for each type, (8) practical uses of the system (self-study, partnership, family, spiritual exercises, parish, discussion groups), (9) **"Jesus and the Enneagram,"** which assigns each type a cluster of qualities of Christ with Gospel passages, (10) the Enneagram and prayer, (11) a Pauline argument that the system is a signpost to freedom, not a determinism, and (12) two essays by Dietrich Koller: a sermon in which the nine sins visit the infant Jesus, and a five-point account of "the repentance that no one regrets" (inner observer, discernment of spirits, spiritual warfare). The register throughout is Christian spiritual direction. Where a statement is theological rather than about the typology itself, it is labeled **[CHRISTIAN FRAMING]** below, and a separate section at the end collects those framings.

**Key vocabulary (authors' own terms):** *pitfalls / dead ends*; *invitations* (their word for "holy ideas" or "ideas of the higher spiritual center"); *root sins* (the passions); *fruits of the spirit* (redeemed passions, roughly the virtues); *life centers* (gut, heart, head); *subtypes* (sexual, social, self-preserving); *idealization*; *self-image*; *temptation*; *avoidance*; *defense mechanism*; *immature / "normal" / mature* (also *disintegration / stagnation / integration*); *wings*; *arrows*; *stresspoint*, *point of consolation*, *false consolation*, *true consolation*; *integration / regression*; *discernment of spirits*; *inner observer*.

## 1. "Repentance" and Reorientation [pp.129-135]

### 1.1 Centers and the claim that everyone has all three [p.129]
The Enneagram sorts types into **gut, heart, and head** types according to which life center primarily defines the person. The three drives also map onto these centers (sexual, social, self-preserving; see 1.5). Yet each person is "a microcosm" in which all three centers operate: all nine types think, feel and act, have a sex drive, a self-preservation drive and social impulses; all nine commit "errors of the mind and errors of the heart." This section is described as the place where each type's specific mistaken attitudes and developmental possibilities are systematized again.

### 1.2 Pitfalls and invitations [pp.129-130]
- **Pitfalls (dead ends in thinking)** are the characteristic wrong turn of each type. The authors link them to the Greek *metanoiete* in Mark 1:15, which they gloss as "Change your mind, think differently!" [CHRISTIAN FRAMING: repentance as re-thinking]. 
- **Invitations (calling)**: each type's counter-offer. The authors say these are called "holy ideas" in Enneagram literature, but they prefer "invitation" to stress that freedom is "primarily an offer from God and not our own doing" [CHRISTIAN FRAMING].
- **The inner triangle:** the three central types 9, 3, 6 (one in each center, linking gut, heart, head) carry the three theological virtues of 1 Cor. 13:13: the invitations Love (9), Hope (3), Faith (6). Love "stands at the top, marking the beginning and the end of the circle." [p.130] [CHRISTIAN FRAMING]

The nine sins promise advancement but produce what they try to prevent: loneliness, absurdity, emptiness; they separate us from God, fellow humans, creation and most of all from ourselves, and block the goal of reconciliation with self, neighbor and God. ### 1.4 Fruits of the spirit [p.131]
The nine **fruits of the spirit** "are images of redeemed passion or sin." They cannot be produced, only allowed to grow; they are "gifts of divine grace." The fruits express the true self, "not disfigured by the power." [CHRISTIAN FRAMING: grace language, Paul's 1 Cor. 12:31]

### 1.5 Subtypes: sexual, social, self-preserving [pp.131-136]
- The three centers correspond to three basic drives: the **sex drive**, the **social impulse**, and the drive for **self-preservation**. These are "the raw material of our being" and "unconvertible"; killing them (as ascetics have tried) truncates the person. The aim is to control them so they serve life. [p.131]
- Imagery: the drives as **dragons**. One can slay them (St. George, the Archangel Michael: "the 'masculine' way") or tame them (Martha's dragon that followed her willingly; Francis of Assisi befriending the wolf of Gubbio). [CHRISTIAN FRAMING: saints' legends as models]
- Everyone is dominated by one drive, giving **three subtypes per type, 27 in all**. Life situation influences which is operative at the moment: the authors assume people mainly live out the **social** subtype at work, the **sexual** subtype in partnership, and the **self-preserving** subtype in solitude and withdrawal; but one subtype dominates the personality overall. [p.131]

## 2. Idealized Self-Image and Guilt Feelings [pp.136-137]

- Through predispositions, parents, environment and society we form **ideals** whose realization we pursue; the **self-image** is largely built from them and we use them to measure others. **Guilt** arises when we fail the ideal; we also reproach others who disregard our ideals. [p.136-137]
- The internalized ideals are "often false or at least exaggerated," and trying to realize them is exactly how we run into our pitfall and sin. We feel guilt mostly when we miss the ideal, not when we "fall into the trap." Example: TWOs feel guilty when not doing enough for others, but identification with the helper role is exactly where they fall into pride. [p.137]
- The formula: **"I'm good when I..."**. These "self-definitions" can help a person identify their type. [p.137]

- Each type is a **continuum** with gradations between two poles: the immature or unhealthy personality and the mature or healthy personality; "normal" or average representatives sit in between. ## 5. The Wings [pp.141-143]

### 5.1 Concept
- The Enneagram circle is "a sort of *perpetuum mobile*, a self-renewing cyclical system." Energies change gradually and continuously around it; there are no jumps between types. Hence each number contains something of its two neighbors, which "help to determine it and balance it out": these are its **wings**. FIVE, for example, contains parts of FOUR and SIX. [p.141]
- **The authors work with two wings per type** (note 2 on p.176: unlike Riso, who assumes only one wing per type). The two wing energies can appear in immature, "normal" or mature form regardless of the main energy's development. [p.141]
- Wing work is "an important first step" in integrating the whole personality. Example: pure ONEs become arrogant, self-righteous, hypercritical; the TWO wing adds love, affection and service; the NINE wing offsets ONE's workaholism ("the workaholic has a lazy wing that... assures the necessary recovery"). [p.141]
- **Life-stage claim:** in the first half of life usually one wing is developed; a task of the second half is to turn to the underdeveloped second wing, which even people who know nothing of the Enneagram do unconsciously. [p.141]
- **Typing heuristic:** if someone wavers between two adjacent numbers, they are probably the type plus one wing; between three adjacent numbers, the middle one is probably the type; between two numbers separated by one (for example NINE and TWO), look to the middle number (ONE). Since people are often blind to their main fault, they may first identify with "neighboring sins." [p.141]
- **Passing through intermediate points:** to reach an energy that is not a direct neighbor, you pass through the points in between. Ebert's example: as a TWO beginning a sabbatical, he unconsciously sought NINE (peace) and had to pass through ONE (cleaning up, paying bills, putting papers in order) first. [pp.141-142]
- A dominant wing changes the picture strongly (a SEVEN with a dominant SIX wing is strikingly different from a SEVEN whose EIGHT wing is more active). Some people have identified with a wing so long that they must first be "redeemed" at that level before taking on the main energy. [p.142]

## 6. The Arrows: False and True Consolation [pp.142-148]

### 6.1 Concept
- The symbol: a circle with connecting lines (arrows) linking **9-6-3-9** (the inner triangle) and **1-4-2-8-5-7-1** (the irregular six-pointed star). [p.142]
- **The direction of the arrows marks the path of regression and disintegration.** Under stress, people seeking relief move **with** the arrow to another type and find only **"false consolation,"** a superficial unburdening that backfires. **Against** the arrows lies integration: in good times, after peak experiences, and on the way to spiritual maturity, people find **"true consolation"** in the positive qualities of the number reached by moving against the arrow, the **"consolation point."** [p.142] (The authors also use "stresspoint" for the false-consolation number.)
- Example: overburdened TWOs show immature-EIGHT features (authoritarian, aggressive, in the worst case violent); false consolation is the self-image "I am powerful." The same TWOs find consolation by going to FOUR: live out their creative and aesthetic side and discover "I am something special, even if once or twice I'm not there for others." [p.142]
- **[CHRISTIAN FRAMING]** The "discernment of spirits" of Paul and Ignatius means a spiritual instinct for which impulses create life and which destroy. According to Ignatius, false consolation shows in spiritual confusion, tumult, darkness, restlessness, disgust, doubt, selfishness; true consolation shows in inner warmth, gratitude, peace, power. [pp.143-144]

### 6.3 Each type's false and true consolation as described in the text
The authors presuppose Part II's personality descriptions. Biblical and historical illustrations are theirs; they are flagged [CHRISTIAN FRAMING / illustration] where they are interpretive.

**ONE** [p.144]
- *False consolation, ONE goes to FOUR.* Unredeemed ONEs want to be right at all costs and judge others: overzealous, impersonal, full of suppressed anger. At the end of their rope they turn aggression on themselves and take on the subjective, melancholy, depressed, self-destructive traits of an unredeemed FOUR (illustration: Luther's self-castigation in the monastery). The FOUR's arrogant self-image ("I'm something special") can make stressed ONEs begin to flatter themselves (illustration: Paul's "fool's boast" in 2 Cor. 11). Worst case: complete resignation, no longer willing to improve oneself or the world.
- *True consolation, ONE goes to SEVEN.* The overstrained, controlled ONE must learn to relax, be happy, "chill out." Redeemed ONEs still work hard but have ease and inner peace; they can accept being on the way and not yet at the goal; a calm *joie de vivre* (illustration: Luther after the tower experience, sociable, music, celebration; his remark about drinking Wittenberg beer while the kingdom grows by itself; Paul's joy in Philippians).

**TWO** [pp.144-145]
- *False, TWO goes to EIGHT.* Unredeemed TWOs make themselves irreplaceable and want to be loved; unacknowledged rage boils at the ingratitude of others (and God). When discouraged too long, rejected or used, they lash out like unredeemed EIGHTs ("I am powerful!"); darkness extinguishes the ability to love; hatred and bitterness can be boundless, mainly as passive-aggression, in exceptional cases violence (illustrations: Martha attacking Mary and Jesus; John wanting to destroy the Samaritan village).
- *True, TWO goes to FOUR.* Abandons the compulsion to make things right for everyone and the unrealistic self-image "I am Love personified!" FOURs face the darker side of their own soul instead of (like EIGHTs) seeking others' guilt. Insight into their own brokenness breaks the TWO's pride and shows they also live on grace. Experience of God's love leads to true self-acceptance ("I am something special!"): making friends with themselves, tolerating solitude, admitting needs, developing creativity for its own sake; able to be grateful and enjoy life; love no longer clinging; "consoled" TWOs take pleasure in small signs of giving, do not overtax themselves or others; their focus is the people needing help, not the thanks expected.

**THREE** [p.145]
- *False, THREE goes to NINE.* With so many roles, THREEs find it very hard to reach feelings. At NINE they are pulled into doubt, lose interest in themselves, can no longer hold the identity-giving image, especially without recognition; roles stop working but there is no motivation to look for the "true self"; life feels empty and senseless; all the hatred developed for competitors turns on themselves. Extremely regressive THREEs whose game is exposed risk suicide ("death as the final sleep"), or neglect, letting go, addiction. They need people who love them and help them find "honest" values and new visions.
- *True, THREE goes to SIX.* THREEs have problems with faithfulness, dependency and trust; faithfulness means going through thick and thin; THREEs tend to break off projects that look like bad bets; commitment means roles and masks will be seen through, so fear of having no self behind the masks shows up as fear of commitment, yet commitment is "the only possible way" to overcome role fixation. It helps if a partner or group lets them show their needy, underdeveloped side. Point SIX ("I am loyal") puts gifts at the service of something bigger than a career; integrating THREEs develop conscience and recognize limits (quoting Riso, p.145). Without integration and subordination, THREEs fall to their own ambition.

**FOUR** [pp.145-146]
- *False, FOUR goes to TWO.* Unreformed FOURs are depressed, confused, alienated, doubting themselves; at the end of their rope they try to reestablish contact with reality by giving up independence, becoming indispensable ("I'm helping!") or clinging; like caricatures of TWOs they seek identity through others' love and attention; incapable then of really engaging another, they want only sympathy; they begin to hate the people they depend on; every relationship breaks down sooner or later (illustration: Kierkegaard's engagement to Regine Olsen, his offensive behavior to make her break it off, and his being unable to bear that she moved on, described as "problems with distance and intimacy typical of the immature TWO").
- *True, FOUR goes to ONE.* ONE's values, clarity and conscience balance the FOUR's emotional chaos, self-doubt and subjectivism. The ONE idealization ("I am right!") helps the FOUR to healthy self-acceptance; for ONEs values outrank feelings, diligence outranks genius, reason outranks inspiration; sobriety lets FOURs get distance and critically evaluate their fantasy world. FOUR artists need the diligence and perfectionism of ONEs to turn inspiration into workable reality: "raw material" refined by the "form" of the ONE. Going to ONE means allowing objective criticism without being crushed by it; ONE's closeness to nature helps FOURs reach their own naturalness and originality (illustration: Thomas Merton submitting to strict monastic discipline and fighting with his abbot).

**FIVE** [p.146]
- *False, FIVE goes to SEVEN.* Disintegrating FIVEs take on nihilistic, schizoid and autistic traits; risk losing contact with reality in illusory mind games and dream worlds. To unburden themselves immature FIVEs turn egoistic, seek diversion and pleasure, retreat into the compartment that says "I'm okay"; lose themselves in pointless activism or silly quirks; seek "consolation" in sensual pleasures, betraying their own strengths (depth of reflection, contemplation, sober wisdom); stay in the head instead of noticing emotion and moving from thinking to doing. The image matching this condition: the decadent English dandy.
- *True, FIVE goes to EIGHT.* FIVE thinking tends to hinder action; at EIGHT they convert knowledge, however incomplete or provisional, into practice: "healthy audacity." Mature FIVEs trust that they need not always take but have something original and indispensable to give (illustrations: Hildegard of Bingen sharing visions and intervening in politics; Bonhoeffer becoming a conspirator, ready to "get his hands dirty"). The venture at EIGHT is to trust provisional knowledge and believe new experience will yield findings "that can't be had at one's desk"; head and gut connect; physical contact with reality.

**SIX** [pp.146-147]
- *False, SIX goes to THREE.* Unredeemed SIXes are authoritarian and pathologically mistrustful; with little self-esteem they depend on any "greater power." The authors offer fascism as the historical illustration of a SIX system disintegrating toward THREE and becoming success-oriented. At THREE SIXes do not gain competence and efficiency; they fall into THREE's hidden hostility: masochists become sadists, venting pent-up anger and compensating inferiority with a superiority complex; "frightened rabbits turn into frightening wolves." They ignore their inner warning signals, capable of lies, betrayal, unfaithfulness to save their own skin (illustration: Peter's denial of Jesus as "the THREE sin").
- *True, SIX goes to NINE.* NINE's calm and composure is "the best medicine for SIX's fear." Integrating SIXes "attain not only security but the ability to trust" (quoting Riso, p.147). Illustrations: Jesus asleep in the storm (Matt. 8); Peter walking on water and sinking when he looks at the waves; Oscar Romero as the prime example of a system functionary made free and fearless by trust in God.

**SEVEN** [p.147]
- *False, SEVEN goes to ONE.* The inhibitive mechanism of an unredeemed SEVEN stops working; fear of contact with pain and darkness intensifies (the thought of counseling or therapy brings panic), strengthening hectic activism or excessive pleasure. Going to ONE is an attempt to regain control of the emotional world: a "waterproof" ideological system aggressively defended; damning everything that blocks the positive view of the world; "peculiar mixture of cheerfulness and intolerance" (the authors cite parts of the charismatic movement); motto: "And if you don't want to be my brother, then I'll beat your head in!" In the worst case, "still smiling, and in the name of goodness," they do permanent harm to others and themselves.
- *True, SEVEN goes to FIVE.* SEVEN optimism "originates in their fear of depth," where pain is feared to lurk. The "voyage within" means giving up some false outer comforts. (Illustration: Francis of Assisi in solitude praying "Lord, who are you, and who am I?" in beautiful places; finding not an abyss but a source of deep joy.) At FIVE SEVENs stop suppressing pain and trust that their fundamental joyfulness and gratitude can bear confrontation with all of life; more composed and reasonable; "no longer consume the world, but rather learn to take responsibility for its preservation."

**EIGHT** [pp.147-148]
- *False, EIGHT goes to FIVE.* The energy of a neurotically regressive EIGHT is hostile and violent. At FIVE ("I am wise!") they brood, doubt, ponder; fears arise, above all fear of losing power. Few EIGHTs withdraw into silence willingly: it comes via sickness, physical weakness, or the involuntary solitude of a jail cell. Enforced silence can bring first-time profound powerlessness; fear that others will exploit the weakness and avenge past humiliations; guilt; the EIGHT's sense of justice suddenly sees their own crimes as demanding punishment; aggression turns inward. Unredeemed EIGHTs without power, sphere of action, targets or dependents are in extreme danger of suicide.
- *True, EIGHT goes to TWO.* TWOs defuse the power instinct and encourage the "soft" side that wants to help, nourish, protect; they want to heal as well as rule; leave self-imposed isolation; become more affable, placid, vulnerable. (Illustration: Martin Luther King Jr. as an integrated EIGHT putting leadership at the service of justice and love, a "revolution of love"; "To be weak, vulnerable, and tender is the greatest heroic deed that an EIGHT can achieve.")

**NINE** [p.148]
- *False, NINE goes to SIX.* Disintegrated NINEs can lose all contact with life. Their lifelong task was suppressing overly powerful impulses, positive and negative; now their only defense, retreat, collapses, and all fears of the world flood in; disorientation intolerable; SIX's self-punishment can be activated; with no real defense, inclination to narcotics, or death as "final sleep," can take over (illustration: Jonah wanting to die in each crisis). Another possibility: masochistic dependence on "authorities" to do their living and deciding for them and restore peace of mind.
- *True, NINE goes to THREE.* THREEs supply goal-directed, constructive action. NINEs conquer the "Jonah syndrome," stop selling themselves short, discover and employ talents; become autonomous and self-assured, no longer defined by others' expectations and impulses; take initiative and are surprised that others feel enriched; "Integrating NINEs can change the world." (Illustrations: Jonah stopping Nineveh's destruction once he took the prophet role; John XXIII "turned the Catholic Church upside down.") People trust integrating NINEs because they sense gifts used out of love, never for self. Ebert's anecdote: a friend, a properly integrated NINE and part-time pastor who needed "time for living," delegated to lay people, preached provocatively on "unchastity and idleness" (AIDS and the sin of the church) in such a serene tone that it prompted reflection not aggression, and took a sabbatical after fourteen years to avoid burnout. [p.148]

### 6.4 Warning about using the arrows (citing Helen Palmer) [pp.148-149]
Do not "pounce" on the consolation point and repress the stress point. Example: a TWO who identifies with the positive FOUR energy and tries to become a FOUR also takes on FOUR's negatives (melancholy, narcissism, hypersensitivity) and may feel entitled to be "something special," feeding their root sin, pride. Conversely, avoiding EIGHT lets its destructive energy work unhindered in the unconscious and break out violently some day; the clarity and determination of mature EIGHTs are exactly what the "soft" TWO needs (for example, learning to say no). The authors quote Palmer that working with the stress point is implicit in techniques like Gestalt therapy, where negative emotions are deliberately cultivated and engaged: "to skillfully raise our passions to the overflow point and to release the compulsion of a negative habit by experiencing it fully and completely" (cited p.149).

## 7. Growing with the Enneagram [pp.149-151]

- **Self-study:** the Enneagram is "first of all a key to self-knowledge." The point is not to nail others down or be nailed down but to ask who I am, what dangers and possibilities are within me, and how to find the "true self, which God put inside me" [CHRISTIAN FRAMING]. Only I can identify myself with a type; the pace is mine. Even those who do not find their type can read their life story in the mirror of the type descriptions; writing down key episodes of one's biography and reflecting on them alone or with a counselor helps. [p.149]
- **Partnership** (the authors note they are "two bachelors," so give only basic points). Couples report previously unspeakable conflicts appearing in a new light; one can view the partner's peculiarities more compassionately and one's own more critically. "Who matches whom?" cannot be carried over: no "super partners"; immature types struggle with anyone, mature persons can respect and love all types. [p.149] Patterns offered:
  - **"Birds of a feather"** (same type): rarely attractive to the same type. Three dangers: the one-sided energy is doubled and dominates; both play the same game so little challenge or complement; seeing one's own mechanisms in the partner can exasperate (shadow-projection). The opportunity: they understand each other "through and through" and cannot put anything over on each other.
  - **"Good neighbors"** (adjacent types): understand each other well; different enough not to bore, similar enough to get along; can help each other integrate wings.
  - **"Arrow types as partners":** Partner A is the stresspoint of Partner B while B is A's point of consolation. Classic example: EIGHT man and TWO woman; she suffers under his severity, unapproachability and irascibility, he finds warmth and protection; more subjective suffering for her than for him; both can grow (he by accepting his tender side through her; she by being challenged to face her own aggression and power demands, find a clear standpoint, and state her needs and limits). [pp.149-150]
  - **"Opposites attract":** never boring, but partners may feel as if on "two different planets"; they challenge each other to tolerance and opening to the "totally other, the not-I." In the Enneagram they meet only indirectly through wings or arrows. Two worked examples: [p.150]
    - **TWO and NINE:** threefold indirect link: (A) ONE is their common wing (political action, family reform, a "progressive" side); (B) EIGHT is the stresspoint of TWO and a wing of NINE, so NINEs can accept TWOs directly when TWOs go to stress and are not scared by EIGHT energy; (C) THREE is NINE's consolation point and a wing of TWO, so the TWO can motivate the NINE to purposeful work. TWOs live for others but long for unconditional acceptance, which NINEs can fully give; TWOs fulfill NINEs' wish to be stimulated and motivated.
    - **THREE and SEVEN:** meet indirectly at SIX (wing of SEVEN, consolation point of THREE): THREE's search for security and SEVEN's tendency to defend family meet there. Both are optimists, hard workers, "growth-oriented," and must watch that shared tendencies to superficiality and external concern are not reinforced; FIVE lies between them: SEVENs find consolation at FIVE (concentrate on essentials) and THREEs reach FIVE via the FOUR wing (inner balance "purifies" the world of feelings; distancing, slowing hyperactivity, self-confrontation). At FIVE, SEVEN and THREE can together "dare to look into the depths"; a shared worldview or faith stabilizes the partnership.
- **Authority and family:** dynamics between superiors and subordinates, parents and children, teachers and pupils can be understood with the Enneagram; family trees show types accumulating in families (the authors' examples: the Bachs musical, the Bonhoeffers intellectual, the Cranachs painters, the Fuggers businessmen); they mention Bert Hellinger's systemic family therapy: people often unconsciously adopt the pattern, role and destiny of an ancestor. [p.150]
- **Religious exercises and spiritual guidance:** used in retreats ("exercises") of several days with lectures, meditations, solitude, counseling, services; meditation and prayer are central "because the Enneagram cannot be mastered on a purely intellectual level, not even by a FIVE." It is "an experiential method" of "inner listening" to the voices within, and "discernment of spirits" is the instinctive spiritual authority telling which voices lead to "abundant life" (John 10:10) and which to "jail and death." Suzanne Zuercher is recommended for therapeutic/pastoral dynamics. [pp.150-151] [CHRISTIAN FRAMING]
- **Parish work:** trends and conflicts in a congregation or council often reflect different temperaments and visions of the church; the Enneagram can show which "energies" are submerged in a parish and where power and radiance are lacking. [p.151]
- **Discussion groups:** study groups can spend an evening on each type; Bible and prayer groups can use the nine faces of Jesus. [p.151]

## 8. Jesus and the Enneagram [pp.151-159] **[CHRISTIAN FRAMING throughout]**

### 8.1 Premises [pp.151-152]
- The Sufis supposedly called the Enneagram "the face of God," seeing the nine energies as nine attributes of God (nine refractions of divine light). Christians see in Jesus Christ "the face of God," revealer and revelation of divine love (Col. 1:15-20). The authors read the Enneagram as an icon of the face of Christ: "the face of God" and "the face of the (true) man." They also cite Heb. 4:15 (Jesus tempted in every respect). [p.151]
- Ebert reports a Lutheran bishop objecting that "we know too much about Jesus" to claim this (since Bultmann, what we know "would fit on a postcard"). Ebert replies: the Gospels, written decades after Easter, are testimonies of faith that preserve oral traditions with a historical kernel and were "inspired by the risen and 'exalted' Christ"; each story links to the earthly Jesus in order to communicate something timeless; his reading is therefore not a naive literalism. [pp.151-152]
- The Sufis reportedly saw Jesus as a "redeemed TWO" and Christianity as a TWO religion. The authors instead make "a statement of faith": Jesus realized "true personhood" in a way that "explodes the possibility of pinning him down to any one personality type"; mature representatives of the types become more alike because they have integrated opposites; Jesus is "the true man, in whom being and action have become one," who nevertheless entered a path of development. The Gospels indicate he understood all nine temptations and brought forth all nine fruits. Meditation on the cited passages helps recognize one's own temptations "in the mirror of his person." [p.152]
- **Figure 20, "The Christ-Enneagram"** places a face of Christ in the center of the nine-pointed figure with each point labeled by three qualities (the same as the headings below). [p.158]

### 8.2 The nine "faces" (qualities of Christ per type) and the argument for each

| Type | Qualities of Christ | Main Gospel material cited |
|---|---|---|
| ONE | Teaching, Tolerance, Patience | Matt. 5:17, 5:20, 5:48, 19:21; Mark 3:1-6, 9:19; John 8:10; Mark 4 parables of growth |
| TWO | Care, Compassion, Solidarity | Matt. 20:28; Luke 17:11-18 (ten lepers); John 12:1-8, 13:1-20; Gethsemane |
| THREE | Ambition, Energy, Vision | Luke 4:16-21; Matt. 4:1-11 (bread from stone), 5-7; Mark 8:33; Luke 19:41-42 |
| FOUR | Creativity, Sensitivity, Naturalness | Matt. 11:25-27, 26:38; John 11:34; Matt. 6:28-29, 10:29; John 9:6, 21:1-17; Matt. 18:4 |
| FIVE | Distance, Sobriety, Wisdom | Luke 2:40-52; Matt. 12:46-50, 7:24-29, 7:28-29; Luke 9:57-62, 14:25-30; John 16:13 |
| SIX | Fidelity, Obedience, Trust | Luke 4:16; Mark 2:27, 12:17; Phil. 2:6-8; Mark 5:36, 14:36; Matt. 20:25-27 |
| SEVEN | Festiveness, Joie de Vivre, Pain | Matt. 9:15, 11:19; John 2:1-11; Luke 6:24-26; Matt. 27:34, 27:46 |
| EIGHT | Confrontation, Clarity, Authority | Matt. 5:37, 5:39-41, 21-23, 26:53; Col. 2:15; John 18:23 |
| NINE | Composure, Peaceableness, Love | Matt. 11:28-30; Mark 4:35-41; Matt. 26:40-45; 2 Cor. 5:18-20; Eph. 2:15-17 |

**ONE: Teaching, Tolerance, Patience** [pp.152-153]. Jesus fulfills rather than abolishes the Torah, calls for righteousness exceeding the Pharisees and works "from a reconciled heart, not from compliance with duty." The much-discussed "Be perfect" (Matt. 5:48): "The path to Christian perfection always runs across the collapse of our own moral efforts and self-established ideals"; a key paradox for ONEs is that "we become perfect by accepting our own imperfection." He did not suppress anger (looking at accusers with "anger and grief"; impatience with the disciples), had no double moral standard, was a gifted teacher with a "spiritual school of life." Unlike the Pharisees he never condemns sinners; "unconditional acceptance with a clear challenge"; the adulteress: "I do not condemn you. Go now and do not sin again!" Growth parables invite perfectionists "not to be always in a state of panic": "Patience turns angry do-gooders into effective reformers."

**TWO: Care, Compassion, Solidarity** [p.153]. The name "Jesus" means "Yahweh helps/saves"; he considered himself a servant. He was disappointed by the one grateful leper (even Jesus expected thanks) and said so directly. He was no "helpless helper": his motive was compassion and friendship, not manipulating or "buying" love. He perceived and lived his own needs (withdrew to recover), could receive love (the anointing before he washed feet), asked disciples to watch with him at Gethsemane and shared his fears, and "could let go" (sent back rash volunteers, trusted disciples after three years). The invitation to the TWO is "the call to freedom": to commit and liberate others, to help and be helped, to be alone and with others; "a person who loves without losing freedom and without abusing the freedom of others."

**THREE: Ambition, Energy, Vision** [pp.153-154]. Jesus had a vision (the reign of God) and committed his life to it; accepted his role as Savior; his "maiden sermon" in Nazareth (Isaiah, "Today this scripture has been fulfilled") and the Sermon on the Mount as "Magna Carta." Greatest temptation, "the only temptation... of which the Gospels speak directly": bread from stone. He withstood "the temptation of the THREE": success without frustration, the miracle as cheap PR, "triumph without the cross, life without death" (calling Peter "Satan" for refusing the cross). He had leadership qualities (delegated, equipped the disciples, made no promises not fulfilled), amazing communication across scholars and simple country people, and was not indifferent to failure (wept over Jerusalem), but chose "a path that appeared to be a failure," because "his hope in his Father, who can create life from death, was greater than his fear of loss and failure"; this hope "can prevent THREEs from resting on their laurels and clinging to the 'security' of status and money."

**FOUR: Creativity, Sensitivity, Naturalness** [pp.154-155]. Rich emotional life ("sky high" to "very sorrowful, even to death"; weeping for Lazarus; admitted sadness without shame). Eye for beauty in nature (lilies, sparrows). A feeling for symbols and dramatic effect: parables from ordinary things; John's "signs"; the mud-and-spittle healing; the entry on an ass (Zech. 9:9); the withered fig tree like prophetic symbolic acts; bread and wine as concrete symbols; the post-resurrection breakfast at a charcoal fire echoing Peter's denial and the threefold "Do you love me?" ("an anticipation of... psychodrama"). Unaffected and natural; like all FOURs he did not divide the world into sacred and profane. Apparent elitism of the inner circle dissolves on inspection: mostly simple fishermen; a child as the greatest. Despite rejection by family, village, elites and disciples' incomprehension, "Jesus does not fall into melancholy self-pity."

**FIVE: Distance, Sobriety, Wisdom** [p.155]. Able to distance himself, withdraw, claim undisturbed space, and reject exaggerated demands for attention. At twelve, the precocious scholar in the temple ("Did you not know I must be in my Father's house?"). He turned away his family's claim ("Who is my mother?"), sent fanatical followers home and demanded they "count the cost," urged building on rock. Teaching carefully thought out and lived; taught "as one who had authority." Retreated into silence to order his thoughts and find his center, but the retreat served preparation and assessment of active service: he did not "remain an indifferent spectator." The incarnation: "The 'word' does not remain word... but becomes 'flesh' and world-changing act." "Christ is no esoteric thinker!" He shares his understanding (promise of the Spirit leading to all truth).

**SIX: Fidelity, Obedience, Trust** [pp.156-157]. "Inner authority" grounded in trust of his Father frees him from outer authorities and norms: he obeys laws and traditions as long as they are not "the real thing" (synagogue attendance), breaks any norm used to oppress (Sabbath healing; "The Sabbath was made for humankind"). Little use for worldly power (Herod a "fox"); "Render to Caesar" is read as "an enormous relativization of state authority" (the coin bears Caesar's image; the person bears God's). He was obedient strictly only to God and his calling (Phil. 2 hymn). He repeatedly invites people to overcome fear ("Do not fear, only believe"; "I have overcome the world") and overcame his own fear of death by trust (Gethsemane: "Abba, Father..."). No hierarchy among disciples (Matt. 20:25-27). The authors then criticize the official church for valuing "power, hierarchy, and norms," following "the scribes and Pharisees," ignoring Jesus's clear teaching on power and money, while inflating subjects on which he said nothing, "in particular, on a series of sexual issues."

**SEVEN: Festiveness, Joie de Vivre, Pain** [pp.156-157]. Jesus was "no child of sadness; he was also no ascetic"; his disciples did not fast (unlike John the Baptist's: "Can the wedding guests mourn...?"); accused of being a "glutton and a drunkard"; ate with Pharisees, a leper, tax collectors; the Cana wine (about 600 liters); the loaves (care for physical well-being). His message in one sentence: "God wants people to rejoice and find true happiness"; reign of God as wedding feast; Beatitudes ("Happy are those..."). But he warns against false, superficial joy (Luke's four "woes"): condemned is "boundless pleasure at the cost of the poor"; joy "goes through the eye of the needle of divestment and of brotherly-sisterly sharing." No Easter joy without the cross; cross is not "representative suffering" letting us bypass pain; quoting Kallistos Ware: "not substitution for our suffering but saving companionship." He refused the gall-and-wine painkiller, endured pain and God-forsakenness: "the gateway to Easter joy."

**EIGHT: Confrontation, Clarity, Authority** [pp.157-158]. Knew what he wanted, defended it without compromise, bore the consequences; "Yes" or "No"; simple folk followed him in greatest crowds; he turned tricksters' questions back on them; in prophetic tradition confronted opponents (entry into Jerusalem, whip in the temple, parables rebuking leaders, woes on scribes and Pharisees). "The picture of the 'sweet' and gentle Savior Jesus is in any case one-sided." On the Sermon on the Mount's non-resistance, citing Walter Wink: turning the cheek, the second mile, giving the cloak are "the most subversive form of nonviolent resistance" (compared to Saul Alinsky's tactics) that deprive the perpetrator of setting the rules, restore the victim's dignity, and let the perpetrator change without losing face. Jesus himself protested when struck (John 18:23), praised those who "hunger and thirst for justice," was rough with rulers and tender with the weak (children, the adulteress). "Strong but not invulnerable": refused to call legions of angels; chose "the power of powerlessness, in order to unmask the powerlessness of power" (Col. 2:15). The authors again criticize his church's succumbing to violence and militarism. EIGHTs like Martin Luther King Jr. followed him "to the final consequence."

**NINE: Composure, Peaceableness, Love** [pp.158-159]. Despite untiring creative power, he radiated calm and peace ("Come to me, all who labor..."); slept in the storm, an expression of deep inner peace and trust in God. Sleep in the Hebrew Bible is a gift (Psalms), but also a symbol of dullness and escape from decision (Gethsemane disciples; Eph. 5:14 "Awake, O sleeper"). For Jesus peace "was anything but idleness. Love activated him." His "passivity" is better called **"permeability"**: he passed on what he received from the Father. His love did not judge or exclude; his life work was reconciliation with God and with each other (2 Cor. 5, Eph. 2). NINEs love community and need it for motivation; Christ lived community and expected disciples to be active; "he even noticed the NINEs, who are otherwise so easily overlooked."

## 9. The Enneagram and Prayer [p.159]

Three basic prayer/meditation modes in all world religions: (1) **from the outside in** (a picture, symbol or text acts on me); (2) **from the inside out** (let inner moods and images arise and express them in words or painting); (3) **emptiness**, the way of the Void (letting go of outer and inner impulses to reach complete stillness; the "object-less" way of Zen). These correlate to the three centers: **heart types want to express themselves; gut types seek emptiness; head types seek input from outside.** The natural approach is set by the dominant center, so it makes sense to begin with what comes easy, which can become a pitfall: heart types who only express must, on the way to integration, enter forms that help them recollect and reflect on themselves, and so on. Gerry Pierse's book on the Enneagram and Christian mantra meditation is recommended for the nine accesses to spiritual life and the nine typical blockages to prayer. [CHRISTIAN FRAMING / practice]

## 10. The End of Determinism [pp.159-161] **[mostly CHRISTIAN FRAMING]**

- The Enneagram "tells it 'like it is'"; healing begins with "acknowledging what's there" (Bert Hellinger); it is a "mirror for the confessional" showing blocks and abysses that enslave. [p.159]
- Comparison of redemption concepts: Hinduism/Buddhism (karma worked out through reincarnations until a condition of no desires, nirvana); Marxism (alienation overcome in history to a classless society); Christianity: the unredeemed condition is "the dominion of the law," described by Paul (Rom. 7:15-23, "I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate"). [pp.159-160]
- In Jesus God "ventured 'under the law'" and was open to its compulsions, to redeem those under it (Gal. 4:4). Faith in Christ leads out of "the compulsions of the inexorable Law"; one can look honestly at pitfalls and defense mechanisms and loosen the grip: "The war is over! You can turn in your weapons that once insured your survival. God is not your enemy, but your friend." One no longer needs to understand oneself as slave of the law, product of parents, victim of environment, or "determined cog in the machinery of the world": "all of that is true in a sense... but it's not the final truth." [p.160]
- **Nicodemus (John 3):** the Pharisee seeking Jesus by night; "new birth" or "birth from above" is the Spirit's work of total transformation; baptism is the outer sign that the "old Adam" is dying. Like the wind, the life of the new person cannot be predicted; under the law "we ourselves as well as others know, rather quickly, 'which way the wind is blowing with us.' We live like machines (Gurdjieff)." Those filled with the Spirit "are ready for surprises" and capable of things "not 'sung to them when they were in the cradle'." [p.160]
- Continuity: the new person remains the child of the forces that shaped them; renewal is lifelong with stagnation and regression; Luther's *simul justus et peccator*; but "the old laws no longer rule my identity." [p.160]
- **Verdict on the system:** "The Enneagram too is part of the realm that the Bible calls 'law.' It conceals what is. It is not freedom, but precisely by spelling out our un-freedom it can become the signpost to freedom." A signpost can be forgotten once the goal is in view: the city of God, a feast, the reign of freedom, "life to the full." [p.161]

## 11. "An Enneagram Sermon on Christmas" (Dietrich Koller, adapted by Andreas Ebert) [pp.162-163]

A narrative piece, not a typology chapter, in which **the nine great sins** hear that the Redeemer is born, fear being "put out of business," and are drawn to the manger. It uses these names for the sins and gives each its voice and then the child's gaze and exchange. (The sin names differ slightly from the root-sin list in 1.3; see note.)

## 12. "The Repentance No One Regrets: Perspectives on Spiritual Work" (Dietrich Koller) [pp.164-169] **[CHRISTIAN FRAMING with practical method]**

Opening: the book is "just a book"; it can give typological "know-how or know-who" and tempt readers to "stick a label or number on people's foreheads," or it can be a map for "a path of return to oneself." Transformation is "at once a task and a grace": without work, grace cannot bear fruit; without grace, the work is in vain; repentance is the process in which "my work and God's work become simultaneous" (Acts 5:31, 2 Cor. 7:10). Five suggestions follow.

1. **The typology serves transformation, not self-mirroring** [pp.164-165]. The Enneagram is a mirror, but one should not narcissistically cultivate one's pattern; the aim is redemption from compulsions, integration of "the nine energy sites," and coordination of heart, head and gut ("head-brain," "feeling-brain," "body-brain"). Do not specialize and become "still more typical" (a pitfall of false self-love); broaden and dissolve the type in "faith, hope, and love." Observation: "the profile of an undeveloped person is at first much sharper, more offensive, and more caricaturable than the profile of a fully developed person"; neurotics "cast sharper and longer shadows than transformed individuals." A transformed EIGHT in selfless service can look like a helping TWO on the surface but be "worlds apart" in motivation. The Enneagram is "not nine isolated energy-sites, but one path with nine different beginnings"; from each point we move "as if in dance steps around the circle through nine different worlds, all of which form a single world of perfection." The goal is divine perfection (the sun and rain on evil and good, Matt. 5:45-48): "godlike perfection... the exact opposite of a moralistic perfectionism."
2. **Self-encounter happens in encounter with God, not egoism** [pp.165-166]. Quotes Martin Buber (the I-Thou / I-It principle; "the God of the moment"). "Know thyself" is "thoroughly ironic": you cannot pull yourself from a morass by your own hair; authentic work meets "the not-I, the Totally Other." "My God" must not be a projection of the ego, or self-knowledge produces pride or despair; the encounter with the incarnate God "brings me down from the delusion of vanity and out of the hole of depression" into "cheerful high-spirited humility." Dialogue releases transformation "far more quickly and profoundly than the monologue of egoism." "Fear of God" is read as "attentiveness," not fear; waking up in the present.
3. **The instrument is content-free perception, not moral self-reflection** [pp.166-167]. Everyone has an "**inner observer**" which, when awake, names the various ego-portions haunted by thoughts and feelings. It does not judge: "At the moment when I begin to give 'grades'... the observer withdraws." It is "neither conscience nor my parents' ego, nor the condemning Pharisee, nor the analyzing judge." By neutral perception it creates "a broad fairway between both automatic reactions: on the one hand repression, on the other self-identifications," into which "unconditional love can pour." A poem (freely after Erich Fried) gives each type's reaction to "what is" versus the divine "It is the way it is":
   - TWO: "It's ingratitude" (injured pride); THREE: "It's a disaster" (calculation); FOUR: "It's a tragedy" (melancholy); FIVE: "It's nonsense" (rationality); SIX: "It's thoughtless" (prudence); SEVEN: "It's not so bad" (comforting skill); EIGHT: "It's pitiful" (high-handedness); NINE: "It's hopeless" (laziness); ONE: "It's a mistake" (morality). Each triad ends: "It is the way it is, says the love of the divine Heart / the Spirit / Love incarnate" (heart, head, gut groupings: 2-3-4, 5-6-7, 8-9-1).
   - "To say what is is the first revolutionary act in a social situation" (Rosa Luxemburg after Lassalle); acceptance of what is "is anything but a false submission to fate." Everything accepted can be redeemed. The first Beatitude ("poor in spirit") and Meister Eckhart's image of the eye receptive to all colors are connected. The inner observer is "the first instrument for transformational work." The work consists in interrupting "eternally selfish programmed reactions" (a young Bavarian pastor's war-diary prayer: "The gift of emptying myself"; Kästner's mouse-in-the-trap epigram). Warnings: entering yourself is dangerous if you are not also going to the God who accepts you; fleeing piously to God to dodge oneself is equally unfruitful: "A soulless theology and a godless psychology are no help."
4. **One does not strive for ideals but discerns spirits** [pp.167-168]. The new mode of being (corresponding to the "holy idea") cannot be "brought off" because it is grace; it grows "automatically" (Mark 4:28 *automatē*). What we can do is remove the thorns and thistles by "continually interrupting the automatism of our false pattern." The idealist strives for sublime ideals and comes to grief; transformational work "perseveres in sober alertness, before which the spirits unmask themselves." Quoting Nicephorus the Hermit on attentiveness as "the sign of a perfect repentance." The Desert Fathers knew the "pattern" or Enneagram number as "the compulsive automatisms of the soul": "demons" with "squatter's rights" over our nerve endings; the false "pattern-spirits" "can only exaggerate or distort our holy gifts." The discernment charism diagnoses pairs: genuine versus false pride; authentic self-love versus narcissism; spiritual knowledge of guilt versus neurotic guilt feelings; realistic versus depressive resignation; prophetic hope versus enraptured illusionism; perfect joy versus manufactured pleasure; subservience versus grown-up obedience; mature love versus symbiotic blind adoration; creative repentance versus fruitless self-pity; real tolerance of suffering versus cowardly conformism; "the sadness of the world" versus "divine sadness." [p.167] **Attentiveness "keeps watch at the gates"** at three levels: the sensuous body (what and how am I seeing, hearing, eating?), the psyche (what feelings are trying to overwhelm me?), and the spirit (which judgments, prejudices, comparisons, intentions, memories, claims and ideals are trying to dominate me?). [p.168] The aim is to become "the image of the ninefold Christ in me"; the "archetype of the warrior plays an irreplaceable role" in this "spiritual war," and the author speculates that such a culture would end "the senseless wars on the external stage." [p.168] Augustine's *Confessions* (the "conversion of a FOUR"; the Milan garden passage with "toys and trifles" tugging at his garment) is quoted at length as an example of perception and discernment. Igumen Nikon (Russian starets under Khrushchev) is cited: "If we repent and continue fighting, we can even draw profit from the plunge"; pray for the gift "to see one's own sins and to weep over them"; "Those who reject a justified reproach are rejecting their redemption"; "the measure of one's spiritual growth is our humility." Thomas a Kempis: "External consolation is no small hindrance and damage to inner divine consolation," so discernment also operates at the level of consolation. [p.168]
5. **We enter the wide world of all-encompassing love** [p.169]. A developmental narrative of "worlds": the world of self-deception and childish egocentrism; then healthy egoism (functioning in society); then the "narrow gate of repentance" into attentiveness and awareness; spiritual war against false spirits; "the world of truth." As the new self forms after the model of the God-man, "the ninefold gift of the Spirit develops in its ever unique individual expression": one discovers one's true calling (the "holy idea," "created in my genetic program"), then the nine fruits in unique concrete form, with charisms "no longer driven by the ego, but by selfless love." This ends in *agape* (Paulo Coelho's "love that swallows"): "Judgment is over. The powerless Lamb... is enthroned in the center of omnipotence." One can still fall within a second into earlier worlds ("the psychic 'elevator' goes up just as quickly as it goes down"), but one is stuck less often and for shorter. Repentance never stops until death; purgatory is read as "the flames of creative repentance," the "anteroom of paradise"; heaven "the infinite unfolding of integration by the love that swallows up all things"; hell "already present as refusal and blockage of the development," that is, "all growth that has bogged down in defiance, laziness, self-pity, or jealousy, all fixed neuroses of the ego that has idealized itself." Closes: "The sadness of the world brings death. But sadness in accordance with God's will brings the blissfulness through the repentance that no one regrets."

## 16. What this range says that is theological (collected) **[CHRISTIAN FRAMING]**

- Re-interpretation of the nine passions as **root sins** and of the nine virtues or holy ideas as **fruits of the spirit** and **invitations**; **grace** is the source of change, not will.
- **Metanoia** ("change your mind") as the template for type change; "repentance that no one regrets" (2 Cor. 7:10).
- The inner triangle 9-3-6 equated to **faith, hope, love** (1 Cor. 13:13); the final summary table pairs the nine types with the clauses of the **Lord's Prayer**.
- **Jesus as the integrated, "ninefold" person** who meets all nine temptations and embodies all nine fruits; the face of Christ in the center of the figure.
- **Discernment of spirits** (Paul, Ignatius, Desert Fathers); false versus true **consolation**; **spiritual warfare**; the "pattern" read as "demons" with squatter's rights; attentiveness at the "gates."
- The Enneagram as part of "**the law**" (Pauline sense), a signpost to freedom, not a determinism; **new birth** (Nicodemus); *simul justus et peccator*.
- Eschatology: purgatory as creative repentance, heaven as unfolding integration, hell as present refusal of growth; **agape** as final stage.
- Church criticism: the institutional church's emphasis on power, hierarchy and sexual norms is contrasted with Jesus's silence on those norms and clear teaching on power and money (SIX and EIGHT sections).
- Typology as secondary to dialogue with God ("Know thyself" is ironic without the Totally Other; Buber's I-Thou).
- Non-Christian traditions are compared and then subordinated: Sufis ("the face of God," Jesus as "redeemed TWO"), Hinduism and Buddhism (karma), Marxism (alienation), Zen (the Void), Gurdjieff ("we live like machines"), Hellinger (family systems).
