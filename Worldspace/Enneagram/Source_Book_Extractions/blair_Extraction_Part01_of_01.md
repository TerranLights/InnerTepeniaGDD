## Overview

This is a popular-level, relationship-focused guide that assumes the reader already knows the basic Enneagram. Part I (Preface and nine chapters) gives, for each type: the integration and disintegration direction, three-band health levels, communication style, intimacy needs, common mistypes, the two wings, and what it means to "accept" that type in a partner. Part II takes each type in turn and walks through its pairing with all nine types (including itself), describing what each partner can give the other, where friction arises (especially under stress), and what to work on. The author states that the book is not a dating guide, not a method for picking a "best" type, and that its advice about triggers is to be used to avoid hurting a partner, never to manipulate one [s.4]. The recurring thesis is that communication is the foundation of any relationship and that knowing each partner's motivations, triggers and stress directions lets a couple catch a slide toward the unhealthy range early [s.4, s.30]. The author identifies himself as a Two married to a Seven and uses that pairing as an example [s.4].

## Introduction [s.4]

- Most people start with the Enneagram by working on themselves; the book turns to how types relate. Certain personalities complement each other; others more easily clash. Communication is "the bedrock of any relationship" [s.4].
- Some types need very clear, straightforward communication; others are comfortable with ambiguity. The book covers what each type needs in communication, how to ask for what they want, and how to affirm their own needs [s.4].
- Structure promised: Preface (brief review: how the Enneagram works, levels of development, integration/disintegration, short type recaps, wings, finding your type); Part I (each type in depth: levels of development, preferred communication, degrees of intimacy, common mistypes, wing nuances, how to be accepting and affirming); Part II (all possible pairings) [s.4].
- Author's stated limits: not an introduction; not a dating guide; not advice on finding a perfect match by type; triggers are listed to be avoided, not exploited [s.4].
- Author's own example (Two with a Seven): the Two's shame-based core can leave him self-conscious and inhibited, which his outgoing, adventure-seeking Seven wife supports; he in turn helps her feel safe expressing a full range of emotions, which Sevens find uncomfortable. "Each number...needs to talk through what they need from their partner, why they need it, and how they can work towards fulfilling each other's personal needs" (paraphrase of s.4).

## Preface

### What Is the Enneagram? How Does It Work? [s.6]

- **Definition:** a system explaining human personality via a nine-pointed diagram; "enneagram" from Greek *ennea* (nine) + *gramma* (written/drawn) [s.6].
- **Centers (triads), each sharing a core motivator:**

| Center (alternate names) | Types | Core motivator | How it plays out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instinctive (Body, Gut) | Eight, Nine, One | Anger | Eight lashes out; Nine ignores it entirely; One turns it inward on self |
| Feeling (Heart) | Two, Three, Four | Shame | Two proves love by meeting others' immediate needs; Three proves success; Four proves uniqueness |
| Thinking (Head; also "mental triad") | Five, Six, Seven | Fear | Five: fear of being inadequate or incapable; Six: fear of instability/being unsafe; Seven: fear of missing out and of pain |

- **Stances** (how a type interacts with others): the author uses "dependent," "aggressive," "withdrawing" [s.6].

| Stance | Types | Direction | Other characterizations given |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dependent | One, Two, Six | Move toward people; seek guidance and validation outside self | Most focused on the present; "thinking repressed" |
| Aggressive | Three, Seven, Eight | Move against people; others may see them as hostile, which is usually not their intent; driven toward a self-set goal; thrive on conflict | Look to the future; "feeling repressed" |
| Withdrawing | Four, Five, Nine | Move away from people; more private | Center on the past; "doing repressed" |

- **Wings:** the type to the left and right of a number. One or both influence personality. Example given: a Seven has a Six wing (to its right) or an Eight wing (to its left); 7w8 is "more forceful, confident, and aggressive," 7w6 "more cautious, suspicious, or skeptical." Wings vary in strength; a person may lean on one or both wing at different times as needed (a Four may need the Three wing's drive at one time, the Five wing's introspection at another) [s.6].
- **Instinctual variants** (also "instincts," "subtypes" though the author says that is not strictly accurate): Self-Preservation, Social, Sexual (also "One-to-One"). 27 combinations of type and variant. Everyone has all three; one dominant, one secondary, one least developed. "Stacking" = writing them in order of dominance (e.g., Eight so/sx; Five sp/so; the least developed can be omitted) [s.6].
  - **Self-Preservation:** immediate personal needs, safety, comfort, adequate resources (energy, money, daily needs); often drawn to aesthetics (fashion, makeup, hair, home decor, beautiful things); the most introverted of the three [s.6].
  - **Sexual (One-to-One):** the most extroverted; drawn to relationships of all kinds, not only romantic; interested in depth and energy shared; highly aware of chemistry; tends to want to merge with others; looks for intensity and strong energy [s.6].
  - **Social:** not necessarily a "social butterfly" (even withdrawing types can be Social-dominant); aware of the people around them; highly adaptable, can be chameleon-like depending on the setting (large work event versus small dinner party); desire personal connection and meaningful shared experiences [s.6].
- **Tritype (author flags it is "not officially part of the Enneagram of Personality"; the term is trademarked as Tritype®):** a dominant type in each center. Example: 5w4 with a stronger Two in the Feeling Center and a stronger Eight in the Instinctive Center is written 528 (or 582 if the Eight outranks the Two). The extra types influence but do not change the basic type; as with wings, the person is not those types [s.6].

### Levels of Development [s.7]

- Nine levels of health per type, originated by Don Richard Riso and Russ Hudson (1999); most people operate in the average range [s.7].

| Band | Levels |
|---|---|
| Healthy | 1, 2, 3 |
| Average | 4, 5, 6 |
| Unhealthy | 7, 8, 9 |

- Tracking development starts with knowing the basic type; then follow the type's path of integration (to health) and disintegration (to stress). Moving toward another number means taking on that number's traits (negative traits in stress, positive in health), not becoming that type [s.7].

| Type | Disintegrates (stress) toward | Integrates (health) toward |
|---|---|---|
| One | Four | Seven |
| Two | Eight | Four |
| Three | Nine | Six |
| Four | Two | One |
| Five | Seven | Eight |
| Six | Three | Nine |
| Seven | One | Five |
| Eight | Five | Two |
| Nine | Six | Three |

### The Nine Personality Types (preface recaps) [s.8]

Opening claim: communication is key; types differ (structured versus emotion/unspoken; direct versus indirect); misreadings are common (directness read as annoyance; quietness read as resentment or ambivalence). Learning types helps build "trust, peace, harmony, sympathy, and empathy." "Without proper and compassionate communication, a relationship will eventually fall apart" [s.8]. Recaps (wing nicknames given in Part I tables below):

- **One (Reformer/Perfectionist):** dependent stance; Instinctive center; sees anger as unacceptable, turns it inward; black-and-white ethics; orderly. Unhealthy: rigid, dogmatic, critical (esp. of self), may turn critique on partner. Should pursue peace rather than perfection; basic fear is of being corrupt/imperfect/lacking; basic desire is a personal sense of honor and balance [s.8].
- **Two (Helper/Giver):** dependent; Feeling; empathetic, warm, outgoing; serves others partly to counteract their own unworthiness. Unhealthy Twos feel they must be loved and needed; self-sacrifice slides into martyrdom. Often cannot say what they need, ignore own needs (health can suffer), and risk resentment. Basic fear: not worthy of love, being unwanted and disposable; must learn to trust they will not be abandoned [s.8].
- **Three (Achiever/Performer):** aggressive; Feeling; one of the more extroverted types; concerned with image, success, status. Twos know their shame and try to disprove it; Threes "ignore and suppress" it and pursue success at any cost. Fear of being worthless; wants to be irrecoverably valuable ("irreplaceable"); equates success with worth and so dissociates from emotion; work bleeds into personal life, partner feels second place. Growth: find value as a person, become comfortable with emotions, see work as one part of their talents [s.8].
- **Four (Individualist/Tragic Romantic):** withdrawing; Feeling; said to be the rarest and most complex type; reserved; proves how unique they are; pursue creativity and honesty, often artists. Unhealthy: self-conscious, self-critical, melancholic or depressed; fear of lacking identity or authenticity; believe something is wrong with them; envy others and overlook relationships they already have. Accepting self and "messy emotions" makes accepting another's love easier [s.8].
- **Five (Investigator/Observer):** withdrawing; Thinking; introverted; accumulates knowledge; independent, thinks outside the box. Unhealthy: retreat too far into the mind, overwhelmed by their own thoughts and fears; eccentric or nihilistic. Fear of being useless or helpless; knowledge is meant to counter fear of being an incompetent community member. Needs large amounts of alone time; must balance self-care with social interaction (neither they nor partner may know this) [s.8].
- **Six (Loyalist/Trooper):** "perhaps the most common of all the types...at least in North America"; dependent; Thinking; values groups, structure, teamwork; "afraid of fear itself"; rehearses worst cases; most reliable and loyal. Unhealthy: stressed, anxious, paranoid; indecisive, little faith in own abilities. Dislikes surprises; worries partners won't be there. Must let go of fear of change ("change is inevitable, negativity is not") and learn self-trust [s.8].
- **Seven (Enthusiast/Epicure):** aggressive; Thinking; fear of pain, somber emotions or unpleasant experiences; shuns whatever does not measure up; optimistic, adaptable, spontaneous, adventure-seeking. Unhealthy: dangerously impulsive, ignores unpleasant or boring tasks; runs from what they don't want to feel and toward what they don't want to miss; flighty, future-living, partners may feel "not good enough." Should learn to be present and value shared experiences [s.8].
- **Eight (Challenger/Boss):** aggressive (most obviously "against" people); Instinctive; comfortable with and expresses anger; most assertive/aggressive type; aggression is not meant personally. Unhealthy: destructive, lash out, bully, physical aggression. Dislikes vulnerability, so turns the tables and makes others vulnerable. Basic fear: being controlled by others; extremely independent and action-oriented; may not weigh partner's opinions. Growth: voice thoughts and motives, include others; shared vulnerability helps relationships grow [s.8].
- **Nine (Peacemaker/Mediator):** withdrawing; Instinctive; introverted homebodies; denies anger entirely; the most relaxed, easygoing, and "least complex"; avoids conflict at almost any cost, "fall asleep to their own needs." Unhealthy: complacent, delays ending a destructive relationship or job to avoid rocking the boat. Core fear: losing connection with others. Sees all sides, so rarely states own opinion. True peace requires also being happy with what they have fought for [s.8].

### How to Find Your Type [s.9]

- Mistyping is common. Start with several online tests; track which types recur and which score low (eliminate those); narrow to three or four. Stance scores can guide (withdrawing types Four/Five/Nine; likewise aggressive or dependent).
- Read the descriptions of the remaining two to four types, focusing on inner motivation, not outward behavior. Example: Eight versus Three, both aggressive stances; Eight driven by anger, Three by shame, "your biggest clue."
- Types can look alike when sharing a stance or when integrating or disintegrating. This is also why guessing another's type is "difficult, if not impossible": no one else knows what drives you internally [s.9].

## Part I: The Enneagram Types [s.10]

Each chapter (s.11-19) uses the same sub-headings: Levels of Health and Development; Communication; Intimacy; Common Mistypes; Wing Personalities; Acceptance. Healthy/Average/Unhealthy bands are tabulated per type. A one-line note on method: the author repeats that integrating or disintegrating "does not mean" the person becomes the other type.

### ONE: The Reformer [s.11]

**Names:** Reformer, Perfectionist; 1w9 = The Idealist; 1w2 = The Advocate [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Seven: playful, spontaneous, better at unexpected change, flexible about shifts in relationship dynamics. Lower-average to unhealthy: rigid, unwilling to see other viewpoints, "only their way is the correct method"; annoyed and resentful at partial work or lack of follow-through; constant criticism and nitpicking can drive people away [s.11].

| Band | Description [s.11] |
|---|---|
| Healthy (1-3) | Keep strong moral principles and ethics while accepting what is realistically possible; recognize the world is not only black and white |
| Average (4-6) | Dissatisfied with how things are; turn to advocacy for idealistic visions; descending, become unbending and angry at failures to meet high expectations |
| Unhealthy (7-9) | Inflexible, intolerant; obsessed with own perfection and others' mistakes; judgmental, hypocritical |

**Communication.** Direct; want to know what to expect from self and others and want the parameters of a relationship stated up front. Diligent, organized; "good enough for everyone else is not good enough." A constant inner voice says it isn't their best. Criticism is meant to improve, not wound; but others may not want endless improvement. Advice: temper criticism with compliments; not everyone handles critique with the One's positivity [s.11].

**Intimacy.** Cannot measure up in their own eyes, so vulnerability is hard; the inner critic makes them "rigid and fragile." Any negative response, even implied, puts them on edge and raises anger. Partner should pair negatives with positives (state what went well first). When a One feels safe and that their effort has had good effect, imperfection becomes acceptable [s.11].

**Common mistypes.** One vs Three: both organized, success-driven; Threes want tangible success (achievements, wealth, possessions) and will cut corners; Ones want success on their own terms, won't take shortcuts, each step matters. Ones may mistype as a Nine or Two via wings, as a Four when very unhealthy (Four is the stress direction), or as a Seven when very healthy [s.11].

**Wings.** 1w9 (Idealist): big-picture over detail; sees all sides, builds bridges; more relaxed and able to let things go; a weaker Nine wing means more preoccupation with doing things correctly. 1w2 (Advocate): leans into the Heart center, so more access to emotions, represses feelings (anger) less, more empathy and commitment to help people in need [s.11].

**Acceptance (what a partner should do).** Accept the desire to improve things; help temper perfectionism with self-acceptance; emphasize that "doing your personal best is not only good enough, but it is perfect enough"; encourage rest at regular intervals [s.11].

### TWO: The Helper [s.12]

**Names:** Helper, Giver; 2w1 = The Servant; 2w3 = The Host/The Hostess [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Four: comfortable with darker, melancholic feelings and the full emotional range; attend to own needs; centered; often find a creative outlet. Disintegrating toward Eight: when denied expected affection or attention, they become possessive, demanding, controlling, domineering [s.12].

| Band | Description [s.12] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Empathetic, compassionate, unconditional love; aware of own needs; service from love and joy, not for return |
| Average | People-pleasing; ulterior motives behind care; flattery to get affirmation; lower, intrude inappropriately and force possessive affection |
| Unhealthy | Guilt-tripping and manipulation to get sympathy and acknowledgment; at worst a victim complex with bitterness and resentment |

**Communication.** Warm, affectionate; give compliments (notice hair, makeup, a color that suits you) and want them back. Offer positive feedback or gentle constructive criticism to push others toward their best. Excellent listeners; seek out people who look uncomfortable ("if even one person is not at ease, the Two won't be either"). Heavy use of second-person pronouns ("How are you doing?" "What are you up to?"); genuinely interested, not superficial [s.12].

**Intimacy.** More concerned with others' needs than their own at all levels of health (reasons differ) and often do not know what they need. Because in the Feeling center, they do favors expecting reciprocity, but often do not say so; others (Thinking or Instinctive types) may not share this expectation. Advice: partners should ask a Two about motives and intentions and help them put needs into words [s.12].

**Common mistypes.** Two vs Six: both social; Sixes want acceptance and approval and are drawn to an authority figure, Twos want acceptance and love and don't need such authority. Two vs Seven: both love people; Twos want a deep, intimate relationship; Sevens do not mind who they are with so long as they are not alone, and enter others' lives only when invited, while Twos invite themselves. Wing confusion with One or Three. Stress or unhealthy Twos may mistype as Eight; integrating Twos as Four. The book notes women often mistype as Twos because the others-oriented, serving traits are "valued and reinforced to a much higher degree in women than men"; a woman scoring high on Two should check her next-highest scores [s.12].

**Wings.** 2w1 (Servant): more concerned with being correct or just; altruistic, social justice, community improvement, "a better vision of the world," warm and generous. 2w3 (Host/Hostess): more social and outgoing; per the author, a Three wing keeps both basic type and wing in the Feeling center, giving more access to feelings and less reserve about expressing them; enjoys planning and organizing social events and meeting people at them [s.12]. [note: in the source the Feeling-center sentence sits in the middle of the 2w1 vs 2w3 comparison, ahead of the 2w1 description; it is attributed here to the Three-wing Two because that is the only wing in the Feeling center.]

**Acceptance.** Others-oriented; astutely aware of partner's needs, less aware of their own; they use small and large favors to communicate wants, probably unconsciously. Highly dependent on others, especially romantic partners; need affirmation not as ego-boosting but to feel loved; without it their sense of self suffers. Generous even when unhealthy, so easy to exploit. Partners should reinforce that the Two is loved for themselves, "not merely the kind deeds they do" [s.12].

### THREE: The Achiever [s.13]

**Names:** Achiever, Performer; 3w2 = The Charmer; 3w4 = The Professional [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Six: focus on how they relate to people rather than on goals; less competitive, work for others' betterment; see others as non-competition; cooperative and able to show real commitment. Disintegrating toward Nine: apathy, lethargy, disengagement, busywork done only to finish something; lose optimism and confidence [s.13].

| Band | Description [s.13] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Self-assured, energetic, self-improving for its own sake; highly competent; accept themselves as they are |
| Average | Consumed with performing; manicured image; impress others; rely on others' expectations rather than their own |
| Unhealthy | Fear of failure; any length to preserve image of success; treat others as competition |

**Communication.** "Chameleons"; adapt to any person or situation and put on whichever mask brings ease; communicate what they think will be acceptable rather than their own needs; may be unaware of own feelings (suppressed). Emotional displays are less apparent than for Two or Four. Prefer positive emotions; use many exclamation points, happy emojis, cheerful words and excited sign-offs. Future-oriented: when they err, they focus on improvements, not dwelling on the past [s.13].

**Intimacy.** Drive for success can shut down emotions and deeper connection; inner world sacrificed for broadly appealing superficiality; the image of success being built takes precedence over actual relationships. Western society rewards material success and overwork, reinforcing the Three's worst tendencies (late nights praised in corporate life, but cause burnout and frayed relationships; the partner feels undervalued). Needs: partners to notice how hard they work, and pull them back when pushing too hard; guidance that "what they accomplish...is not the sum of their entire worth" [s.13].

**Common mistypes.** Wing confusion (3w2 vs 2w3; 3w4 vs 4w3). Rule of thumb for 3w2 vs 2w3: comfort with pursuing goals (a 2w3 may feel ambition is selfish) and access to feelings (Twos have more). For 3w4 vs 4w3: Fours have much freer access to feelings; Threes suppress them to stay on task. Three vs Seven vs Eight: all three assertive, future-focused, goal-oriented. Threes pursue success; Sevens pursue the experience itself ("live for the adventure instead of the end result"); Eights want to dominate and wield power but are generally uninterested in status or others' views; Threes "are the true status seekers" [s.13].

**Wings.** 3w2 (Charmer): sociable, drawn to people, a more extroverted Two; flashier, more image-focused; success partly for how it looks to others. 3w4 (Professional): better understanding of emotions, more likely to have a creative outlet, more introverted, tempers flashiness, shows caution, may spend more time alone [s.13].

**Acceptance.** Can fully dissociate from feelings when focused on a goal; may seem distracted in conversation; loved ones feel ignored. Future-oriented, so uninterested in rehashing what went wrong; respond to suggestions for improvement, strategies and goals to strengthen the relationship. Don't mind critique if mixed with praise; "everything Threes do needs to be affirmed," but they also need to know they are loved for who they are, not only for what they achieve [s.13].

### FOUR: The Individualist [s.14]

**Names:** Individualist, Tragic Romantic; 4w3 = The Aristocrat; 4w5 = The Bohemian [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward One: grounded, focused, present-moment (Fours are past-focused); set emotional boundaries thanks to the One's black-and-white thinking; less controlled by emotion's ferocity; can complete tasks regardless of mood; follow through on what they say. Disintegrating toward Two (another Feeling-center type): people-pleasing, possessive, manipulative for affection and affirmation; believe themselves defective; at worst perpetual victims seeking rescue [s.14].

| Band | Description [s.14] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Inspiration and creativity, personal self-reflective art; aware of own emotions and their effect on others; intuitive; devoted to honesty, authenticity, self-knowledge |
| Average | Imaginative, artistic, but oversensitive, self-absorbed, take things personally, self-conscious; feel nothing meets expectations; escape into fantasy and daydream |
| Unhealthy | Self-destructive from disappointment; contempt or hatred of self; drug or alcohol self-medication |

**Communication.** "Most emotionally expressive type on the Enneagram"; in touch with emotions at every level of health, which can overwhelm less aware types. Inclined toward darkness and melancholy; drawn to poetry, art, beauty and embellished language, "paint with words" (own or other artists'); often prefer to write, at length; express emotions dramatically. When upset (often) they either withdraw with an extreme cold shoulder or release a wild stream of emotion. Feelings must be validated, not dismissed. Do not tell them to cheer up or smile: melancholy does not mean they are depressed or sad [s.14].

**Intimacy.** Appear sad or depressed to others but are not; more than any other type they can sit with grief, sorrow, melancholy. Problem: other types withdraw, unable to hold such dark emotions; the Four then feels "too dramatic, too dark, or just too much," seals off further, and fears sharing depth. Needs a partner who can handle the full range of emotions and understands "just because they aren't happy, it doesn't mean that they are sad" [s.14].

**Common mistypes.** Four vs Five (4w5 vs 5w4 especially hard): both withdrawn, introverted, value independence; Fours far more in touch with feelings; Fives live in the head. Four vs Six: mostly Sixes who think they are Fours (pessimism, identifying with Four's stress traits); Fours stressed by disappointment in themselves, Sixes by outside entities such as authority figures; Sixes are sociable, Fours content alone. Four vs Nine: Nines (especially artistic ones) mistake themselves for Fours; Fours put their own experience into art, artistic Nines use mythological motifs, archetypes; Nines dissociate from emotions while Fours wallow in feelings and withdraw only from what has hurt them rather than from the pain [s.14].

**Wings.** 4w3 (Aristocrat): both basic type and wing in the Feeling center; more driven and focused, better at follow-through on ambitions, traditional work ethic, more social and extroverted, knows what is expected to succeed and sell their creative work. 4w5 (Bohemian): wing in the Thinking center; research, knowledge, intellectualism; more introverted and withdrawn (Fives are the most isolated and reserved); eclectic, isolated, independent [s.14].

**Acceptance.** Among the most private types (with Five); feel strongly but second-guess; emotions are layered (e.g., sadness, nostalgia, hope and loss within minutes); prefer not to share because others have failed to understand. Need acceptance and validation, especially after opening up. Often need time to process; frequent withdrawal is normal and should be met with understanding [s.14].

### FIVE: The Investigator [s.15]

**Names:** Investigator, Observer; 5w4 = The Iconoclast; 5w6 = The Problem Solver [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Eight: more confident, self-assured, extroverted, comfortable socially; in touch with emotions and body (Eight in the Instinctive/Body center), "get out of their head and back into their body"; take action rather than only think. Disintegrating toward Seven: erratic, disorganized, distracted, short attention spans, seek distraction from worries including substance abuse; dangerously impulsive; thorough thinking replaced by "almost nothing" [s.15].

| Band | Description [s.15] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Deep comprehension of complex ideas, open-minded, mastering varied interests, independent, comfortable with their eccentricities |
| Average | Amass knowledge to guard against insecurity; withdraw for long periods, build safe inner worlds; cynical as they descend |
| Unhealthy | Recluses, dark or nihilistic worldview; aggressive when forced into social contact; self-medicate with drugs, alcohol |

**Communication.** Most introverted and withdrawn of all types, even among the withdrawing three; less practice communicating, especially with heightened emotion (they don't associate closely with how they feel); prefer to observe; excel when explaining something thoroughly researched. Likened to "the stereotypical university professor": formal, technical, unbiased, precise language. In altercations (even annoyance) can be passive-aggressive. Give them space to speak and don't spotlight them when they do [s.15].

**Intimacy.** Heady and intellectual; dislike the spotlight, so "What are you thinking about?" feels like interrogation, whereas "What do you think about this?" invites their expertise. They suppress feelings; don't draw attention when they do show them. Many do not participate in discussions, calls, meetings, but are still taking notes and filing information. Need a partner who understands that "silence is synonymous with neither agreement nor anger," supports alone time, and gently nudges them beyond their comfort zone [s.15].

**Common mistypes.** Five vs Four (see Four chapter); Fives are analysis-focused (data, science, information) while Fours favor free-flowing creativity; whichever is stronger is likely the basic type. Sixes may mistype as Fives (both Thinking, analytical; Sixes research practical topics that help them feel safe); Nines may mistype as Fives (both withdrawing, alone, different motives). Other types mistype as Five far more often than Fives mistype as another (apart from their two wings) [s.15].

**Wings.** 5w4 (Iconoclast): seeks knowledge for identity or to express emotion and ideas, drawn to aesthetics and melancholy, more in touch with feelings and creativity; both types withdrawing, so "perhaps the most reserved and isolated of all the Enneagram types." 5w6 (Problem Solver): seeks knowledge for safety and security, more analytical and data-oriented, motivated by protecting self and others; less introverted, genuinely enjoys others at times and seeks them for human contact; can dissociate from feeling and decide on logic alone [s.15].

**Acceptance.** Need a lot of alone time; have difficulty with energy levels and feel they lack energy for their goals; knowledge-gathering counters fear of inadequacy; often unaware when they give loved ones the cold shoulder. Need a partner who pulls them out of isolation from time to time yet gives enough space to pursue what they love [s.15].

### SIX: The Loyalist [s.16]

**Names:** Loyalist, Trooper; 6w5 = The Defender; 6w7 = The Buddy [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Nine: relaxed, less attention to worry; with Nine in the Instinctive center, they listen to gut reactions; more independent; normally need affirmation from an outside authority figure or system but now listen to their "interior authority." Disintegrating toward Three: brutal competition, high self-opinion; workaholic, obsessing over details of work, life or relationships, micromanaging self and others to gain security [s.16].

| Band | Description [s.16] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Trust self and others more deeply; independent; build relationships, invest in community, cooperate to build safe, stable environment |
| Average | Turn to structure and order; trust authority figures or organizations; imagine worst cases and plan for them; descending, blame others, become passive-aggressive and sarcastic |
| Unhealthy | Actively aggressive; us-versus-them mentality; may turn to violence, conspiracy theories, extremism; irrational, self-destructive |

**Communication.** Want a lot of information; asking many questions is not a challenge but a way to plan and reduce anxiety; short on information, they overthink. Dislike surprises, prefer predictable routines. When comfortable, they may share pressure and anxieties, and are not asking the partner to fix them, only to acknowledge and possibly help work through them [s.16].

**Intimacy.** Extremely loyal to those they love, stemming from fear of being left behind, taken for granted, or abandoned; play out every scenario and counter; insecurities define relationships; unwilling to take risks or be vulnerable. Need a personal connection reinforced so they know on an intimate level the partner will remain; need nonjudgmental listening to fears however illogical; partners should help them look to their own inner voice as well as authorities [s.16].

**Common mistypes.** Wing confusion. 6w7 vs 7w6 test: how they view planning; the 7w6 enjoys planning even if they drift from it, the 6w7 does not enjoy drawing up plans and may be more spontaneous. Sixes more often mistype as Five than the reverse. Most common mistype: Six vs Nine (both want stability and few surprises, both relationship-oriented); tell them apart by response to stress: Nines slog through difficulty without showing much emotion, Sixes show anxiety and defensive postures [s.16].

**Wings.** 6w5 (Defender): more reserved, self-reliant, introverted, independent; Five and Six fears reinforce each other; suspicious of others' motives; can be "doubly anxious" and withdraw. 6w7 (Buddy): more sociable and outgoing; more optimistic; spontaneity and love of the new counterbalances the Six's anxiety [s.16].

**Acceptance.** Need safe, secure environment; dislike surprises, even good ones; their wariness should be accepted and communication kept open, direct and transparent ("how you feel, what you are doing, and why"); if caught off guard "it can take a long time to put them at ease and regain their trust" [s.16].

### SEVEN: The Enthusiast [s.17]

**Names:** Enthusiast, Epicure; 7w6 = The Entertainer; 7w8 = The Realist [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Five: slow down, introspect, savor small moments with loved ones rather than chasing the next experience or "bucket list" item; deeper relaxation. Disintegrating toward One: critical, demanding of perfection, order and correctness; nitpick; rigid, judgmental, even controlling [s.17].

| Band | Description [s.17] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Gratitude rather than greed; still extroverted, spontaneous, enthusiastic but more practical and able to focus on goals instead of running from difficulties |
| Average | Keep many options open, want no limits; lose focus when things get hard; constantly on the move, rarely rest; descending, selfish and self-centered |
| Unhealthy | Anxious; impulsive, erratic, can lose control; exhausted by being unable to rest; risk of substance abuse and addiction |

**Communication.** Fast-moving minds; jump between ideas; appear frenzied but with an internal method; quick to share and to say yes; hurt if ideas are dismissed (though rarely show it); can be read as rude or shallow due to interrupting or topic-hopping. Sevens need to give others space, but accusing them of not listening or being rude is "not a constructive method" [s.17].

**Intimacy.** The most free-spirited; seldom homebodies; avoid heavier relationship experiences and conversations; "the exact opposite of the Four" (Fours find enjoyment in melancholy; Sevens flee sadness, pain, anger). Partners should keep difficult conversations short, and allow Sevens time to process negative emotions [s.17].

**Common mistypes.** Wing confusion (Six or Eight). One when very unhealthy (rigid perfectionism fades as they become healthier). Seven vs Two: both warm and affectionate; Sevens more dramatic in emotions; Twos more devoted to interpersonal communication; Sevens don't intrude unless invited. Seven vs Three: both aggressive, future-oriented; Sevens pursue enriching experiences "as a way of accumulating a rich history of sensations," Threes pursue success, wealth and fame for image: "Sevens wants stimulation while Threes prefer status" [s.17].

**Wings.** 7w6 (Entertainer): mix of introvert and extrovert qualities; tempered relationship with commitment and stability (Sevens fear commitment, Sixes are devoted to it); both types in the Thinking center, so a stronger link to underlying fear and anxiety. 7w8 (Realist): two very extroverted, unapologetic types; Eight is in the Instinctive center, so more access to gut reactions and anger; more energetic, passionate, decisive [s.17].

**Acceptance.** Spontaneous, adventurous, constantly active, most fun-loving; but this is often all they search for or allow; run from the negative or unenjoyable, including things they need to do or feel. Future-oriented; feeling held back causes claustrophobia, hence struggles with long-term commitments (fear of being trapped). Partners must understand the fear of missing out and unwillingness to feel darker emotions; Sevens need a partner to help them slow down, savor, and draw closer to emotions they have ignored [s.17].

### EIGHT: The Challenger [s.18]

**Names:** Challenger, Boss; 8w7 = The Maverick; 8w9 = The Bear [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Two: take others' perspectives, listen to what people feel and think; allow vulnerability. Author notes Eights' lack of concern for others' feelings is not because they don't care "but because it simply does not initially occur to them." Disintegrating toward Five: though extroverted, behave like introverts, isolate, remain out of touch with emotions and more detached; see everything as a threat to be dealt with; rigid; lash out at anyone who crosses them [s.18].

| Band | Description [s.18] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Strength and self-confidence tempered by restraint; comfortable with vulnerability, gentleness that listens to and values others; resourceful, excellent leaders especially in great difficulty |
| Average | Unapologetic individualists, work hard, value independence in self and others; descending, self-centered, egocentric, use threats, intimidation, bullying |
| Unhealthy | Reckless, adversarial, defiant; may turn to crime or violence; build images of power not based in reality; take revenge if crossed |

**Communication.** Straightforward, get to the point, often loud, can feel overly direct or aggressive without meaning to; come across as strong, forceful, brusque, unemotional; hide vulnerabilities because they fear being controlled; can feel as deeply as other types and expose it only when very safe. Value honesty; view flattery as fake and manipulative; prefer confrontation based on honest feelings to friendly superficiality [s.18].

**Intimacy.** "All about protection": themselves and the people they care about; unafraid to be aggressive, confrontational, threatening, intimidating; perhaps the most physical type, many play sports; drawn to strength, run from vulnerability. Partner advice: always be honest, stand ground in conflict (Eights respect those who stand up to them); do not use flattery; Eights need a partner who is not overpowered by their personality and who offers a safe place to feel vulnerable [s.18].

**Common mistypes.** Eight vs Three: both competitive; Eights are not driven by material success or status, rarely compare themselves with others, whereas Threes compare most; Threes more often mistype as Eights than the reverse. Eight vs Seven: Sevens seek variety of experience, Eights seek intensity and depth. Twos in stress mistype as Eights (Eight is their stress point), especially male Twos culturally pressured to be forceful; unlikely the other way; Twos may try to control others but not overpower them, while Eights would more likely use force than mere manipulation [s.18].

**Wings.** 8w7 (Maverick): ambitious, aggressive, very extroverted; both types aggressive; poor impulse control; resourceful and generally meets goals. 8w9 (Bear): laid back, easygoing, Nine wing balances aggression; can lean on the Nine wing to be gentle and diplomatic; chooses which conflicts matter, since "not every battle is worth fighting with their limited internal resources" [s.18].

**Acceptance.** Most aggressive type, but not vicious; accept the forceful, demanding, straightforward style while providing a safe space for vulnerability without being taken advantage of. Because they fear being controlled, they often control others to ward off threat. With acceptance and safety, Eights become more playful and vulnerable, open to acceptance, and less drawn to conflict [s.18].

### NINE: The Peacemaker [s.19]

**Names:** Peacemaker, Mediator; 9w8 = The Referee; 9w1 = The Dreamer [s.8].

**Integration/disintegration.** Integrating toward Three: energetic, focused, driven; see their significance, contribute actively to relationships, projects and communities; confidence; set goals and steps. Disintegrating toward Six: anxious, stressed, frozen in fear, unable to act; suppressed emotions may erupt in anger after being held too long [s.19].

| Band | Description [s.19] |
|---|---|
| Healthy | Secure in identity; little self-consciousness; calming presence; take action to reach goals, shed lethargy |
| Average | "Go along to get along"; fear conflict; untruthful about what they want; procrastinate, which isolates them further |
| Unhealthy | Incapable of handling conflict or disagreement; repress anger and other dark emotions; numb to self and others |

**Communication.** Avoid confrontation at almost any cost and often don't fully express themselves; if dismissed, talked over or ignored they seal themselves off; won't fight for themselves unless pushed. Not talkative but need space and pointed questions to put their ideas into words; need more time to formulate thoughts; may seem aloof but usually aren't [s.19].

**Intimacy.** Value peace and tranquility; avoid arguments and drama, largely subconsciously; "merge" with people, agreeing or appearing to agree; calm, measured, polite, aware of surroundings; reserved, so true feelings take prodding; need safe environment and people who won't challenge directly or aggressively. Self-suppression breeds resentment [s.19].

**Common mistypes.** Nines have the most difficulty finding their type, with a less defined sense of self from lifelong merging ("one reason why the Nine sits atop the Enneagram looking down at all of the others, absorbing their qualities"). Wing confusion more common with One than Eight (Eights are far more aggressive). Nine vs Two: often by average Nines and women socially conditioned toward Two traits; Twos have hidden motive for affection (wanting love, acceptance, being needed in return) while Nines are more selfless, don't need reciprocal reinforcement. Nine vs Four: Nines more often think they're Fours; both withdrawing, introverted and creative, but Fours' art is very personal while Nines draw on mythology, archetypes, subconscious motifs [s.19].

**Wings.** 9w8 (Referee): more aggressive and forceful, social, active, open to teamwork; more likely to intervene and mediate. 9w1 (Dreamer): more creative and imaginative, prone to fantasies and daydreams; can lean on the One wing to order life and pursue goals; less outgoing, more reserved; prefers to work alone [s.19].

**Acceptance.** Can see every side without taking sides; impartial; yet merge, lose identity and preferences; go through the motions; retreat. Resentment of being ignored or undervalued makes them passive-aggressive (subtle clues rather than Eight-style aggression). Easily taken advantage of since they will agree to avoid altercations. Partners should be direct about what they want, ask pointed questions ("What do you want to do?" "What do you think about this?") and listen to answers, to draw out the Nine's individual personality and avoid merging [s.19].

## Conclusion [s.30]

- The Enneagram helps people understand themselves and then their relations to others; find dominant type and stronger wing, then explore behavior in health and stress, and observe how others' types influence you, especially in romantic relationships. If single, the teachings can help one understand what type and kind of person might suit them in future [s.30].
- Each type has its own journey, difficulties, weaknesses, triggers and insecurities to overcome and turn into strengths. Knowing both partners' integration and disintegration directions lets a couple catch a slide into lower regions "before your relationship might be irrevocably harmed" [s.30].
- The book offers "a framework" to understand oneself and oneself in relationship, improving "communication, understanding, empathy, and compassion"; the Enneagram's layers can take "years, if not an entire lifetime" to peel back [s.30].
