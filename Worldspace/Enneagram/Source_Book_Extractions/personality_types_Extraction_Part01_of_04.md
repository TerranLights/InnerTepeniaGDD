## Overview of this range

This part contains the book's foundations and the first two of its nine type chapters. The Authors' Note and Preface frame the Enneagram as a modern, evolving, non-oral-tradition system in which self-understanding precedes transformation and the healthy Levels of each type are the "virtues" of that type; the Direction of Integration is living from Essence. Chapter 1 argues why personality typology is worth studying (Kierkegaard's "subjective toward others, objective toward ourselves"), compares Freud, Jung and Horney, and states the Enneagram's organizing principle: nine types in three Triads (Feeling, Thinking, Instinctive). Chapter 2 gives an unusually skeptical history: the symbol's ancient and Sufi origins are conjecture; Gurdjieff brought the symbol to the West; Oscar Ichazo is credited with joining the nine types to the symbol; Claudio Naranjo, the Jesuits and Riso carried and developed it; and Riso's own contribution was the continuum of healthy, average and unhealthy traits (the Levels of Development, 1977). Chapter 3 is the practical manual: the structure of the symbol, the dialectic of each Triad, the "basic type" rules, a self-typing guide, the wing, the nine Levels, the Directions of Integration and Disintegration (with the Level-matching rule and the "four types" picture), and advice on typing others. Chapters 4 and 5 then give, for the Two and the Three: a profile, an overview (Triad role, hostility and identity or narcissism, parental orientation), nine named Levels, the stress and growth arrows, and two named wing subtypes each.

## Front matter (Authors' Note, Preface, Acknowledgments) [s.6 to s.8]

**Authors' Note for the Second Edition (March 1996) [s.6].** The first edition of *Personality Types* appeared in 1987; this revised edition adds about fifty thousand new words.
- The Enneagram is "a work in progress"; the authors approach it "in the spirit of scientific inquiry." There is no "Enneagram bible" and no oral tradition of any kind passed down; the first edition may have encouraged that mistaken idea. The system is "largely a modern development, the work of Oscar Ichazo, Claudio Naranjo, the authors, and other contemporary writers."
- Clear, precise language is stressed as vital to transformational work: the more specifically we see ourselves, the more possible it is to let go of old patterns, and as patterns loosen, awareness expands and the sense of self shifts.
- Tone: a "softer, warmer voice"; efforts to remove bias and inequality in how the nine types are presented, so that readers can acknowledge the problematic aspects of their type with less difficulty.
- New in this edition: **the Core Dynamics with their Levels of Development** for each type (previously available only to students of the authors' Professional Training Programs); presented explicitly in the appendix and implicitly in each type chapter's Levels. Expanded account of the origins and history of the symbol; clarified childhood patterns and developmental origins (for example Type Three is now "connected to the nurturing-figure," who may or may not be the biological mother and need not be "positive"); longer descriptions and specific names for each of the eighteen wing subtypes (for example the Four with a Three-wing is "the Aristocrat," the Four with a Five-wing "the Bohemian"); a Level-by-Level description of each type's Direction of Disintegration as an "early warning signal" of acting out under stress; expanded Directions of Integration; a brief original explanation of the instinctual orientations (self-preservation, social, sexual).
- Renamings: "the Status Seeker" became **the Motivator** (the Three); "the Artist" became **the Individualist** (Four); "the Thinker" became **the Investigator** (Five); "the Generalist" became **the Enthusiast** (Seven). Triads renamed: "Relating Triad" became the **Instinctive Triad**; "Doing Triad" became the **Thinking Triad** (so Triad names match the three traditional centers: thinking, feeling, instinct). Additional thematic material on each type's relation to its Triad: resistance is central to Eight, Nine and One; Two, Three and Four all seek validation for a self-image.

**Preface: The Enneagram and Transformation [s.7].**
- Overriding theme: acknowledge and understand our inner states so we can move beyond them. Self-understanding is the prelude to transformation, to moving beyond the ego and "false personality." Transcending the ego is "the gate to every spiritual path," and the Enneagram shows each type what that gate is and how to pass through it.
- The types map "the precipitous cliffs, arid deserts, and treacherous quicksands of the soul" and also "the fertile oases, restful forests, and life-producing springs." It is a map not only of personality states but of what lies beyond personality.
- Spirituality shows up in daily life as a changing array of **virtues**, which are spontaneous expressions of the human spirit and are "the traits we find in the healthy Levels of Development of each type." When healthy, we are being virtuous and moving out of ego states toward integration. Moving in the **Direction of Integration** is living out of one's **"Essence"**: "an expression of our best and truest self."
- The Enneagram "is itself not a form of spirituality but a means to spirituality of all kinds." Gurdjieff claimed it was the "philosopher's stone" turning lead to gold; the authors apply that to transformation of ourselves and our lives. Yet it is "merely a tool and an intellectual system," giving wisdom to choose well and objectivity to see the truth; this prepares one to accept Grace, "which alone can transform our lives."

**Acknowledgments [s.8].** Personal thanks (first edition 1987 by Riso; revised edition 1996 by Riso and Hudson). Content points: Tad Dunne suggested reading Karen Horney; Bob Fecas encouraged developing the healthy side of the types; public lectures gave the early confirmation of the system's validity. Students contributed to the revision: Tom Markey argued for the parental orientations of the types; Joan Jennings had a seminal insight into the structure of the Triads in relation to integration. Hudson found the book about a year after its 1987 publication; the partnership began in 1988. Closing line: the Enneagram "shows us the pattern of our dreams. What it cannot reveal to us is what we will discover if, finally, we awaken." (Rest of acknowledgments: names and thanks only, summarized.)

# PART I

## Chapter 1: Understanding Personality Types [s.10]

Epigraph: Pope, "The proper study of mankind is man."

**Why study personality types.**
- Objection answered: cramming unique people into categories seems odious, and typologies may be too academic or too vague. Reply: human beings are "inherently interesting and dangerous"; we meet dozens of people daily, and without insight we may be abused by a "monster" we thought we knew, or overlook "a diamond in the rough" or leave a relationship worth saving. Understanding ourselves and others "should make us happier."
- Everyone wants insight into others, few into themselves. Competitive culture turned Delphi's "know thyself" into "psych out the other guy."
- **Kierkegaard's advice** adopted: be **subjective toward others** (put yourself in their place, understand how they see themselves) and **objective toward ourselves** (see ourselves as others do, resisting extenuating circumstances). Needs courage toward ourselves and empathy toward others.
- The paradox: we cannot know others until we know ourselves, and cannot know ourselves until we know others; both are "two sides of the same coin," understanding human nature. A map of human nature is needed.

**The Enneagram.** Pronounced "ANY-a-gram." Offered as "the map of human nature which people have long sought"; the symbol is ancient and many psychological roots are old, yet it is contemporary because human nature has not changed. It is a distillation of several spiritual-wisdom schools combined with modern psychology.
- Typology history: from Hippocrates (fifth century B.C.) Greeks recognized types; classifications have had "problems, inaccuracies, or contradictions" (too few categories, too abstract, or only neuroses). A typology that also shows how the types relate, hence how people change and grow, was the unsolved problem.
- **Other systems and their organizing principles (as the author presents them).** Freud: oral, anal, phallic types (fixation of psychic energy), and the ego/id/superego dominance version; both correlate with Enneagram types. Jung: eight types from extroversion or introversion modified by one of four functions (feeling, thinking, sensation, intuition). Horney: moving toward, away from, against others; she would probably have reached nine types had she worked out all subtypes. (Correspondences are developed in the Theory chapter, outside this part.)
- **Organizing principle of the Enneagram:** nine types arise from three types in each of three groups, the **Triads**: **Feeling Triad** (emotions and self-image), **Thinking Triad** (thought processes and how security is found), **Instinctive Triad** ("gut" instincts and how one relates to the world).
- First one-line characterizations: **Feeling Triad**: Helper (Two: encouraging, demonstrative, possessive); Motivator (Three: ambitious, pragmatic, image-conscious); Individualist (Four: sensitive, self-absorbed, depressive). **Thinking Triad**: Investigator (Five: perceptive, cerebral, provocative); Loyalist (Six: committed, dutiful, suspicious); Enthusiast (Seven: spontaneous, fun-loving, excessive). **Instinctive Triad**: Leader (Eight: self-confident, assertive, confrontational); Peacemaker (Nine: pleasant, easygoing, complacent); Reformer (One: rational, idealistic, orderly). [note: the Eight is here called "the Leader"; later chapters of the book may use another name for the Eight.]
- The Enneagram works on several levels of abstraction at once (Freudian, Jungian, Hornevian viewpoints); it bridges depth psychology and behavior. The nine categories are discrete but not "ironclad"; the system is fluid, with movement toward integration or disintegration essential. Because descriptions run from highest health to deepest neurosis, they predict behavior as well as describe it.
- Practical uses named: therapists (common language between client and therapist whatever the school); lawyers (client credibility, cooperation, divorce and custody cases); physicians; clergy; teachers (aptitudes, learning approaches); managers, hiring officers, team builders; journalists, politicians, advertisers. But "this is really a book which has been written for you, the individual."
- **Caution:** not a typical self-help book and promises no miracles. "Knowledge would be virtue, and it is not"; knowing more is a means, not a bestowal of virtue or happiness. No one has a "God's-eye view" of human nature, so psychology always has an element of faith "not necessarily religious," a set of beliefs beyond what can be shown scientifically. Final objective truth about ourselves is probably unattainable; "being searchers on the quest" matters more, and gradual honest seeking frees us from painful, limiting beliefs about who we are.

## Chapter 2: Origins [s.11]

**The symbol's origins are lost.** Writers claim Sufi origin (from the tenth and eleventh centuries) or Babylon about 2500 B.C.; the authors call these "mere speculations."
- **J. G. Bennett's account (quoted at length, summarized here):** a brotherhood of wise men arose in Mesopotamia about 4,500 years ago, kept the secret of perpetual self-renewal in Babylon, revealed it 2,500 years ago to Zoroaster, Pythagoras and others in Babylon in Cambyses' time; custodians migrated north and reached Bokhara about a thousand years ago; in the fifteenth century Islamic mathematicians discovered zero and the decimal system, and noticed the recurring decimal when one is divided by three or seven. These properties combined in a symbol "that could be used to represent every process that maintains itself by self-renewal." Nine lines, hence Enneagram. Bennett elsewhere ties the form to Samarkand (fourteenth century) and notes Gurdjieff attributed it to the "Sarmāan Brotherhood"; "both versions may be true."
- **Mathematical structure (Bennett, quoted):** number the nine points; 3, 6, 9 form the triangle; 1-4-2-8-5-7 is the hexagon of the recurring sequence for division by seven. This works only in a decimal system.
- "Ennea" is Greek for nine; "Enneagram" means "a nine diagram." Plausible conjecture offered: it rests on ancient Pythagorean and Neo-Platonic mathematics and was passed West by Muslims in the fourteenth or fifteenth century, used by Sufis, particularly the Naqshbandi Brotherhood.
- **Authors' correction:** the symbol is by no means common to all Sufi traditions; most Sufi orders have never heard of it; "Sufi" names many diverse esoteric schools from North Africa to Pakistan. More likely some Sufi schools preserved it as Christian monasteries preserved Greek and Roman thought; calling it Sufi "is like calling Aristotle and Greek mythology Christian." More likely sources: early Judeo-Christian teaching and early Greek philosophy; the geometry suggests Pythagoras (the authors give his school as flourishing in the fourth and fifth centuries B.C.).
- **Remote origins of the nine types:** the **Seven Deadly Sins** (pride, envy, anger, gluttony, avarice, lust, sloth) from medieval Christianity, plus two more "sins" to make nine; older roots in the Kabbala, Neo-Platonic philosophers, and the Desert Fathers (early Christian ascetics who probably originated the seven deadly sins concept).

**Gurdjieff (ca. 1877 to 1949).** Transmitted the *symbol* to the West. Adventurer, teacher, seeker of "practical secret knowledge about human nature"; an enigma, perhaps a charlatan or perhaps underrated, deliberately mysterious. The Enneagram became known in Europe in the 1910s and 1920s through lectures in St. Petersburg and Moscow, then his school near Fontainebleau (Institute for the Harmonious Development of Man), then private study groups in Paris, London, New York.
- James Webb (*The Harmonious Circle*): Gurdjieff's enneagram was a circle with nine points joined as a triangle and an irregular six-sided figure; triangle = presence of higher forces, six-sided figure = man; he claimed the symbol was exclusive to his teaching. Webb found a figure called an "enneagram" in Athanasius Kircher's *Arithmologia* (Rome, 1665), but it is made of three equilateral triangles, not Gurdjieff's triangle with inner hexagon: "a crucial difference" that Webb glosses over.
- Gurdjieff's "type" ideas are only tangentially related to the nine types: his "Man Number One, Two, and Three" correspond to the **Instinctive, Feeling and Thinking Triads** respectively, "but that is as far as it goes." Relevant Gurdjieffian ideas: **personality** (the learned, acquired part) versus **essence** (the innate part of human nature) addressed for real transformation. He taught the Enneagram mainly through sacred dances and movements; it is a "living symbol," in constant motion, combining three universal laws. Whatever he knew about type was never linked to the symbol or transmitted completely.

**Oscar Ichazo.** Founder of the Arica Institute; per the authors "the sole originator of the Enneagram of personality types." Attributes his work to the Hebrew Kabbala and Neo-Platonic philosophy. In a 1993 interview (Michael Goldberg, "The Enneagram Wars," LA Weekly) he denied a Sufi origin ("They couldn't care less about the Enneagon") and named Plotinus' *Enneads* (described as nine mystical states, origin of the doctrines studied in the Kabbala as the ten Sephirot) and then Pythagoras seen through the Kabbala. [note: the text itself dates Plotinus "second century B.C.", as printed in this edition.]
- Ichazo's contribution: assigning the nine **"ego fixations"** and **"passions"** around the symbol, with practices to open latent spiritual capacities. Passions (based on the seven deadly sins plus two): One Anger; Two Pride; Three Deceit; Four Envy; Five Avarice; Six Fear; Seven Gluttony; Eight Lust; Nine Sloth. (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material)
- Ichazo began teaching at the Institute for Applied Psychology in La Paz, Bolivia, later in the 1960s in Arica, Chile; came to the U.S. in 1971 and founded the Arica Institute. Early students from Esalen included John Lilly, M.D., and psychiatrist Claudio Naranjo, M.D.
- The authors give Ichazo full credit for "combining the core ideas of the nine types with the Enneagram symbol in the correct sequence and combination," agree the Enneagram works best within serious commitment to psychological growth and spiritual practice, and list what Ichazo includes that they omit: "traps," "holy ideas," passions and virtues, organs and bodily systems, "mentations," astrological signs, mantras. Their own interpretation diverges in making the types more comprehensive and accessible and in aligning them with modern psychology; basic type descriptions taught in Arica and by other teachers differ significantly.

**Claudio Naranjo.** Chilean-born psychiatrist; in the first Esalen group with Ichazo in Arica in 1970; taught it in Berkeley to a group he called **SAT (Seekers After Truth)**, after a Gurdjieff group. Credited with expanding the type descriptions and correlating them with psychiatric categories, by interviewing self-sorted groups (using his psychiatry and Gestalt therapy skills), which evolved into the **panel-of-exemplars** method used in workshops.

**The Jesuit stream and Riso.** Early 1970s: American Jesuits (notably Rev. Robert Ochs, S.J.) learned it from Naranjo's SAT group; taught it at Loyola University, Chicago; spread via seminarians and counseling; first brief notes written 1972 to 1973 at Jesuit centers (Berkeley, Loyola). Riso met it in 1974 in Toronto as a Jesuit seminarian at the University of Toronto: the "Jesuit material" was nine one-page impressionistic sketches; Jesuits called the types "Sufi numbers."
- Riso's account (first person): skeptical, saw it as a California fad, disliked being pigeonholed; a "conversion" one winter morning in 1974 reading the sketches before dawn, recognizing his own type and then others'. It made him see "personality types" as real classes within which people are still unique, like kinds of primates. After about two years of personal use he tried to interpret it by modern psychology; began full-time work on it in 1975.
- Problems found: three differing sources (Gurdjieff, Ichazo, the Jesuits); additions and arbitrary changes by successive teachers (colors and animals assigned to types differ across teachers); contradictions on basics like the Directions of Integration and Disintegration; **misattribution of traits** from one type to another; material that focused on the unhealthy side only. So he developed the healthy and average traits, sorting hundreds or thousands of traits and fitting them into a unified whole, "relying on the psychological principle that neuroses grow out of distortions and conflicts in normal modes of behavior." This yielded the **continuum of healthy, average and unhealthy traits for each type, the Levels of Development**.
- Retained: the nine types; discarded: esoteric elements and interpretations neither useful nor accurate. There is "no scientific proof"; Riso did no formal research beyond observation, intuition and reading; interest is "on the humanistic side." The proof is "the ring of truth" and "a shock of recognition" on finding one's type.
- Gurdjieff's advice quoted as applicable to the book: judge everything by common sense, "become the possessor of your own sound ideas and don't accept anything on faith."
- A diagram titled "The Transmission of the Enneagram" appears (image; contents not in the text).

## Chapter 3: Guidelines [s.12]

**Structure of the Enneagram.** Nine equidistant points on a circle, numbered 1 to 9 with Nine at the top (by convention and symmetry). Each is a type. **Three, Six, Nine** form an equilateral triangle. The other six connect: **1 to 4, 4 to 2, 2 to 8, 8 to 5, 5 to 7, 7 to 1** (an irregular hexagram). Suggestion: sketch it yourself.

**The Triads.** Three types per Triad, characterized by the assets and liabilities of that Triad's faculty. Each Triad is a **dialectic** (thesis, antithesis, synthesis): one type *overexpresses* the faculty, one *underexpresses*, one is *most out of touch* with it.

| Triad | Overexpresses | Most out of touch | Underdeveloped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling | Two (only positive feelings shown; negatives repressed; sometimes histrionic) | Three (suppresses feelings to function and impress) | Four (underdeveloped personal expression; reveals self indirectly via art or aesthetic living; feelings dependent on thinking about them) |
| Thinking | Five (thinking substitutes for doing; ever more complex yet isolated thought) | Six (out of touch with own thoughts; seeks reassurance; circular thinking ungrounded in experience) | Seven (leaves trains of thought incomplete; thinking depends on activity and anticipation) |
| Instinctive | Eight (moves on gut hunches and reactions; does not pause to foresee consequences) | Nine (disengages from instincts and reactions to keep inner peace) | One (represses instinct with a strict superego-driven conscience; instinct dependent on feelings, especially anger) |

- **Primary types** (on the triangle): **Three, Six, Nine**: most blocked in feeling, thinking or instinct respectively. **Secondary types** (hexagram: One, Four, Two, Eight, Five, Seven): more mixed, less out of touch. (Developed in Advanced Guidelines; also see below if covered in this range.)
- Everyone has all three faculties; one becomes the type because the ego, from early childhood, identifies more with one faculty than the other two. The Enneagram is "a universal mandala of the self."

**The Basic Personality Type.**
- One of nine points describes you more fully than any other. Personality is influenced by the child's relationship with parents or significant persons; by about age four or five the child has a separate sense of self; heredity gives temperament, but the basic type "represents the total outcome of all childhood factors... including genetics," the way the child unconsciously adapted to family and world.
- Five points: (1) **people do not change basic type**; they change in many ways but remain that type for life. (2) Descriptions are universal; **no type is inherently masculine or feminine**. (3) Not everything applies at all times, because people fluctuate among healthy, average and unhealthy traits; if you became healthy or unhealthy you would do so as the Enneagram predicts. (4) **Numbers** are used because they are value neutral and not pejorative like psychiatric labels (obsessive, depressive, psychopathic, antisocial); numeric rank is meaningless. (5) **No type is better or worse than another**; some are rewarded more by a given culture, such as aggressive extroverted types in a competitive society, but each has limits and assets.

**Identifying your basic type.** Choose the one word that best describes you most of the time, then check these four-word descriptions:
- Two: caring, generous, possessive, manipulative.
- Three: adaptable, ambitious, image-conscious, hostile.
- Four: intuitive, expressive, self-absorbed, depressive.
- Five: perceptive, original, detached, eccentric.
- Six: engaging, committed, defensive, paranoid.
- Seven: enthusiastic, accomplished, uninhibited, manic.
- Eight: self-confident, decisive, dominating, combative.
- Nine: peaceful, reassuring, complacent, neglectful.
- One: principled, orderly, perfectionistic, self-righteous.

**Feeling Triad (Two, Three, Four): assets, liabilities and common problem.**
- Healthy Two: sustains positive feelings for others, compassionate, generous, loving, thoughtful, goes out of the way to serve. Average Two: possessive, controlling, needy but unable to state needs directly, intrudes. Unhealthy Two: deceives self about negative feelings, especially rage and resentment; wants to be seen as loving and good while manipulating and acting selfishly.
- Healthy Three: wants to improve self, adapts, builds self-esteem, motivates others by being genuinely admirable in a socially valued way. Average Three: most out of touch with emotions and individuality; suppresses feelings to perform and be liked. Unhealthy Three: hostile and extremely malicious if denied admiring attention.
- Healthy Four: intuitive self-awareness, personal, communicates feelings so others contact theirs. Average Four: too aware of (negative) feelings, withdraws, lives in imagination. Unhealthy Four: depressed, alienated, tormented by self-doubt and self-hatred; suicidal when unable to cope.
- **Common problems:** identity and hostility (turned on self, others or both). They reject the authentic self for a persona believed more acceptable; self-esteem, personal value, appreciation, shame, and validation of the created self-image are central concerns.
- **How each copes:** Two rejects true feelings and qualities (hurt, need, shame) to see self (and be seen) as entirely loving; seeks validation outside from other people; if unresponsive, represses disappointment and redoubles effort, driving people away and growing resentful and heartbroken. Four is nearly the reverse: never sure who they are, builds a self-image inward from feelings and imagination, a self-image that only resembles real life, so they reject real life for an idealized self they cannot live up to; Fours suppress *positive* feelings to maintain the "victim" self-image, and derive identity from difference, even alienation, suppressing the "ordinary." Three, the center, both looks outward for what valued people value and inward (inner "pep talks," long-term fantasies of success and adulation); Threes are potentially the most estranged from their own feelings and needs; having rejected the authentic self, "none of their accomplishments can really affect or satisfy them."

**Thinking Triad (Five, Six, Seven).**
- Healthy Five: most profoundly perceptive, knowledgeable in some area, brilliant inventive solutions. Average Five: more at home thinking and imagining than doing; gets lost in the mazes of the mind while life and opportunity diminish. Unhealthy Five: isolated from reality, creates more problems than solved, cannot tell real from unreal.
- Healthy Six: thinks systematically, foresees problems, loyal and faithful, committed. Average Six: seeks "permission" from authority or belief system, unsure unless authority is on their side, yet asserts against authority to prove independence. Unhealthy Six: anxiety, inferiority, insecurity; self-destructively brings about feared consequences.
- Healthy Seven: quick avid minds, many things done exceptionally well, exuberant. Average Seven: restless thinking, moves topic to topic before finishing; the more they do, the less satisfied; keeps mind occupied so anxiety stays away; fear of "missing out"; wants more of everything to avoid deprivation. Unhealthy Seven: self-centered, dissipated escapist, flying impulsively out of control.
- **Common problem:** insecurity and anxiety from a profound lack of support (from others or the environment); each uses a different solution for security. Five: environment feels threatening and overwhelming; security by expertise and by reducing connections and dependencies; withdraws into mind until skill develops. Seven: reverse; seems unafraid, but is anxious about inner reality (losses, grief, pain); plunges into activity at the first sign of fear; "Fives retreat from the external world of activity into the security of their minds, Sevens flee from the anxiety in their minds by finding security in the external world of activity." Six, the middle: anxious about both outer environment and inner fear and grief; builds support systems in the world and a consistent belief system; depends on anything from a secure job to a friend to a philosophy or religion.

**Instinctive Triad (Eight, Nine, One).**
- Healthy Eight: tremendous vitality, intuition that sees possibilities in people and situations, courage, leadership that inspires great accomplishments. Average Eight: dominates, asserts impulsively, indulges needs for control and satisfaction regardless of consequences. Unhealthy Eight: bully and tyrant, ruthless.
- Healthy Nine: open, identifies intimately with a person or belief, centered and calm when others panic; receptive, optimistic, peaceful, creates harmony so others flourish. Average Nine: disengages from real connection with own drives, others and the environment; idealizes the other (person or abstraction). Unhealthy Nine: dangerously fatalistic and neglectful, clinging to illusions and dissociated from reality.
- Healthy One: impartial, wise, conscientious, guided by principles and inner "knowing." Average One: out of balance with drives and instincts, which they over-control; strives for absolute perfection, cannot accept things as they are. Unhealthy One: intolerant, self-righteous, obsessed with corruption in others while ignoring own contradictions; cruel to self and others in the name of ideals.
- **Common problem:** repression and aggression, each resisting some part of experience to maintain ego-boundaries. Eight is openly assertive, expresses instinctive energy directly, resists the external world, especially other people, for fear of being controlled or harmed; adopts a tough defiant stance, repressing vulnerability, tenderness and desire for closeness. One is almost the reverse: aggression is more subtle and mostly self-directed (steady self-criticism); aggression toward others is often an attempt to deflect the superego's attacks; resists the *inner* world, repressing impulses and instincts; more control produces more volatility; worn down by the inner war. Nine, the center, resists both the outer world (like Eight) and inner drives (like One); because instincts are repressed, responses "frozen"; "a kind of cancellation of their own instinctual life and drives," apathy, loss of vitality; occupies a safe middle ground and an idealized version of reality; gains freedom from conflict but sacrifices "a real and vibrant experience of their own lives."

**Practical guidance on typing yourself.** Narrow to two or three candidates and read the full descriptions. A common error is picking the type you would like to be; being objective is hard. You may see yourself in many traits; when you read your own type, "you will know it" (chills, uneasy stomach). You visit other types' traits, but your type is **"home base,"** the lens through which all experience is interpreted; understanding it reduces distortion. Descriptions can be emotionally difficult (anxiety or depression); put the book aside if so; reflecting on them can be cathartic.

**The Wing.** No one is a "pure" type. Most people are a blend of the basic type and one of the two adjacent types, the **wing**. The basic type dominates; the wing is the "second side" and adds important, sometimes contradictory, elements. A Nine has a One-wing or an Eight-wing. Degree of wing influence varies; everyone has some of all nine types; some people have both wings about equally; the authors find most have a **dominant wing** and the two combinations differ so much they are separate **eighteen wing subtypes** (Four with a Three-wing vs. Four with a Five-wing), each named and described in the type chapters. Diagnose the basic type first, then read both adjacent types' descriptions and the wing-subtype descriptions at the end of each type chapter. A diagram "The 18 Riso-Hudson Wing Subtypes" is an image. (Wing names are listed in the repository's existing Enneagram material.)

**The Levels of Development.** Each type's analysis moves from healthy to average to unhealthy traits: the **Levels of Development**, discovered by Riso in **1977**; **nine internal Levels per type** give a framework for all constituent traits and motivations and make misattributed traits evident. To understand someone you need basic type, wing, and where they lie along the Levels (healthy, average or unhealthy); two people of the same type and wing differ greatly if one is healthy and the other unhealthy. The continuum is intuitive: we change constantly for better or worse by shifting within our type's spectrum. Analogy: a photographer's **gray scale** from white to black, healthiest traits at top, descending Level by Level to "the pure black of neurosis." (A diagram of the Levels is an image.) How and why we move along the Levels is covered in Advanced Guidelines.

**The Levels of Development (second, fuller treatment in the same chapter) [s.12].**
- Nine Levels per type: **three healthy, three average, three unhealthy**. Traits at each Level are "arrayed in related clusters," not arbitrary; each type description shows key traits from each cluster as you move from health toward neurosis.
- The Levels give a frame for placing every trait. In the healthy range we can move in the **Direction of Integration**; in the average-to-unhealthy range we "act out" in the **Direction of Disintegration**.
- **Disintegration is "the path of least resistance,"** the result of acting on the habitual mechanisms of the personality. **Integration always involves conscious choice and effort**, but not "the effort of striving and straining, or condemning ourselves for our failures" (that usually produces inner conflict, more anxiety, even descent to lower Levels). The effort required is **the effort of letting go of old personality patterns**. As the Enneagram shows habitual thoughts, reactions and behaviors working against our interest, we find "the serenity and freedom to let go." "Observing our fears, hurts, and weaknesses without judgment and without justification is one of the most compassionate things we can do for ourselves," and frees us to extend this to others.

**Directions of Integration and Disintegration (the arrows) [s.12].** (also in the repository's existing Enneagram material, `Enneagram_Dynamics.md`; this is the book's own formulation.)
- The lines on the symbol are psychological: each type has a Direction of Integration (health, self-actualization) and a Direction of Disintegration (unhealth, neurosis). Types are "not static categories; they are open-ended."
- **Disintegration sequences:** hexagram **1-4-2-8-5-7-1**; triangle **9-6-3-9**. Under increased stress and anxiety, an average-to-unhealthy One acts out average-to-unhealthy Four behaviors; Four acts out Two; Two acts out Eight; Eight acts out Five; Five acts out Seven; Seven acts out One; Nine acts out Six; Six acts out Three; Three acts out Nine.
- **Integration sequences (the reverse):** **1-7-5-8-2-4-1** and **9-3-6-9**. Integrating One goes to Seven, Seven to Five, Five to Eight, Eight to Two, Two to Four, Four to One; Nine to Three, Three to Six, Six to Nine.

| Type | Integrates to | Disintegrates to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 | 4 |
| 2 | 4 | 8 |
| 3 | 6 | 9 |
| 4 | 1 | 2 |
| 5 | 8 | 7 |
| 6 | 9 | 3 |
| 7 | 5 | 1 |

- The book notes the two directions can be drawn on one Enneagram with plain lines; it advises memorizing both sequences. [note: in the text's printed Direction of Integration diagram label the sequence reads "1-7-5-8-1-4-1", an apparent typo; the running text and the second diagram give 1-7-5-8-2-4-1.]
- They are **metaphors for psychological processes**; there is no literal movement around the figure.
- **Worked example (Six).** Lines go to Nine and Three. Healthy Six moving to Nine becomes relaxed, accepting, peaceful, more self-possessed and less anxious. Stressed average-to-unhealthy Six (anxious, feeling inferior, dependent on external support such as job, family, friends or belief system) acts out Three: driven and competitive to keep others' good regard; disengaged from feelings and identified with tasks and performance, brisk and "professional"; arrogant and self-promoting to overcompensate for inferiority; in overwhelming anxiety, the neurotic Three: covering mistakes, deceiving, driving after whatever restores security and self-esteem.
- **Level matching rule:** a person acts out the other type's behavior at roughly the **same Level** at which they function in their own type (a Six at Level 5 acts out Three at Level 5; an Eight at Level 4 acts out Five at Level 4). Each type chapter gives the Level-by-Level description of disintegration.
- **Four-type picture:** "no matter what your basic personality type," the types in both of your Directions also influence you. The fullest picture blends **basic type, wing, and the types in the Directions of Integration and Disintegration** (four types). Example: a Two has a One-wing or Three-wing, plus Eight (disintegration) and Four (integration).
- Method for further detail: to learn what a Two integrating to Four involves, read the healthy Four with the healthy Two in mind; to learn a Nine disintegrating to Six, read the average-to-unhealthy Six and apply to the Nine. "When a type integrates, it appropriates the healthy traits of the type in its Direction of Integration, and when it deteriorates, it appropriates the commensurate average to unhealthy traits of the type in its Direction of Disintegration."
- Why the lines predict: each Direction of Integration is "a natural outgrowth of the healthiest qualities of that type"; each type "flows into the next," with the integration type a further development and the disintegration type "further enmeshment in conflicted ego states."
- **Ultimate goal:** to move "completely around the Enneagram," integrating what each type symbolizes and gaining active use of all the types' healthy potentials, to become a balanced, fully functioning human being. "Which personality type you begin life as is ultimately unimportant. What matters is what you do with your personality type."

**Getting Started: how the type chapters are organized [s.12].** Each type chapter has: **Caricature and Profile** (over a hundred key adjectives as a checklist); **Overview** (comparison within the Triad, parental orientation and childhood patterns, Jungian and other correlations, major themes); **analysis by Levels** (healthy, then average, then unhealthy); the **Direction of Disintegration** and the **Direction of Integration**; the **two wing subtypes** with famous examples (educated guesses, not statements of health or neurosis; public personas may differ from private selves); parenthetical quotations are flavor, not real citations.
- **Typing practice (the book's guidance).** Learn the traits that go with each type and observe how people manifest them; like a beginning medical student diagnosing syndromes, it takes practice; psychological aptitude varies; there is no easy automatic method. **Do not diagnose from a single trait.** Example, being "bossy": Eights order people around as if saying "Do as I say because I have power over you and I will punish you if you disobey me!"; Ones as "Don't argue with me: just do as I say because I am right"; Twos as "You really don't want to hurt me, do you? You may as well do what I'm asking"; Sixes via blustering aggressiveness; Sevens by demanding that others give them what they want. Likewise "depressive" and "feeling misunderstood" are not unique to Fours: all nine types can be depressed or feel misunderstood. You must see each type as a whole: overall style, approach to life, basic motivations.
- **Purpose.** To become a fully functioning person; not perfection, not world-denying asceticism; "we will always be incomplete and limited." Becoming fully human yields a list of paradoxes: free yet constrained by necessity; shrewd yet innocent; open to others yet self-reliant; strong yet able to yield; centered on the highest values yet able to accept imperfection; realistic about suffering yet grateful for life as it is. "The way to make the most of ourselves is by transcending ourselves."

# PART II: THE NINE PERSONALITY TYPES [s.13]

*(Within this range the book gives only Type Two and Type Three; Type Four begins at the very end of the next section and is not in this range.)*

## Chapter 4: Type Two, The Helper [s.14]

### The Two in Profile (the book's checklist)
- **Healthy:** empathetic, compassionate, feeling with and for others; caring; outgoing and passionate; thoughtful, warm-hearted, forgiving, sincere; encouraging, appreciative, seeing the good; nurturing, generous, giving. **At their best:** deeply unselfish, humble, altruistic, giving unconditional love to self and others; feel it a privilege to be in others' lives; radiantly joyful and gracious.
- **Average:** "people pleasing" to be closer; overly friendly, emotionally demonstrative, full of "good intentions"; seductive attention (approval, "strokes," flattery); talkative about love and relationships; overly intimate and intrusive: "need to be needed," hover, meddle, control in the name of love; give but expect a return; mixed messages; enveloping and possessive "self-sacrificial, parenting" persons who create needs to fulfill; self-important, feel indispensable, seek specific forms of repayment; hypochondria, "martyr"; overbearing, patronizing, presumptuous.
- **Unhealthy:** manipulative and self-serving, instilling guilt by making others feel indebted; abuse food and medications to "stuff feelings" and get sympathy; belittling, disparaging remarks; extremely self-deceptive about motives; domineering, coercive, feel entitled, bitterly resentful; somatization of aggression, chronic health problems, vindicate themselves by "falling apart" and burdening others.
- **Key motivations:** want to be loved, to express feelings for others, to be needed and appreciated, to get others to respond to them, to vindicate their claims about themselves.
- **Examples listed:** Mother Teresa, Desmond Tutu, Eleanor Roosevelt, Barbara Bush, Leo Buscaglia, Luciano Pavarotti, Florence Nightingale, "Melanie Hamilton Wilkes" (*Gone with the Wind*), the "Tin Woodsman" (*The Wizard of Oz*), and the "Jewish Mother" stereotype (others in the text).

### Overview of the Two
- **Love defined.** Twos think of love as positive feelings, taking care of others, self-sacrifice, intimacy and closeness. The book's correction: at its highest love is "more closely aligned with realism than with feelings"; it wants the best for the other even at risk to the relationship; wants the beloved strong and independent even if the Two must withdraw; "Real love is never used to obtain from others what they would not freely give"; it outlives non-response, selfishness and mistakes; "If it can be [taken back], it is not love."
- **Central insight:** on the surface Twos offer love, but beneath they are searching for it; they believe that if they love enough, others will love them back; until they learn to love themselves, no response will make them feel loved.
- The Two's range of "love": from disinterested genuine love, to flattering effusions of "pleasers," to desperately needy manipulation and the "stalker."

**In the Feeling Triad (the Two).** Overexpress positive feelings and ignore negative ones; "love" with repayment attached; self-image invested in having only certain positive feelings. Healthy Twos are the most considerate and loving of all types; unhealthy ones deceive themselves about their needs and aggression and are "extremely selfish in the name of utter selflessness," doing terrible harm while believing themselves completely good.
- **Mechanism:** even average Twos struggle to see themselves as persons of mixed motives with personal needs, because their **superego** tells them that pursuing what they want directly is selfish and will be punished. So they must convince themselves they have no needs and see themselves only in positive terms, laying the groundwork for self-deception; they demand others accept their interpretation, even against plain facts.
- **Jung correlation:** the **extroverted feeling type**. The book quotes Jung's description of over-compensated, hollow, gushing displays ("The lady doth protest too much") that can flip to the opposite pronouncement, and reads it as the Two's ambivalence of feelings: healthy love genuine; average mixed; unhealthy, hatred fueled by resentment. The authors qualify that hatred is not just "a slight alteration" from love but that step by step, along the Levels, this is what happens.
- **Problems with Hostility and Identity.** Twos deny any hostile feelings, concealing aggression even from themselves; they act aggressively only when it can be justified as for another's good; fear that selfishness or aggression would contradict the virtuous self-image and drive others away. Their motivation (need to be loved) can deteriorate into desire to control; making others dependent breeds resentment; in conflict they feel "more sinned against than sinning," see themselves as martyrs; repressed resentments become severe psychosomatic complaints that force others to care for them. Core fear: "others would not love them unless they made others love them" (Twos characterized briefly as persons who, "fearing that they are unlovable, spend their lives trying to make people love them").
- **Irony:** unless healthy, the Two's attention is essentially on themselves, on the positive feelings about themselves reinforced by others' reactions. Average to unhealthy Twos look for very specific signs of affection and discount other signs; they must work out which kind of person to be to elicit the responses that "count." The identity problem: growing disparity between loving self-image and the needy person beneath. Remedy: recognize ignored needs, grief and shame and "apply their extraordinary nurturing skills to someone who desperately needs them—themselves."

**Parental Orientation (the Two).** Twos were **ambivalent to the protective-figure** (often the father but possibly the mother or an older sibling): the person responsible for guidance, structure and discipline ("patriarchal" qualities). They neither strongly identified nor fully separated; they fit into the family by taking a **complementary, "matriarchal" role** ("little nurturers"), believing nurturing the family would win the protective-figure's affection and protection. This sets the pattern for everyone who might give them love.
- Their **self-esteem is conditional**: contingent on being absolutely good and "unselfish" (the more dysfunctional the family, the more they feel they must sacrifice and repress needs). The more they see needs as selfish, the more indirect their means. The superego judges both the Two's selfishness and others' responses to the Two's help (example inner voice: "That was a nice thing Brenda said, but if you were really a lovable person, she would have given you a hug."). They try even harder to prove they are good, selfless, needless.
- Healthy Twos nurture themselves, know self-nurturance is "essential if they are going to be of any real help," and love without agenda. Lower on the continuum, "love" is "a veneer for the desire to create dependencies."

### Analyzing the Healthy Two

| Level | Name | Core |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Disinterested Altruist | Unconditional love, no strings; giving is a choice, not a compulsion; humble; radiant joy |
| 2 | The Caring Person | Empathetic; generosity of spirit; sees the good |
| 3 | The Nurturing Helper | Service; clear boundaries; the good parent archetype |
| 4 | The Effusive Friend | People-pleasing, intimacy talk, flattery |
| 5 | The Possessive "Intimate" | Envelops, meddles, needs to be needed |
| 6 | The Self-important "Saint" | Indispensable; vainglory; demands gratitude |
| 7 | The Self-Deceptive Manipulator | Guilt as tool; self-deception; pity replaces love |
| 8 | The Coercive Dominator | Entitled; belittling; volatile aggression |
| 9 | The Psychosomatic Victim | Illness as a bid for care |

**Level 1: The Disinterested Altruist.** Amazingly unselfish; unconditional, continuing love "with no strings attached"; free to love or not, others free to respond or not; others may grow away. It is "an immense privilege to be allowed to be a part of someone's life." Possible because Twos at this Level have learned to focus on their own real feelings and nurture themselves, so they no longer need to extract love; they can assess their own needs honestly and so see others' needs objectively ("sometimes the best thing they can do is to do nothing"). Unselfconscious about goodness ("let not your right hand know what your left is doing"); not angry if another takes the credit; delighted at others' fortune. "Disinterested" in the truest sense (no hidden self-interest). **Paradoxes:** the more they give to themselves the more they enjoy giving; more revered, more humble; more power given, less wanted; less love sought, more received. Virtue's enduring reward is happiness; very healthy Twos are among the most radiant people; they come as close to saints as anyone, too humble to think so.

**Level 2: The Caring Person.** The most empathetic type: feel with another as if the feelings were their own; weep with the grieving; the marital or job problems of friends touch them. Some loss of Level 1's freedom: attention shifts toward others and they start seeing themselves *as* people who have good feelings. Hearts rather than heads lead; they do not judge or keep strict accounts of right and wrong. They see themselves as good because they are; confidence rests in the value of the goodness they believe in. **Generosity of spirit** (not primarily material): charitable, positive interpretation, they do not find fault even when fault exists; "love the sinner, not the sin."

**Level 3: The Nurturing Helper.** Service is the keynote: feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, visiting the sick, philanthropic volunteering; going out of their way; extraordinary in crisis ("the kind of people you know you can call in the middle of the night"); generous with time, attention, money; sharing talents (singing, performing, cooking). **Clear boundaries:** they know physical and emotional limits and look after their own health and rest while looking after others'. Stimulating companions: good listeners with a genuine sense of fun. Their love is **particular**: others feel truly seen as individuals; they sincerely praise, uplift spirits, instill confidence, build self-esteem; they create "a climate of expectation which enables others to do wonderful things." **Archetype of the good parent** to everyone they meet; "the embodiment of the ideal of charity in action."

### Analyzing the Average Two

**Level 4: The Effusive Friend.** A "reverse gear" engages: attention shifts from doing real good to seeking reassurance of being loved. They equate love with intimacy and closeness, even where inappropriate; talk about the relationship ("Isn't it wonderful how close we are?"); friendly, talkative, sentimental, tactile (touching, hugging); regard acquaintances as friends instantly. **People pleasers:** appreciation turns into flattery meant to win appreciation back. They are "faultlessly well-intentioned" rather than good; a hidden ego inflation. **Religion** often plays a large part: sincere, but also congenial to their self-image, giving them a vocabulary and respected value system for love and sacrifice, making them "on the side of the angels" so few dare question their motives, appealing to secret wishes to be savior, miracle worker, rescuer ("killing the other person with kindness"); psychic abilities may likewise be "value-added" gifts. They cultivate friendships, encouraging confidences, wanting to be the "special friend" and confidant; their lavish praise is a source of power over those hungry for approval, but "not without cost."

**Level 5: The Possessive "Intimate."** They gather a circle of increasingly dependent people, an extended family or community with themselves at the center, making others feel indebted for being invited in. Like the "Jewish Mother" stereotype (applied to all religions and sexes), forever feeding people literally and emotionally. They seek out those who need them, but pick the needy and dysfunctional (addicts, the infirm, the emotionally wounded), who are least able to reciprocate. Fear the loved ones will love others more; look for ways to be needed. "Love becomes their excuse, their rationale, their every motive": their love is the solution to everybody's needs. They press attention on others, hover, interfere, give unrequested advice; invent needs to fulfill: "they need to be needed." They become busybodies (matchmaking, finding jobs, decorating advice), others experience meddling and withdraw (the very thing Twos fear). Intimate talk degrades to **gossip** that advertises their many close friends ("Let's talk about us"); pointed personal questions, one-sided information flow (they pry more than they reveal: they "do not have problems"). The smothering love "suffocates," yet because it is self-sacrificial the recipients cannot complain.
- Possessive, jealous, constantly "checking in"; do not introduce friends to each other; secretly like others' crises; cannot let go.
- **Specific responses only count:** a card will not do if what they want is a hug; they project ("You look like you could use a good hug") or simmer and find more ways to be "helpful"; their superego forbids asking directly; pride prevents admitting hurt. They "hold court," like being treated as a guru, want to be the "social switchboard." They keep up appearances of thoughtfulness superficially (birthdays, calls) but avoid tying to real needs so they can influence more people.
- **Overextension:** a problem with commitment; fickle, not by dropping people but by seeking love from yet another source; those who depend on them find them off helping someone else; committees, advice, burdened and worn out; **histrionic** qualities, dramatizing aches, hypochondria, breakdowns.
- Their acts are loaded with ulterior motives ("I was just doing it for you"); in effect **bribing others to love them**; "priming the pump" for a response means they never know if it would have come unprompted, creating a new anxiety about being appreciated for themselves.

**Level 6: The Self-important "Saint."** They feel they have done many good things and others take it for granted; ungrateful and thoughtless people "must be reminded of how good they are." Egocentric under a veneer of modesty, patronizing, indispensable, self-congratulatory; **vainglory** is the capital sin of average Twos: reminders of how many friends love them, name-dropping ("Imagine someone like me becoming friendly with someone like you!"). They are probably unaware of their pride; like to shine, to overhear themselves praised; others become "appendages to their egos." Pride blocks acknowledgment of resentments and suffering (they think admitting them would cause abandonment; actually others feel the hurt and are repelled by mixed signals). "The servant has become the master": they need an unending stream of gratitude, expect favors and repayment "in cash or kind," feel the beneficiary is forever in their debt, overvalue what they give and undervalue what they receive, take credit for everyone's successes ("You have me to thank for that").
- Still extremely needy and now **less discriminating**: pursuing any hint of attention, eager for any situation promising connection (every social get-together, long phone calls, possibly sexual escapades; sexual attraction becomes proof of lovability). They can resemble Sevens in scattered focus, but Twos run toward anything that might make them feel needed and loved (Sevens run from anxiety). People closest to them feel abandoned. Expectations too high; furious if others do less than hand over their lives; they "stuff" feelings with food, alcohol or prescription drugs; resentments smolder, prelude to manipulation, coercion and revenge.

### Analyzing the Unhealthy Two
A fall into the unhealthy Levels usually takes "a background of chronic abuse or a major catastrophe."

**Level 7: The Self-Deceptive Manipulator.** Aggression aroused but in conflict with the all-good self-image, so it is expressed indirectly by manipulation; the responses they manipulate never satisfy. Not feeling loved calls their whole value system into question; what passes for love is "extreme forms of codependency and desperate need." They are **"maestros of guilt"** who play others like an orchestra, play people against each other and against themselves, pulling them off their own center; they undermine while presenting as healers (prick with one hand, soothe with the other; put-downs followed by left-handed compliments; reopen old wounds then stitch them up): "one's best friend and, unwittingly, one's worst enemy." Compelled still to do things for others but too unhealthy to help; hypochondria (Level 6) becomes real illness used to elicit attention: **"In the unhealthy Two, pity comes to substitute for love."** Notoriously resistant to therapy; claim moral superiority; insist on purity of motives, which calls others' into question; religious rationalizations; they have turned "love, and do what you will" into a license. Underneath: tremendous suppressed rage and a fragile ego; terror of being left alone and fury at those they "love." **Self-deception** is the defense mechanism; they rationalize their entire lives, not individual acts: they are "helpless victims"; likely have been seriously victimized; desperate to hold onto any connection, even painful. Level 7 Twos are bitter and in pain; aggression still primarily psychological.

**Level 8: The Coercive Dominator.** [note: the source heading reads "Coercive Domintor," a typo.] Possessiveness becomes coercively demanding love on neurotic terms: a delusional **sense of entitlement**; everyone owes them because of past sacrifices. Nearly hysterical fear of being unloved; they no longer have energy to maintain selflessness ("tired of being selfless"); the ego makes demands "with a vengeance." Reckless pursuit of emotional gratification; often a highly dysfunctional or abusive childhood; they compulsively recreate whatever connection they had with their protective-figure (abusive, violent or neglectful); promiscuity, destructive affairs, sexual acting out. Deep resentments surface: **belittling people "in the name of love,"** to faces and behind backs "for their own good"; punishing by withdrawing love ("Well, you can just try to get along without me!"); dire predictions ("You're going to fall right on your face without me"); the veneer of love drops; torrents of complaints about how they were treated, their health and how unappreciated they are; endlessly dredging up past help ("Remember what I did for you? Is this the thanks I get?"). Their complaints bring the wrong kind of attention (others' resentment), feeding the vicious circle; they feel mistreatment justifies any action ("If one judges love by most of its results, it is closer to hatred than friendship."—La Rochefoucauld).
- The book states that some forms of pedophilia and child molestation may have roots here and that Twos "as a group" may "figure disproportionately" in such behavior (they often hold trusted roles: teachers, clergy, daycare workers, nurses); neurotic Twos lacking peer intimacy may turn to children, whose helplessness attracts them; they can comfort the child they terrorized, "playing the role of savior once again." (Content note: this is the book's own stated claim; sensitive material.)

**Level 9: The Psychosomatic Victim.** Illness is the unconscious route to care: "being cared for is not the same as being loved, [but] it may be as close to love as they are going to get." They unconsciously desire to "go to pieces," to escape responsibility for words and deeds and for the hypocrisy their aggressions revealed; physical suffering "proves" their claims (selfless, victimized, worn out). The strain of contradictions and a toxic superego produce breakdown. **Hysterical conversion reaction:** anxiety converted into physical symptoms: skin eruptions, gastrointestinal problems, arthritis, high blood pressure; invalidism becomes a way of life. Not masochistic enjoyment: the suffering is real; they enjoy its benefits. The book quotes Horney (*Neurosis and Human Growth*): suffering put in the service of claims is felt more intensely and leads to exaggeration, not mere pretense. Physical suffering is also a permanent guilt-instilling rebuke. "The 'saint' has become a drain on everyone."

### Dynamics of the Two

**Direction of Disintegration: the Two goes to Eight (from Level 4 down).** A way of acting out the repressed negative and aggressive feelings when ego defenses fail.
- Level 4: direct and blunt, "cut to the chase," shrewd and pragmatic; work harder; concerned about family survival needs.
- Level 5: bluffing, boasting, big promises; gossip and name-dropping meant to say "I hope you are aware of how important I am in your life"; swaggering, domineering; starting to throw weight around.
- Level 6: patronizing and self-importance collapse; flagrantly aggressive and controlling; threats; undermining others' confidence to keep them; pretenses of love vanish and rage and betrayal are exposed (Twos rationalize and forget; others do not).
- Level 7: failure to "keep the lid on"; frightening tempers; may strike or scream abusively.
- Level 8: even sweetness gone; releasing aggressions, going after what they want with entitlement; "relentless and brutal"; feel powerful, justified, unstoppable.
- Level 9: unconscious breakdown has been adaptive because incapacity removes the possibility of violence; but if they recover, a move to Eight can erupt; hatred toward those who have not loved enough; "A deeply neurotic Two at Eight can become physically violent, even murderous"; family at greatest risk; "The invalid... the suffering saint becomes a monster, sacrificing others."

**Direction of Integration: the Two goes to Four.** Contact with feelings, especially aggressive ones; self-knowledge; accept negative feelings as fully as positive (without acting on them); emotional honesty with the full range of emotions; **unconditional self-acceptance** as they accept others; give something deeper and more personal; they can be loved "for who they are" not for what they do; harness fuller feelings into **creativity**; more self-aware, reflective, intuitive of the human condition, whether as artists, parents or friends.

### Major Subtypes of the Two (wings)
- **Two with a One-wing: "The Servant."** Both Two and One are strongly superego-oriented, so heightened **altruism**; tension between Two's emotional, interpersonal, histrionic traits and One's rational, impersonal, self-controlled ones. Strives for love through goodness and selfless service; added circumspection, severity, duty; muted interpersonal display; can be mistaken for Six. Examples: Mother Teresa, Eleanor Roosevelt, Desmond Tutu, Danny Thomas, Alan Alda, Ann Landers, Florence Nightingale, Lewis Carroll, "Melanie Hamilton Wilkes," "Jean Brodie." *Healthy:* teaching, improving lives, working for causes; found charities and religious and philanthropic organizations; seriousness of purpose; fine teachers and encouraging parents; simple, functional personal style. *Average:* tension between personalism and idealism; judgmental; difficulty saying no; controlling of self and others; wants importance yet awkward drawing attention; more guilt and self-condemnation; more trouble asking for what they want. *Unhealthy:* self-righteous, inflexible, moralistic; cannot be proved wrong or selfish; deny aggression; hypochondria and psychosomatic disorders.
- **Two with a Three-wing: "The Host/Hostess."** Traits reinforce each other; both relate easily to people; adds charm, "personality," adaptability; seeks love through intimacy and personal connection; "more seductive" side; relationships central. Examples: Luciano Pavarotti, Barbara Bush, Barry Manilow, Richard Simmons, Sammy Davis Jr., Leo Buscaglia, Kathy Bates, Doug Henning, Tommy Tune, John Denver, Pat Boone, Lillian Carter. *Healthy:* charming, friendly, outgoing, self-assured, wholesome self-enjoyment; free-spirited worldly attitude confusable with the Seven's joie de vivre; "gift giver" more than "servant" (cooking, entertaining, singing, listening); social qualities valued over moral or intellectual ones; less task-driven, less self-critical. *Average:* wants an image of outstanding warmth; Twos validate goodness, Threes desirability (physical attractiveness, sexual desirability); hard-working, wants tangible signs of success; excessive friendliness, "cuteness," exaggerated sentimentality; flattery and gossip; right friends, name-dropping; self-important and narcissistic though masked; more direct about wants but draws attention to services; fears humiliation and loss of status more than guilt. *Unhealthy:* manipulative, exploitative, deceptive, entitled; hostility all-consuming; "beneath their apparent charm lies viciousness"; potentially psychopathic; emotional obsession, stalking, ruin what they cannot have; pathological jealousy, violent crimes of passion.

### Some Final Thoughts (Two)
Conflict between the desire to love and the need to be loved; "to paraphrase Othello, average to unhealthy Twos have loved neither too wisely nor too well." Unhealthy Twos bring about what they fear (wanting love, ending up hated). A "darkly comic" irony: the only person attracted to caring for an invalid, neurotic Two may be another Two, leading to "a macabre dance of death." Lesson: Twos are right about the value of love but wrong in their manner of loving; "As soon as ego masquerades behind love, love becomes tainted and eventually corrupt."

## Chapter 5: Type Three, The Motivator [s.15]

### The Three in Profile
- **Healthy:** self-assured, energetic, high self-esteem; adaptable, well-adjusted, charming, often attractive and popular; realistic and purposeful; ambitious to improve themselves ("be all that they can be"), often outstanding, "a kind of human ideal," motivating others to be like them; high-spirited, goal-oriented, persistent, effective and industrious. **At their best:** inner-directed and authentic ("everything they seem to be"), accept limitations, self-deprecatory humor and childlike innocence; charitable, genuinely modest, benevolent.
- **Average:** highly concerned with performance, being superior; compare with others for status; driven careerists and social climbers; "winners"; image-conscious, present themselves according to others' expectations; pragmatic and efficient but "studied," losing touch with feelings beneath a smooth façade (problems with intimacy, credibility, expediency); self-promotion, narcissistic with grandiose inflated notions; exhibitionistic and seductive ("Look at me!"); arrogance and contempt as a defense against jealousy.
- **Unhealthy:** fearing failure and humiliation, misrepresent themselves; unprincipled, covetous of others' success, "whatever it takes"; exploitative, opportunistic, deceptive; pathological lying, extreme hostility, delusional jealousy, betraying and sabotaging; vindictive, ruin what they cannot have; obsessive about destroying reminders of their shortcomings; psychopathic tendencies, murder.
- **Key motivations:** want to feel valuable and worthwhile, to be affirmed, to distinguish themselves, to have attention, to be admired, to impress others.
- **Examples listed:** Bill Clinton, Christopher Reeve, Richard Gere, Shirley MacLaine, Paul McCartney, Sting, Tom Cruise, Tony Robbins, Denzel Washington, Arnold Schwarzenegger, Truman Capote, O.J. Simpson, and others.

### Overview of the Three
- **Cultural diagnosis:** the book argues the United States is "fast becoming a dysfunctional 'Three' culture": driven, narcissistic, image-oriented, style over substance, symbols over reality; everything packaged and marketed; politics as public relations; television and "show biz" values ("the package is the message"); self-promotion and exhibitionism acceptable; "dress for success"; "if you manage your image properly, you too can become a star—or a god."
- **The Three exemplifies the search for validation of the self:** looks to esteemed others to determine who to be; becomes successful by achieving what peers value; applies equally "in a Buddhist monastery in Thailand" and "a fast-track corporate culture." In an unhealthy society that manipulates such fears, Threes gain the most attention and success but are among its greatest victims: "estranged from their own heart's desire, empty, and emotionally isolated."

**In the Feeling Triad (the Three).** The **primary** type of the Triad, the **most out of touch with emotional life**; they put their own feelings and desires aside in order to function; believe they will be valued only for what they do; habitually create good impressions. Over time they have trouble accessing real feelings: "a profound split develops between who they seem to be and who they are"; "their image becomes their only reality"; no amount of praise helps because the affirmation responds to an image. The great challenge: to become **inner-directed**. Many Threes do not know the dreams they pursue are not their own.
- Healthy Threes are admired and even idolized, but feel valuable because in touch with their own heart; self-esteem is real and unaffected by opinion; warm and genuine; "human nature's stars." Average Threes "do not feel self-esteem": they believe they will feel good only by achieving, being "number one in the class"; intensely competitive; look outside to find what a winner needs; project images; calculating; as emptiness grows they hype themselves, but attention "cannot touch them." Unhealthy Threes deceive themselves and others to preserve the illusion of superiority; devious when facing exposure; terrified anyone will see their disorder; dangerous to those perceived as threatening the image.
- **Problems with Hostility and Narcissism.** Hostility in less healthy Threes is **more directly** expressed than in Twos and Fours (arrogant distancing, snide humor, sarcastic putdowns, sabotage, betrayal); it compensates for inadequacy and keeps away people who undermine self-esteem (even those they admire or are attracted to). Average Threes are **the most narcissistic type**: in love with their inflated image, not their actual selves; internal "pep talks"; narcissists care about others only as they reflect on themselves, limited empathy, little capacity for lasting mutual relationships ("both parties are in love with the same person: the Three"); competitive with those whose admiration they want; show off to a presumed adoring audience; give "attitude" if no applause; demand admiration from those they hold in contempt. Narcissism is not self-esteem: it rests on capturing attention, not developing capacity; finely attuned to reactions; projecting whatever image the moment needs, with no corresponding reality ("being 'politically correct'"). Behind the façade is a hidden **dependency** on others they cannot acknowledge: "they cannot live with people and they cannot live without them."

**Parental Orientation (the Three).** Threes were **connected to the nurturing-figure** (the person who mirrored, cared for, and gave affection and a sense of personal value; usually mother or mother-substitute but may be father, sibling, nanny or grandparent). Young Threes are highly adaptable and tune in to the nurturer's unspoken hopes. If the nurturer is reasonably healthy, the child matures with good self-esteem; to the degree the nurturer has unresolved narcissistic needs, the child must abandon themselves to be approved; in pathological cases must disconnect from own feelings almost entirely; the expectations are unrealistic so the child is doomed to fail, leaving "deep narcissistic wounds and an intense underlying hostility." As adults they seek validation from **specific esteemed individuals** (not everyone), are highly vulnerable to rejection, work tirelessly to avoid being a "loser," and seek "the admiring gaze" they once sought from the nurturer; without it they feel empty and hostile. Because they were valued for accomplishment rather than themselves: effective work habits but **fear of intimacy**; they may end relationships before others know them well; believe others love only their image and would reject the real person beneath; "It seldom occurs to them that the most important person who has rejected them is themselves." Giving up the performance feels enormously risky; success without being themselves; the book notes tragic stories of highly successful people who contradict their image in startling ways.

### Analyzing the Healthy Three

| Level | Name | Core |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Authentic Person | Inner-directed; self-accepting; benevolent |
| 2 | The Self-Assured Person | Adaptive; reads others; "can do"; attractive |
| 3 | The Outstanding Paragon | Self-development; models cultural values; motivates others |
| 4 | The Competitive Status-Seeker | Compares; careerist; networking |
| 5 | The Image-Conscious Pragmatist | Self-presentation; commodity; emotional hollowness |
| 6 | The Self-Promoting Narcissist | Grandiosity; exhibitionism; contempt |
| 7 | The Dishonest Opportunist | Lies to protect image; exploitation |
| 8 | The Malicious Deceiver | Pathological lying; sabotage |
| 9 | The Vindictive Psychopath | Vindictive triumph; violence |

**Level 1: The Authentic Person.** Transcend the desire to be affirmed; accept themselves; become **inner-directed and self-generating**, planting "the seeds of their own interiority." Being inner-directed (rare) is not being led by emotional reactions, fantasies, avoidance, others' opinions or prevailing tastes, but following "the truth of one's own heart." Direct, authentic self-expression, deep feeling without sentimentality, "childlike simplicity and compassion." **Self-acceptance** defined as viewing oneself compassionately without condemnation or justification; it does not mean celebrating neuroses; it means loving oneself enough to accept painful truths; the experience of oneself "here and now, as a complete human being." Modest, direct, deeply contented, disarming self-humor ("wise to their own tricks and can laugh at them"); tender, loving, attentive listeners; image and reality converge; feelings present rather than postponed; able to take others' love in. Become benevolent and charitable out of real concern, feeling part of "the greater human family."

**Level 2: The Self-Assured Person.** More typically healthy Threes look outward to find what others value and adapt to become a person with those qualities. This follows from succumbing to their **Basic Fear** of being worthless without value in themselves (similar to the Two's dilemma). They develop skill at reading expectations. **Exquisite social instincts**: respond to attention "the way leaves turn their faces to catch sunlight"; sense a room's mood on entering; put others at ease. A "feedback loop" of attention and adaptation sustained by really embodying admired qualities and giving genuine affirmation. Their self-esteem "requires maintenance": self-affirmation ("I am a valuable, significant person"). They focus on **unlimited potential**, a "can do" attitude ("You can be anything you want to be if you set your mind to it"); the family "hero" (athletic star, straight A's, lead in the school play); tempered by realism and steadiness of purpose. Attractive in the broad sense: magnetism, an aura of desirability; attractiveness serves species propagation and social need.

**Level 3: The Outstanding Paragon.** Positive feelings about themselves reinforce each other; they fear disappointing others; invest in development (academic, physical, cultural, professional, intellectual); ambitious to **make more of themselves**, not for money or fame. They embody values their own culture admires ("living models of the culture's values, the paragons by which we see and assess ourselves"; the Olympics, West Point, medical school). Motivate others: they teach dancing, share gym tips, help others into the market. At work: capable, goal-focused, see projects through, build team morale, persist, effective communicators and spokespersons. Energetic, youthful, "like healthy animals frisking in the sunlight"; self-mocking humor; in great social demand. "Almost everyone would like to be a healthy Three."

### Analyzing the Average Three
**Level 4: The Competitive Status-Seeker.** Shift: they want to **be noticed** and distinguished; compare themselves; fear being overshadowed; subtle competition; want to show superiority through hard work and **symbols of success** (diplomas, raises, a prestigious address, a recording contract, a special position with a teacher or guru). "Type A" and workaholic. The same pattern in a homemaker ("model home," outstanding children) or a Buddhist monk (most holy of his brothers). They create rivalries where none existed (even though they need the positive regard of those rivals). More directed by others' values, hence growing **estrangement from own desires**; feelings become a distraction. Three highest values: **career, success, recognition.** Success means being number one, a "winner," constantly improving status ("the world of the résumé and the 'rat race'"). Careerists, willing to sacrifice marriage, family, friends and emotional health; prestigious title reinforces self; unemployment threatens self-esteem doubly. They learn **diplomacy**, which starts a move away from authentic expression: masking motives, communicating to produce a response; networking, cultivating people, sizing others up by "prestige value" ("How can you help me achieve my goal?"); status consciousness ("keeping up with the Joneses"); goals distort from self-improvement to winning recognition.

**Level 5: The Image-Conscious Pragmatist.** A significant stage: from here down Threes are interested in improving their **self-presentation, their image**, not genuine self-improvement; **"Style over substance."** Fear of rejection makes them abandon themselves in search of the "right combination" that makes them acceptable; polished veneer; they become "less desirable as genuine human beings and more desirable as commodities." The book quotes **Erich Fromm's marketing orientation** (*Man for Himself*): experience of oneself as a commodity valued by exchange value; success depends on how well one sells oneself; "the very changeability of attitudes is the only permanent quality"; a quality is "only a role, the pretense of a quality, to be readily exchanged." They obsess over impressions, feel every eye is on them, and have increasing difficulty knowing their own feelings. They act on the image's needs, project one simulated emotional state after another (sincere, friendly, modest, repentant, virtuous) with "slickness" and "emotional hollowness." Advertising, fashion and image consultants exploit this; the **chameleon** analogy; the image can become the standard others judge themselves by. Subtle and hard to detect even in intelligent, educated Threes; not limited to game-show hosts or "cloned yuppies": found in every profession. The **clue is apparent perfection**: a rehearsed, studied quality; "almost nothing 'essential'"; no idiosyncrasies or passion beneath; "perfectly engineered machines." They fear genuine intimacy lest their inner emptiness be found; may create an impression of intimacy; concern with **credibility**. Disconnection from feelings makes them efficient and pragmatic ("what works"), unconstrained by principles or turbulent emotions; when feelings arise they feel out of control, emote quickly and strongly, and want to get "back to business." They can take either side of an issue and switch with ease; rely on techniques, formulas, jargon; master manipulators of symbols (elect a president, sell a toothpaste). Not good leaders (no personal vision or values), yet drawn to leadership for prestige; "they lead by following, by telling people what they want to hear rather than what they need to do."

**Level 6: The Self-Promoting Narcissist.** Fear of exposure drives them to "up the ante" with self-promotion: they want others to admire and envy them, to forget the needy inner self, "to become their image." Grotesquely inflated self-image; relentless advertising of themselves, bragging, name-dropping, hype ("the great success they are about to have"); oversell; claims about a "glorious self which does not exist." Everything is "Look at me!"; shameless braggarts about culture, education, status, bodies, spouses, sexual conquests. Self-centered conversation bores people. Overt competitiveness yields conflicts, trouble with peers; comfortable only when superior. **Fame** is the ultimate success (being known by many proves they are not "nobodies"). They promote **status symbols** and **exclusivity**, becoming arbiters of who is "in" and "out"; status is "one-upmanship played by those who idolize success." Contempt shields self-esteem and ensures rejection does not matter. Physical attractiveness matters most: poses, "attitude," exhibitionistic, seductive (hypermasculine or hyperfeminine displays); those not attractive substitute intelligence, money, fame. **Narcissism is essentially passive**: they want to be the object of desire; rest on laurels, coast on charm; drop conquests; frustrate those who get close ("You can look, but you can't touch"), fear of intimacy against need for affirmation. Pretensions ("My discovery will win the Nobel Prize."); easily slighted exactly when narcissism is most inflated; hostile, sarcastic, contemptuous; put people down "under the guise of being honest"; treat friends shabbily. Beneath: disappointment and rage. The way back: get in touch with shame and self-alienation; otherwise risk severing the connection with who they really are.

### Analyzing the Unhealthy Three
**Level 7: The Dishonest Opportunist.** Failure is the most humiliating prospect; to avoid being seen as a loser, they deceive. Pragmatism degenerates into **unprincipled expediency**; since they are completely identified with the image, ensuring its survival means survival; they lie on résumés, plagiarize, cut corners, take credit for others' work, recount accomplishments that never occurred; sell out, lie, change loyalties. Cut off from conscience and discernment of truth, so little guilt. Exploitation is needed to maintain the inflated image; they are also **exploiting themselves** ("sell themselves out," sacrificing health, careers, integrity for narcissistic "fixes"): hustlers, "people on the make." Others become **"narcissistic suppliers"**; nothing reciprocal; a tip-off is the lack of long-term relationships, a staggering turnover of friends; no real goal-orientation. **"It is axiomatic that Threes always seem to be healthier than they really are,"** which prevents them from getting help. A catastrophe may expose inner desolation as depressive breakdown, which they hide.

**Level 8: The Malicious Deceiver.** Afraid of exposure, they support false claims with more destructive activity while covering their tracks ("False face must hide what the false heart doth know."—Macbeth). **Pathological liars**, compulsive about making an impression; unable to tell what the truth is; lies about the immediate objective; terror and panic beneath a calm surface; remorseless, capable of betraying friends, playing people off each other, destroying evidence; sabotage others, since the downfall of others is the only way to feel superior ("It is not enough that I succeed, but that others must fail."—Oscar Wilde); crimes entangle; may descend into "complete maliciousness and madness."

**Level 9: The Vindictive Psychopath.** Fear that falseness, emptiness and wrongdoing will be exposed; others' success mocks them; move from deception to "unmitigated vindictiveness" ("I will triumph over you no matter what it takes!"). The book cites Horney (*Neurosis and Human Growth*) on the **vindictive triumph**: mostly unconscious impulses to frustrate, outwit or defeat others, hidden behind frantic ambition, the need "to defeat and humiliate others by rising above them." Hostility becomes irrational malice from **extreme jealousy**; anyone authentic or capable of feeling or loving is a threat. Others fear confronting them. They can be utterly **psychopathic**: acting out cruelest revenge fantasies; dissociation may leave no memory of the act; assault, arson, sabotage, murder "with as little remorse as normal people have in killing a fly"; even random-seeming violence (a sniper) has a motive of regaining superiority. The book says male psychopaths' victims are frequently women and links this to Threes' identification with mothers, and suggests that reaching Level 9 almost certainly involves incredible childhood abuse (nothing won the nurturing-figure's approval; rage displaced onto substitutes; rape, torture, sexual mutilation named as frequent outcomes). They may trip themselves up from a need for victims to know who did it, and **notoriety** affirms they are "somebody." (Content note: dark, as the book states it.)

### Dynamics of the Three
**Direction of Disintegration: the Three goes to Nine (from Level 4 down).** A way of disengaging from relentless activity.
- Level 4: when competitiveness shows, become conciliatory and apologetic; lower profile, conform to group norms.
- Level 5: stress of denying own desires leads to disengaged attention, busywork and routine; strangely unresponsive and unfocused; complacency.
- Level 6: self-promotion exhausting; after career setbacks, collapse into apathy, aimlessness, listless resignation, wishful thinking and fantasies of the next big success; drinking and substance abuse.
- Level 7: deception and unethical or illegal acts to keep the illusion; stress of the ruse leads to deep apathy and depression; ineffectual, unable to perform basic tasks; can last months or years rather than falling to Level 8 or 9.
- Level 8: covering up misdeeds; collapses reveal distress; regression to childhood triumphs or traumas; alternation of depressive, dissociative periods and hysterical outbursts; they restore the image whenever anyone is present.
- Level 9: full of rage; when the horror of compulsive behaviors registers or others discover them, they go to Nine: a psychotic-like dream world, everything unreal; dissociation from hostile feelings so completely they feel "absolutely nothing"; "deadness," flatness; depersonalization, weight gain, vegetative state, possible break from reality (fracturing into subpersonalities, catatonia-like state).

**Direction of Integration: the Three goes to Six.** Frightening: commitment exposes them to rejection and intimacy threatens the belief that only image and accomplishment are valuable. Healthy Threes commit to something or someone outside themselves and discover their value is not diminished; "by their commitment to something outside themselves, they paradoxically begin to grow as persons within themselves"; solid values take root; commitment lets them show real feelings and vulnerability and explore fears (often within a committed relationship; a spiritual conversion may help); helped by falling in love with someone clearly more developed with whom they do not compete. They stop impressing; use talents to affirm others' value; serve causes that bring no direct praise; find real self-esteem in cooperation; commitment gives more affirmation than competition; their image drops away and "their real self emerges."

### Major Subtypes of the Three (wings)
- **Three with a Two-wing: "The Star."** Traits reinforce each other; extraordinary social skills; enjoy being the center of attention; charming, sociable, popular; emphasis on personal touch and presentation; reaching out. Examples: Bill Clinton, Prince Andrew, Elvis Presley, Paul McCartney, Brooke Shields, Jane Pauley, Whitney Houston, Denzel Washington, Shirley MacLaine, Jack Kemp, Kathie Lee Gifford, Dick Clark, Vanna White, Tony Robbins, Joe Montana, Sylvester Stallone, Arnold Schwarzenegger, O.J. Simpson, "Hedda Gabler," "Lady Macbeth." *Healthy:* more outwardly emotional and friendly than Threes with a Four-wing; perky, vivacious (can resemble the Seven); talkative, helpful, generous like Twos while poised; want to be loved as well as noticed. *Average:* energetically reach out to engage; heightened ability to project feelings or "the illusion of feelings" (actors, models, singers; "turn it on"); want the spouse to be "a catch" who reflects well on them; children, home, hobbies, vacation spots as narcissistic extensions; narcissism more open than the Four-wing subtype; excessive "charms," exhibitionism, seductiveness; may court even negative publicity; unaware of resentment toward those who do not respond to their "sacrifices." *Unhealthy:* deceptive and self-deceptive; manipulative and entitled; hostile when not on top; jealousy; rage may surprise even themselves; "charming psychopaths" who turn violent against those closest.
- **Three with a Four-wing: "The Professional."** Complex, conflicting traits (Three interpersonal, Four withdrawn); more focus on work, achievement and recognition than interpersonal skills; can seem quiet, private, subdued, with artistic interests; more emotionally vulnerable but more restrained. Examples: Jimmy Carter, George Stephanopoulos, Sting, Richard Gere, Barbra Streisand, Meryl Streep, Tom Cruise, Michael Tilson Thomas, Dick Cavett, Bryant Gumbel, Truman Capote, Andy Warhol, Somerset Maugham, "Iago." *Healthy:* some intuition directed at self and others; more potential for self-knowledge; artistic sensibility, strong sense of style; emphasize intelligence over attractiveness; honing craft, technical mastery (can be mistyped as Five or One); self-assured yet introspective and sensitive. *Average:* the Three's competitiveness plus the Four's self-doubt create tremendous pressure; self-esteem rides on specific others' reactions to each project; still suppress feelings; the Four-wing adds self-accusation; failure is "truly terrifying"; moodier, pretentious, aloof; "subtle showoffs." *Unhealthy:* alternate wildly between ego-inflation and self-doubt; depressions with emotional and physical shutdown; repeated return to old routines until burnout, life changes or pathology; may be misidentified as manic-depressive (underlying issue is narcissism not anxiety); possible self-destruction and suicide if constantly frustrated.

### Some Final Thoughts (Three)
Threes abandon themselves, rejecting their heart's desire; develop only parts of themselves "like bodybuilders" focusing on one aspect; healthy qualities are real but "parts, not the whole"; average Threes overdevelop an insubstantial part, the image; unhealthy Threes sacrifice true selves and integrity. They bring about what they fear: cut off from "the only source of true value a human being can have, their own inner life." "They are more and more dependent on others to affirm their value when there is progressively less and less of value about them to affirm." Yet when Threes "brave their fears of worthlessness, stop the performance, and reveal the more simple, vulnerable human beings they really are, they become profoundly affecting and powerful."
