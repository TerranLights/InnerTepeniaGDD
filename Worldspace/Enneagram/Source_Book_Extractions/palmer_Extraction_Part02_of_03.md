## Overview of this range

Palmer's method here is the oral-tradition panel: people of one Enneagram type describe their own inner life, and in the Directory, couples and work pairs describe each other. Each type chapter in Part II follows a fixed template (Worldview, Spiritual Path, Concerns, Personality Bias, Subtype Focus, Focal Issues, Security and Risk (the "dynamics of change" arrows), "X in Love" (Living with X, Intimacy, Signals sent: positive, negative, mixed, inner), "X at Work" (In the Workplace, Leadership Style, Classic Conflicts, Conflict Resolution, Employee Participation, Team Building)). Part III then takes every pair of types (each pair described once, as a couple of peers and as a manager-employee work partnership) and insists that no pairing is "best"; a psychologically mature partner is the best match, and the useful information is the predictable friction, projection and repair for each pairing. Her recurring teaching moves: every type has a "veil of illusion" that comes on under pressure; a partner can help by specific behaviors; "one works on change while the other works on acceptance."

# PART II (continued): THE PERSONALITY TYPES IN LOVE & WORK

## SIX, THE TROOPER (end of chapter: Six at Work) [pp.185-189]

*(Chapter continues from the previous part. This range picks up inside "Six at Work.")*

### Workplace: the success-tension [p.185]
- A Six has trouble locating the source of tension tied to success. Voices: "Is it that my subordinates do not like authority?" "Am I sensing a behind-the-scenes attack? Is a takeover likely?" "Why don't I feel the pleasure of a win?"

### Leadership style [pp.185-186]
- Sixes "come alive under adversity" (company headed for bankruptcy, the quarterback down fourteen with two minutes left). Doubt disappears when you can measure yourself against a known difficulty instead of an imagined worst case; focused action releases reserves of strength; fear is "largely in the mind."
- When conditions ripen for a win, vitality and interest drop; procrastination appears without opposition; overpreparation, delayed decisions, several courses of action, a wish for certainty.
- **Honest feedback to Sixes must include moderate, intelligent opposition** (which part fell flat; who criticized what). Untempered compliments do not sound genuine; others do not sound astute unless they have something constructive to say.
- Sixes are ambivalent about visible success; they rally around underdog causes; their belief is sparked by focusing others' faith (teaching strengthens their convictions). They lead well when the cause is defined by obstacles. Remove the obstacles and "suddenly you're visible"; being an authority can be shocking.
- To stay in leadership: keep underdog momentum; reliable follow-through system; steady feedback from a respected mentor to cut through doubt attacks; air all negative expectations before turning to positive alternatives; set expansion plans in motion inside a structure that cannot be changed so the success-to-expansion transition is protected; the Six then returns to a troubleshooter position.
- Upside of doubt: keeps a leader honest; successful Sixes don't inflate; they see themselves as having mastered fears; like Ones, they focus on flaws and refine; great endurance; no need for immediate gratification.

### Classic conflicts [pp.186-187]
- "Yes, but ..." ("Have you considered ...?" "What about the other side?") when enthusiasm is high; like One perfectionism it is perceived as negativistic. Remedy: ground rules (a brainstorm period where anything goes, then equal time for troubleshooting).
- Antiauthoritarianism: some Sixes hate being told what to do; they do well on a team with non-threatening people who can hear them out without agreeing; it diminishes with safety and a success or two behind them.
- Conflict resolution: see Part III (the Directory).

### Employee participation [pp.187-188]
- Sixes are "one of us" or rebels. **Loyalist:** stays safe by being dutiful and pleasing. **Rebel:** uses an aggressive style to find out who is safe; provocative; challenges the status quo "just to see where everyone stands." Any overture of friendship reduces anxiety; known quantities are safe; "Troopers want to know what you're thinking."
- Security is key: "What will happen in the future?" They respond to clear guidelines, clear penalties and a clear chain of command free of favoritism. Creative and cooperative if ideas and efforts are recognized; they can postpone gratification; unusually loyal when management is loyal.
- Safety lies in full information. Bad news is better than doctored or withheld news; error is forgiven when known, but secrecy "feels like manipulation" and makes Sixes rebellious. Any unequal power arrangement triggers fear.
- Daily competition with colleagues is a lose-lose that triggers paranoia: guilty about winning, bad if not doing their best. Some quit promising careers over it. **Controlled competition** (known adversary, ground rules, time to prepare; e.g., trial lawyers, a short, strictly monitored aggressive contest after which parties part) is good territory.

### Team building [pp.188-189]
- Sixes focus heavily on interpersonal relations; being liked means fitting in, finding agreement, being on the same side. "Intense interaction among a few people can be the ultimate reality check. Paranoia dies when there's certainty."
- Public recognition: a Six who is a star works best if applauded in that area and otherwise treated as an equal; a Six in difficulty should be encouraged privately and not exposed to public ridicule. Next to aggressive stars, the Six either challenges them to equalize power or starts an "us against them" alliance.
- Preferred team shapes: team against management; team competing as a unit against other teams; a team of noncompetitors who get along by agreement. Worst: a team of competitors seeing each other daily.
- Natural team players given a consistent environment and secure future; weak in follow-through; highly creative, good at initiating and troubleshooting; do not flourish in uncertainty. Turnoffs: mixed messages, covert alliances, competing for attention. Inner questions: "Why put out good ideas that are going to be ripped off?" "Why let yourself be exploited?" "Who can I trust?" Creative loyal players, or, if disadvantaged, disruptive.

## SEVEN, THE EPICURE [pp.191-213]

### Chapter summary box [p.191]
| Item | Seven |
|---|---|
| Personality bias | mental: planning; emotional: gluttony |
| Essence qualities | work; sobriety |
| Subtype focus | sexual: **Suggestible** (fascination); social: **Sacrificial**; self-survival: **Family Defender** |

- **Worldview:** "The world is full of opportunity and options. I look forward to the future." [p.191]
- **Spiritual path:** "Work" here means essence as single-pointed attention, the ability to shift inward and tell inner from outer reality. If the mind is fascinated by the delights of outer life, spiritual work degrades into "pleasant planning and gluttony for life experience." "Sobriety" is a return to essence through moderation, concentration and commitment. [pp.191-192]

### Concerns (the author's list, compressed) [pp.192-193]
Seeks stimulation and peak experiences; avoids limitation; keeps the energy up and leaves the party "while it's still interesting"; joy of the mind (options, plans, images); replaces painful feelings with positive alternatives; **gluttony acted out in three areas** (suggestible in one-to-one; sacrificial in obligations to others/groups; family defender in belonging to like-minded people); confuses ideas and actualities ("Mentally it's already done"); self-focus, does not notice others' needs or pain; overvalues own ability ("I can do anything," "People think well of me"), narcissistic; **equalizes authority** ("I'm the same as authority"); "We're all equal" really means "You do your thing and I'll do mine"; superior if unchallenged, punctured and inferior if challenged, then uses charm to regain superiority; avoids conflict with authority, "goes through the cracks"; diffuses fear by charm ("Sevens are fear types who move forward into friendly contact with people"); chicanery, fakes it; masks fear with boredom ("This feels limiting," "People are limited"); angry when ability questioned, terrified when charm fails; values spontaneity and open-ended agreements; likes beginnings and planning, procrastinates final commitment; interrelates and systematizes information, which yields either commitments with loopholes and rationalized escapism, or **nonlinear analysis, remote associations, synthesis of different systems**.

### Personality bias [pp.193-194]
- The Seven's world is full of options; "the real pleasure is mental"; the mind hungers for experience and anticipates the future. Childhood pleasures are remembered, bad times fade. Movement into pleasure is movement away from pain; Sevens are "the Enneagram's optimists."
- **Gluttony = "a banquet of experience":** stuff the schedule, fill the mind with plans; disappointments barely surface. Sustained by inner worth they move through life with little awareness of others' pain.
- If entitlement becomes automatic, self-observation stops: over-positive spin, blurring potentials and facts; being told what to do is irritating; limitations are "the product of small-minded thinking"; "it feels like dying when your options shrink."
- **Growth:** staying instead of leaving, dealing with pain; "stay for one more minute instead of going away." Helped by significants who accept both pleasure and pain, notice friends' needs, see when interests spread, and set a framework for emotional depth.

### Subtype focus: gluttony in sexual, social and survival concerns [pp.194-196]
| Subtype | Palmer's description |
|---|---|
| **Suggestibility / Fascination** (sexual, one-to-one) | Charm is the instrument. "The first sparkling hit is the best"; rush of initial attraction ("This is it!"). Riveted by brief encounters and people's stories; the discovery phase is delightful; outer directed, nonjudgmental, likable; people tell them their dreams. They "fall right into your picture" (shared imagination feels like intimacy; what sounds like suggestion sounds like fact: "You've been to Hawaii? ... It's a place I'd visit"). "The Don Juans and Juanitas of the Enneagram." Often unaware of impact; sincerely interested, then it is time to move on. In long monogamous marriages they report feeling limited by one relationship; commitment is "the C word," associated with pain: "This is it?" |
| **Social Sacrifice** (social) | Group interests reflect an idealized social order; they sacrifice immediate pleasure for a future dream (cause, commune, church). Gluttony touches the social arena through like-minded affinity groups mirroring their inner worth. See strength in numbers but feel martyred by others' blundering ("You can't go solo in a group enterprise, but you wish that you could"); "hostage to their peers." **Equalizing authority:** no leaders, no followers; they eliminate authority by making everyone the authority; an egalitarian structure guarantees personal freedom and spares them empathy for different needs; they hate being dragged along on someone else's idea of a good time. |
| **Survival through a Family of Like-Minded Defenders** (self-survival) | A chosen family of people who appreciate the same possibilities, not the family of birth; "defenders of the dream." Act like "old-fashioned circuit riders," visiting friends to see how projects are going; secure when every sector of a future life plan (gardener, traveler, families with children, doctor, dancer, mystic, priest) is flourishing; pick up conversations months later as if no time had passed. |

### Focal issues [pp.197-201]
| Term | What Palmer says |
|---|---|
| **Options** | Stay excited and joyous; long-standing personal interests (music, running, chess); lists and plans made "not to follow or to do them" but to see and have many options. Multiple options are insurance against feeling stuck; something pleasurable placed on the far side of anything unpleasant. |
| **Positive imagining** | The imagination sets you up for disappointment; reality is a crushing comparison; when ideas become fact, alarm at the gap between ideal and actual; impatience with real accomplishment: "What's next?" |
| **Procrastination** | Five, Six and Seven are "the Enneagram's fear types"; hallmarks: ambivalence about authority and procrastination. Sevens procrastinate by taking on too much (a good idea sets off a tree of associations; "Why was this meeting called?" "We're back at the beginning," "I forget."). You weren't there. You would have done the same."). Think of themselves as sensitive to feelings, but people who disagree seem limited. "Someone who loves me would want what I want." Pleasure should be idyllic, intimate, supportive; guilt rarely enters; whatever is done with love in the heart "ought to be OK." |

### Security and risk (dynamics of change, point Seven) [pp.202-203]
| Arrow | Description |
|---|---|
| **Security: toward Five** | Sevens "never turn into Fives"; withdrawal appears in a Seven-like way. Pressed for commitment they withdraw to weigh options; look contracted and miserly. Even a good, secure choice causes a crisis of choice because it feels limiting next to imagination. "Sevens never look angrier or more depressed than when they have to make a strategic choice." Commitment feels like giving away all other options. ### Seven in love [pp.203-207]
**Living with Sevens (list, compressed)** [pp.203-204]
- "The main problem is getting a Seven to see the problem." Ideal mate: someone who adores the Seven and keeps company during the good time.
- Wants high stimulation, adventure, multiple options; diffuses disagreement and sweetens ("Shouldn't we go to dinner and a show?"); wants partners who mirror their high self-image.
- Pleasant when admired; when challenged or placed in an inferior position they "ridicule or discount you or the situation": "They make nice or make fun of."
- Acutely sensitive to boredom and repetition; adopt new interests to keep the spark alive; "go with the flow," cycle in and out of encounters, arrive on a high note, leave with good feeling, return when the flow brings you together. Angry when the flow is interrupted by "someone of lesser mind."
- Aware of limitation when commitment is called for; can be committed for decades and uneasy with the concept; commitment is "a process." Multidimensional intimacy: fascinated by your various aspects, will do many things with you and support your dreams.

**Intimacy** [pp.204-206]
- Upbeat, independent, many interests; mental lists ("That's an interesting possibility," "Let's try to fit that in"), fluid priorities; if an activity sags, move on; boredom means "wrong approach"; "the escape route from difficulty lies in the direction of pleasure."
- Cannot sit and feel bad; mind goes to a positive option on the mental list (the possibility is enough, doing it is not necessary). When a partner insists on discussing negative impact, they feel forced to stay with something unpleasant; if trapped, very angry: "It feels like I'm fighting for my life. My freedom is being taken away."
- Many solutions and exits before anger; "verbally slippery." "Sevens can bend your mind": you aren't wrong, you just don't get it; caught with a hand in the cookie jar, they accuse you of a small-minded attitude toward cookies (reframing the definition of terms until the cookies fall through the cracks).
- Young Sevens define their own reality: commit to a process but not its specifics; redefine key words (e.g., authority as "knows something about" rather than "masters in," so beginners who know slightly more than those around them are authorities).
- Spontaneity is essential; decisions made at the last moment because they treasure the possibility of changing their minds; intimacy as spontaneity of shared imagination, "brainstorming the possibilities of a full life together."
- **Strategic choices** are painful: choosing shows you can't have it all; the mind fixates on the disappearing option ("I just lost a piece of the dream"); a choice doesn't feel final "even when you're on the plane." Limiting choices are met with grudging acceptance, then anger (pointing the finger: "It's your fault," "You got me into this mess") or depression; being limited feels demeaning and like being trapped; accusations are "a statement of struggle." One more minute could open the door to failure.
- Young Sevens want to experience everything; many reach capacity later; dissatisfaction can be a good sign ("I'm repeating myself," "I know how this story turns out"). Crisis surrounds committing with real dedication; a "sober" or single-minded Seven keeps the pleasant diversions of a complex mind but stays steadfast in the painful phases. Helped by significants who note signs of rationalized avoidance, bring attention back to the present, and welcome "negative" emotions when appropriate.

**Signals Sevens send** [pp.207-208]
| Signal | Content |
|---|---|
| Positive | Playful, imaginative, inventive; daily tasks infused with pleasure; optimistic visions encourage significants to accept full potential; the ordinary made extravagant (a park walk becomes a convergence of nature cycles). |
| Negative | Narcissistic preoccupation with personal agendas makes them seem uncaring or unreliable; the partner begins to feel inferior (they are open-minded, you are narrow and attached; your anger or depression marks you a lesser being); when threatened they take a superior stance. |
| Mixed messages | A multioptional lifestyle sends many messages. They may make apparently opposing commitments and feel internally consistent: a series of loves is one learning process with one high purpose, so "no one was abandoned and no one was chosen" and the first affair can be re-engaged. The message to significants: "I'm here when I want to be, and I will go without feeling bad." Coping: focus on the couple's permanent needs (raising a family, a deep shared interest); attention will cycle in and out but they are loyal to a genuine commitment. |
| Inner signals | Hard to tell a genuine direction from a tangent; attention jumps to what must be sacrificed; decision stalls; thoughts: "I'm being pushed into this. This is for someone else's benefit. I'm being held back"; feelings: "I may be losing the opportunity of a lifetime"; stance: "People should be committed to the process of their own development ... The specifics are incidental." Outsiders see paralysis. |

### Seven at work [pp.208-213]
**In the workplace (list)** [pp.208-209]: offers a "sweet solution" to authority (equalize it, as fair peers or by engineering that no one may give orders); insists on impractical ideas; prefers theory to implementation; opens a task to new approaches rather than face routine; goes through the cracks instead of confronting, broadening definitions to get around rules; excellent in open-ended projects (networks, plans, synthesizes); measures self against others ("Am I superior or inferior? Do I stand above or below?"); positive self-image punctured by negative feedback; bends minds to win support (reframes objections, puffs possibilities, convincing generalities with loopholes, "suggestions that sound like promises"); delightful, forgiving, creative in hard times, "the office person who wins the popularity poll."

**Leadership style** [pp.209-210]
- Strength: correlating different kinds of information into a coherent pattern; positive future visioning; coordination points among systems; ingenuity, invention, thinking "ahead of the pack." Verbal, convincing, voices the aspirations of a project's people; best in fast-changing, planning- and synthesis-heavy environments; best in early phases; planners not implementers; several projects at once ("a book for every room in the house").
- Weak points: chaotic decisions; delegate right after the plan is positioned and relieve of repetitive duties; priorities shift, staff pick up the pieces; schedule changes unrelated to business needs; conflicting directions, lack of supervision; "A crisis occurs and the Seven leader goes on vacation." Unless informed they assume things are moving; may make the bearer of bad news guilty; find faults in others when trapped; hard to see own mistakes ("The plan looked flawless. If it went astray, then someone who carried out the plan must have blundered"); they rationalize, reinterpret, recontext, reframe.
- Palmer's note on managers of a Seven: success depends on steady follow-through and ending impractical directions; under a Seven boss a manager's own type becomes more pronounced (a One more rigid, a Two more other-referenced, a Three more assertive). [p.210]

**Classic conflicts** [p.211]: short-term gains from escaping limits invite long-term pain of missed opportunities ("no pain, no gain"); equalizing authority and unwillingness to accept actual level of ability cause conflict; repeating the same mistakes ("I've been here before"); avoid conflict and criticism that could tarnish self-image; classic conflict is exaggerated, promissory plans ending in poor follow-through, results rationalized ("The results are really OK. The results were what we should have expected"); trouble in the nitty-gritty; accused of not carrying their load.

**Employee participation** [pp.211-212]: fun to work with; process over product; excitement from the directions that arise from a single idea; what authority says is secondary; peer acceptance matters ("That was great ... I really enjoyed working with you"); self-approving, self-motivated early on; trend toward equalizing authority leads to difficulty understanding organizational limits; "seem compliant while actually bending the rules." Equalizing authority as natural expression of freedom: "Someone who's the same as me won't tell me what to do," "Someone who's the same as me will want the same things that I want"; like-minded people as "independent, emotionally autonomous mirrors." Quick studies, like fast tasks with many options and a general scheme, learn on their feet, less effective in routine.

**Team building** [pp.212-213]: ensure creative ideas and awareness of new developments; liaisons with other groups; good representatives who put the product in a positive light; move quickly from idea to action; useful risk takers; ingenious around obstacles. Difficulties: insisting an untenable approach is stubborn; obstinate about routine; completion phase may trigger brilliant new possibilities; insistence that possibilities are responsible lines of investigation; splitting attention. Helps if they can voice difficulties and ask for help. **Sign of commitment:** they begin to complain, fight for what they believe in, and do not have to be the life of the party.

## EIGHT, THE BOSS [pp.215-237]

### Chapter summary box [p.215]
| Item | Eight |
|---|---|
| Personality bias | mental: vengeance; emotional: lust (excess) |
| Essence quality | truth |
| Subtype focus | sexual: **Possessive/Surrendering**; social: **Seeking Friendship**; self-survival: **Satisfactory Survival** |

- **Worldview:** "The world is an unjust place. I defend the innocent." [p.215]
- **Spiritual path:** the preoccupation with justice points to a search for truth; if undivided truth prevailed, control would be unnecessary. The Eight child saw truth distorted and innocence betrayed, learned innocence is read as weakness and the strong dominate the weak; confrontation fueled vengeance and mobilized power, energy and lust for satisfaction. Eights confuse subjective needs with objective truth; keeping a personal power base is mistaken for protective acts that mimic service to objective truth; it is "a child's attempt both to ensure fair treatment and to protect a connection to the undivided truth of essence." [pp.215-216]

### Concerns (compressed) [p.216]
Justice and fair use of power; avoids weakness; controls possessions, space, territory; excess is a life stance, easy access to energy and anger; "If something's good, let's go for it"; lust acted out as possessive/surrendering tactics (one-to-one), friendship (social), satisfactory survival (self-preservation); "My way or the highway"; intolerance of difference masks fear of being disadvantaged; "larger than life: too much, too loud, too many"; sets boundaries, sees others as controlling, acts in self-defense; sees rules as controlling and tests limits and consequences; confuses self-serving versions of truth with objective justice; softer emotions and dependency needs appear only when safe; all-or-nothing attention (fair or not fair, warrior or wimp), leading either to denial of personal weakness or to "an ability to exercise appropriate force in the service of others."

### Personality bias [pp.217-218]
- Everyone enters the Eight perspective "when we see the truth with complete certainty and act accordingly." Mind stops questioning; "we find ourselves in motion before we know what we will do." "When the truth is at stake, we couldn't draw back if we tried."
- Eights were respected for strength in childhood; assertion valued over compliance; leaders needed rather than rules. Respect earned through power teaches control of feelings: "You can't be vulnerable and invincible at the same time"; no room for tenderness, fear or regret on the line of battle; the aim is to control the territory and get there first.
- **Lust develops from a battle mentality:** know what you stand for and who stands against you; test comrades' loyalty; protect perimeter, supplies, information; entrench in a small, secure area in a protracted war and conserve strength "to blast out at a moment's notice." The energy switch is on or off. A larger-than-life demand to be seen and heard; urgency to get more of whatever is vital and good "and to get it first."
- If top-dog works, you forget impact: forget to consult, inform or get consent; objections sound stupid, obstacles incidental; energy comes on with thoughts of being disadvantaged. When this is automatic, self-observation stops: "You're the only one left on the field after the war is won."
- **Growth:** questioning ideas of justice, hearing the other side, learning to wait; the choice appears when secure enough to relax position and watch yourself escalate. Helped by significants who stick to their own version of truth, hold ground under fire, deal fairly, and model power in appropriate service to others.

### Subtype focus: lust (excess) in sexual, social and survival behavior [pp.218-221]
| Subtype | Palmer's description |
|---|---|
| **Possession/Surrender** (sexual, one-to-one) | Possessive attitude toward intimates and friends; all secrets shared; want to know all, advise, be consulted, take part in decisions; often take charge of a loved one's life. "One of the highest energy positions on the diagram"; lusty in love, commanding in business; quick to stave off encroachment; seen as relentless competitors. The **control struggle** is part of the pleasure ("It's the struggle that's interesting. Winning may erase interest"); it tests strength, honesty and protectiveness, which underlie commitment. Possession = access to the mate's body, mind and spirit whenever the occasion arises. **Surrender** = fully possessing a mate's loyalty and affection once certain the mate will not put you at a disadvantage. |
| **Social Friendship** | Friendship as tested alliance; a friend can hold ground and assert themselves honorably, is a safe channel of information, does not exploit weakness or misuse private information; feelings come out within a close circle; Eights can try on viewpoints through a friend. Often loners seen as testy, so social invitations and affinity groups are welcome. A full-scale quest for friendship: booked time, commandeered volleyball court, "legendary stamina for carousing," marathon talks from baseball to Zen. Camaraderie: you can go full out because limits are tested; generous with time and attention. |
| **Satisfactory Survival** (self-survival) | Territorial control over space, belongings, a steady supply of creature comforts; security system, neighborhood watch, checking every window and door; "survivalists" with a personal bunker; "just enough is the most satisfying"; supper, cat and current book within reach. Fear being deprived or "out in the rain alone"; elaborate supply systems (food clubs, laundry, a hardware store that stocks everything; knows where to get ten kinds of pizza). Comfort preoccupation ("Where is my pen? My favorite saucepan?"): a mislaid shoe can feel like invasion, a domino effect; don't put their shoes in the closet or sort their mail; carry layers; collect eatery information; triple-check tickets and hotel. |

### Focal issues [pp.221-225]
| Term | What Palmer says |
|---|---|
| **Innocence** | A quality of higher being: open, undefended awareness outside personality; children go openly toward what pleases them and know how much they need. Eights were such innocents and learned to close down, be savvy and aggressive, "a good opponent." |
| **Anger** | The emotion of choice; flares quickly, easily expressed; hard to have a relationship with someone "you can't push up against"; anyone interesting enough will be a target; it takes years to understand impact; Eights feel relieved by truth expressed while effects on others are devastating; "Anger makes you feel better, but it can ruin your partnerships." Eights feel defeated if they can't say what they think ("mentally write letters"); being cut off on the freeway or having a price beaten down is taken personally; once expressed it dissipates; they can resume friendship right after a fight ("Where did everyone go?"). |
| **Control** | If truth comes out in a fight, they are fascinated by how people handle aggression (sneaky? withhold? act behind your back? fair? punish? manipulate? collapse?). They are more interested in predicting conduct in a fight than winning; may run a small "stress test"; if you pass, it is safe to be friends. |
| **Boundaries** | Alert to manipulation, passive aggression and withholding, which seem as aggressive as honest anger; meet covert hostility with direct confrontation; to people who contain anger by other defenses this looks horrifying, so Eights are misperceived as violent; they are distressed that they appear physically dangerous ("Why? I've never hit anybody!"). A soft approach and a quiet voice build trust. **Partners help by calling tactics:** "Fight fair," "Don't punish," "This is hurting me," and agreeing to talk it through at a set time ("I will come back and talk in an hour"). |
| **Excess** | Craving for satisfaction; unaware of consequences or feedback once something desirable is in mind; obstacles seem minimal. Hard to feel pleasure when overstimulated (numbing coupled with overcharged desire); famous high pain threshold; don't feel a stimulant until "under the table." Substitutes seem boring; no limit until over it; feel in control during excess (driving fast, eating, making intense demands); part of the fun is seeing how far you can go without being affected. |
| **Self-forgetting** | A Boss "hardly ever goes first": more reactive than proactive (challenging a known position, refining a procedure). The 8-9-1 triad is "the Enneagram's place of self-forgetting" with Nine at its core: Eights lose priorities through excess and express anger to defend what they want; Nines merge with many points of view and express anger indirectly; Ones replace priorities with "the correct thing to do." Eights' emotional needs come from a deeper, more submissive nature; they suppress softer feelings for survival and find it hard to remember what really matters. [p.224] |
| **Justice** | "Do Eights take justice into their own hands? Will they seek revenge?" The revenge motive is evening the score; it probably won't be acted out but feels good to think about. Compromise feels like showing the throat. Eights can be mollified by a face-saving solution that saves pride on both sides; an honorable opponent does not invite retaliation: "Set clear rules and consequences. Do not humiliate the loser. Be fair." **Revenge has active and passive components:** passive = doctoring or withholding information (the adversary need not know); active = rationalized as justice. [pp.224-225] |

### Security and risk (dynamics of change, point Eight) [pp.225-227]
| Arrow | Description |
|---|---|
| **Security: toward Two** | Trust develops through toe-to-toe confrontation; once trust is gained defenses relax and the submissive side develops; grateful when someone is attracted to them; as "Giver strategy" they can be excessively generous, romantic, attentive, then suddenly withdraw support ("aggressive giving and taking makes it quite clear who's in control"). "Overtly possessive and secretly sentimental." There is a close connection of anger and lust: the sensation of anger is pleasant, powerful, cleansing; an intimate moment that can turn sexual; they like the contact of fighting, sex and adventure with people they love. Keep the horizon big enough to engage an Eight; with a mutual interest moving against softens into cooperation; friendships outlast initial attraction. **High Two:** support rather than aggression. **Low Two:** manipulating partners' affections through strategic generosity; giving used to control. |
| **Risk: toward Five** | Stress first intensifies Eight defenses; then a withdrawn, private Five stance: closed door, little communication, introspection; they reappear when the issue is manageable, often without explanation; some Eights love hours alone on intellectual pursuits and could pass for Fives. **High Five:** self-awareness, observing themselves and others for hidden motives; "very powerful" because insights can be tested in real life. **Low Five:** psychological denial acted out physically; shutdown, wall staring without insight, waiting out a bad time. |

### Eight in love [pp.227-232]
**Living with Eights (list, compressed)** [pp.227-228]
- Likes independent, strong partners; fighting, sex and adventure as ways of making contact; deep joy in sexuality, willing to match partner's intensity.
- Late hours, heavy entertainment, binges ("too much, too loud, too many"); all-or-nothing, so the partner may have to balance life areas.
- **Strict control followed by disobedience** is a demonstration of power: first make the rules, then break them to stimulate interest when bored.
- Need to predict your intentions; fear of being controlled displaces into territorial control of schedules, objects, space; cannot tolerate ambiguity or lack of information, so small oversights read as betrayal.
- With softer emotions they may deny feelings by withdrawing, claiming boredom, or self-blame for past misdeeds. Rarely allow themselves to be hurt; if hurt, they want to manipulate circumstances to get back, thoughts of revenge forestalling vulnerability. Rallying points and "towers of strength in dangerous times."

**Intimacy** [pp.228-229]
- Main concern is personal freedom (too many rules, bureaucrats, "brain-dead drivers"; traffic as a small instance of their predicament). "Much of their seemingly dominant behavior is in fact an effort to make sure nobody dominates them." They will work until they drop for a loved one's interests, especially when not told to; being told, or love conditioned on good behavior, provokes resistance; even rules in their own interest are hard to obey.
- Convinced dependency means powerlessness, they can mistake tenderness for dependency; "Sex can be moving without being intimate"; compromise is weakness; acceptance equated with fear. Extremely direct: you know what they want and like because they tell you. "Happy Eights bring the party with them," generous with time, talk and energy; assume others have the same energy.
- Gut-based, act in their own interests, seldom question their own motives ("Doesn't everyone act from self-interest?"); may be inadvertently selfish. "You have to question Eights bluntly."
- **Bored Eights are trouble:** won't submit to a dull evening, can't be charmed or bribed; slouch, yawn, point to watches ("Can't we leave yet?"). Solution: independence; leave the door open and do your own thing.
- Relating includes confrontation: conflict is a test of power; anger can erupt over trivia; fights can draw closer ("Are you fair? Are you truthful? Do you cave in or stand up for yourself?"). Like Twos, Eights in relationship are sensitive to rejection. Helped by significants who are bluntly honest, stand firm in an argument, do not manipulate, withhold information, or make love contingent on good behavior.

**Signals Eights send** [pp.230-232]
| Signal | Content |
|---|---|
| Positive | Incredible support; want to empower people, test and trust; excitement and intensity ("plug into the juice"); courageous, determined, persistent, fair-minded, truthful, unpretentious: "What you see is what you get." |
| Negative | Defend excess by denial and anger, often with counteraccusation ("I'm not a controlling person, but you are"); take over through intimidation and intrusion; transgress boundaries when a misdirected sense of justice violates rights and property; punish loved ones and, when denial lifts, punish themselves without mercy. |
| Mixed messages | Seem like two people: a tough persona covering a soft heart; the **Eight-Two crossbrace** is a conflict between going against and moving toward people; "An emotional Giver is protected by the domineering Boss." Want to protect loved ones and need to protect themselves from tender feelings. Partners are confused: "Are you loving or angry? Are you with me or against me?" Answer: both. Utterly open and sensitive when denial lifts, but punitive at the least hint of disadvantage. |
| Inner signals | Like Nines, energy follows inertia (body at rest/in motion), an all-or-nothing switch; a "physical red alert" makes action and anger indistinguishable and certainty impossible to question; inaction would be foolish. No tolerance for ambiguity or mixed messages; an uneasy feeling triggers a search for the source: "I feel uneasy. Who's the responsible party?" "Why should I have to feel uncomfortable? This feels unfair." They become provocative to discover why they feel threatened ("What are you hiding from me? ... Let's test you out!"); a fight discharges tension but the aftermath is shocking. **Inner task: recognize blame as a signal of vulnerability** ("Blame feels strong and certain, the opposite of weakness or doubt"). Thoughts: "Admit what you've done. Take responsibility and we can continue our friendship." Feelings: "Come on strong ... Don't show your throat." Maturity: "I might be making an assumption. Don't act prematurely. It's safe to wait." |

### Eight at work [pp.232-237]
**In the workplace (list)** [p.232]: controls office hierarchy, sets limits for self-protection ("Who's in charge? Is the leadership fair?"); sees compromise as weakness; assumes leadership, watches strong contenders, respects honest leadership, likes a worthy opponent; unwittingly polarizes people into factions and provokes to get clear answers; anger is direct, no hidden agenda, no grudges once expressed; "My way or the highway"; enforces rules that support personal advantage and bends the rest; demands full information (changes in details may suggest manipulation).

**Leadership style** [pp.232-234]
- "Eight and Three are the dominant American business styles." Direct, assertive; spearhead figures in a go-ahead or expansion phase; "We all line up behind a Boss when we need a plan of battle"; effective when feelings are polarized; armored against opponents' anger, calculate consequences as real damage rather than damage to fragile egos; centralize power over delegating; top-down reorganization (clearing out deadwood, hiring and firing, show of strength); protective of a hand-picked cadre of "my people."
- Weaker in consolidation: daily management lacks the excitement of open competition and the energy turns to trouble-making; they invest minor incidents with great importance, meddle in office politics, "displace anger" so small procedural matters trigger war. Redirect their attention to useful projects before trouble.
- **Working with Eights:** self-disclosure is the key; you need not agree but must make views known; do not soften, doctor or withhold information; don't act on your own initiative; consult when in doubt; constant reportage builds trust. "It's OK to bring bad news to this leader. Bad news can be handled, but no news is death." They hate being blindsided; blame is the first defense ("Who's responsible for this?" "I want a report") and shifts easily to contempt; inaction looks like admission of guilt.
- **If blamed:** assume the burden, document your position, don't make excuses or shift blame, take the spotlight, put your explanation on the table, define the perimeter of the problem, state recommendations and append a direct report system for personal checking.
- Delegation is difficult; they keep an eagle eye; observation of your work may be an unannounced test (do one piece well and the next may follow). **No news is good news:** praise is sparse; a spot check that generates no memo means full steam ahead; "We're doing better" may mean you've gone over the top; good news may arrive secondhand. [p.234]

**Classic conflicts** [pp.234-235]: "Unfair" and "unjust" are buzzwords; distinguish a personal gripe from an early warning of organizational discontent; often spokespersons for others' battles, cast as cranks because they disdain delicacy of presentation; ambiguity and mixed messages are red flags and they push for clarity; "classic conflict is fight technique": they fight to win; sensing deceit they can go to character assassination; disgruntled Eights go over your head ("Who do you report to? What's the grievance procedure? Who do you know that I know?"); they escalate when you expect concession (compromise feels like capitulation; mediation is "for wimps"); want a clear avenue of rewards and punishments and push the limit to see if you enforce consequences; key phrase "It looks like I lost but I got concessions."

**Employee participation** [pp.235-236]: "Eights are really the boss even though you have the title." Developed Eights are natural leaders; immature ones are "workplace disasters." Focus on the power structure; practical, think in livelihood, security and benefits; want fair exchange; mature Eights shield the "innocents" of the organization (good communication with management; can also be a platform for personal power). **Immature Eights:** antiauthoritarian, lead by intimidation, fixated on inequalities of status, money and power, illusions of invincibility, excess and self-abuse (denial of physical limits, burnout, self-defeating behavior). Disgruntled Eights work the system (slowdown, walkout, petition, strike). Do not be ambiguous with recalcitrant Eights: "set limits and ... enforce penalties. This is one position on the Enneagram where boundaries and penalties can improve participation."

**Team building** [pp.236-237]: dedicated competitors, work until they drop when interested, embrace difficulty, reach key positions by sheer presence. Notoriously low tolerance for meetings ("Another form for us to deal with?"); control details when out of control on larger issues; "State your business, give your opinion, and get out." Paradoxically hurt if left out of social events ("They may not come, but they must be invited"); a weekly Thank God It's Friday lunch; "For Eights, friendship equals safety." Give them a designated area; solo performers who take over without realizing; set clear boundaries of responsibility; territorial about shared space, equipment or information; dead-ahead once in motion with little attention to new developments. To get cooperation: clear objectives, incentives, who gets credit; excellent support players in a fair arena; do not put them at a disadvantage or make them prove themselves. High-decibel discussion may be just thinking out loud; types who retract from anger back down or confer behind their back; "A show of interest is an excellent sign" (they won't bring enthusiasm unless invested); prodding and urging cover vulnerability.

## NINE, THE MEDIATOR [pp.239-261]

### Chapter summary box [p.239]
| Item | Nine |
|---|---|
| Personality bias | mental: indolent (self-forgetting); emotional: sloth (laziness) |
| Essence qualities | love; right action |
| Subtype focus | sexual: **Union**; social: **Participation**; self-survival: **Appetite** |

- **Worldview:** "The world won't value my efforts. Stay comfortable. Keep the peace." [p.239]
- **Spiritual path:** babies "are" essence, awareness permeated by love ("unconditional pleasure of pure being"); when personality formed, attention turned to momentary comforts and the child became indolent or forgetful of the self-perpetuating pleasure of essence. **Sloth is "an overaccommodation,"** a failure to initiate right action toward the essential features of life; merging with others' agendas mimics the comfort of union with environment and all beings. [pp.239-240]

### Concerns (compressed) [p.240]
Comfort of neutrality; avoids anger and conflict; replaces essential needs with unessential substitutes; ambivalent about personal decisions ("Do I agree or disagree?"; sees all sides); decisions are easy when not personally loaded (emergencies, political opinions); postpones loaded decisions by repeating a familiar solution; "Seems like there's endless time. Why act now?"; goes along; easier to know what you don't want than what you do; sloth acted out as **union**, **participation**, and **appetite for material interests**; can't say no, hard to separate or be the one to go; dampens physical energy and anger, diverts energy to trivia, delayed reaction time for anger, passive aggression ("Anger equals separation"); controls by going stubborn and waiting it out; attends to others' agendas, leading to difficulty forming a personal position but also "the ability to recognize and support what is essential to other people's lives."

### Personality bias [pp.240-242]
- Everyone enters the Nine perspective when feeling inseparable; boundaries drop; someone else's life becomes the motive for our own; "one skin and a single being."
- Nines felt overlooked in childhood; blended in; shelved their agenda "so they wouldn't be forgotten"; the tension between complying to be loved and defying to assert independence ("Do I agree and go along, or disagree and create conflict?"). Agreement feels like giving in but it is hard to say no; you see the worth of their position more easily than your own.
- **Sloth = laziness about life:** all sides overwhelm your agenda; attention cycles from central issue to secondary matters (chores, backlogs); momentum slows; "holding pattern"; primary energy siphoned to secondary pursuits; when shelving is automatic, self-observation stops and you forget yourself.
- **Growth:** paying attention, structuring own agenda, staying on track; the choice appears when you can watch yourself absorbing a new position. Helped by significants who encourage separate goals, give solid reinforcement for progress, and remind them of deeply held purposes; partners help them to express a position and move through the discomfort of separating and choosing.

### Subtype focus: sloth in sexual, social and survival attitudes [pp.242-243]
| Subtype | Palmer's description |
|---|---|
| **Union** (sexual, one-to-one) | Wants total merger; a beloved becomes the "focalizer for life." Union is "a mutual existence" rather than a separate individual; absorbs another's life; feelings of being overlooked vanish in an undivided state; moments of "we-ness," "We are the same being," "Who has this face?" Lazy about own direction, partner's effort becomes the reason for being; swept along by others' enthusiasm, merging with a partner's agenda unconsciously. Hates feeling discounted, likes being connected. |
| **Participation** (social) | Group activity as comforting inclusion and the place of greatest laziness: energy that could serve a personal agenda is shunted into activities. Defined activity supplies goals, procedures, timetable, predictable expenditure; gravitates toward groups with meetings and agendas permitting low-level participation, or leadership when energy is surplus; joins and attends regularly without full commitment; ambivalence is constant ("Do I belong here or not?"). |
| **Appetite** (self-survival) | Attention fixates on secondary gratifications (food, travel, TV, collecting); cutting off the substitute feels threatening because it is the crux of a holding pattern that spends surplus energy predictably; "voracious attachment to the replacement"; automatic comfort and temporary replacement for love; zoning out on food or mystery novels; couch potato with newspaper, chips and beer; shopping fever "doesn't feel lazy. It feels exhilarating" yet means lack of commitment to oneself. |

| **Security: toward Three** | Antidote to laziness is high-level energetic habits; secure Nines are in a productive routine, enjoying the same regard as a hardworking Three; agenda similar to Three (recognition for performance and task) but they also want to be loved for themselves and can tell task attention from real acceptance; operating at the high end of inertia; real concentration for a personal agenda; can tell automatic habit from authentic choice. **Low side:** start-stop syndrome; depend on deadlines and others' agendas; image in place but internally a struggle ("constantly fighting low energy ... reset, restart, and reengage"). ### Nine in love [pp.251-255]
**Living with Nines (list, compressed)** [pp.251-252]
- Once merged it is hard to separate; relationships continue years beyond the natural stopping point; hard to give up memories of old relationships so new ones can develop.
- Divert attention from feelings to unessentials; search for alternatives to forestall arguments; laconic ("Let the unspoken remain unsaid"); retreat into habit and trivia ("lots of little things to do"); energy spreads to the mechanics of living together (house repairs, mortgage rate). "As a Nine's partner, you will find yourself being the active agent for change."
- Say back what you want to hear without agreeing; your needs sound louder than theirs. Fantasize about merging with an ideal partner and being swept into a new life; the flip side is blaming you when things go wrong. Relationships deepen when the Nine can merge **"without any loss of personal identity."**

**Intimacy** [pp.252-254]
- Know others' wishes more clearly than their own; easy to step into someone else's shoes ("Why do things my way rather than your way?"); by weighing each side so carefully they lose a clear sense of their own position. Having a position means defending it; is it worth alienating loved ones? "It's easier to keep the peace." Time slips by; deadlines and family needs act as a wake-up call.
- **Participation may not indicate commitment:** may go through the motions; without a strong personal position they move with the flow; partners should draw out actual interest; with you present they may be buoyed by your eagerness, but alone the momentum dies. Indecisiveness challenges partners wanting leadership; "Should I, or shouldn't I?" Nines "fall into goals rather than make choices" (career, livelihood, residence): "this is happening to me" not "I choose." Forcing a choice is ineffective; **activate your own direction without requiring the Nine to join** ("Do your own thing"); set the example of a self-starter. They assert agenda covertly through complaint, "Not that, not that," charting a direction by elimination.
- **Embedding in a familiar activity eliminates choice:** a high-powered routine takes time and attention and leaves none to consider new directions; autopilot at high speed; advice: put decision-making on a timetable sandwiched between structured, familiar activities so energy doesn't die when asking what you want.
- Paradoxically they resist help in decision making; "comfortably certain about being uncertain"; fence-sitting is a base of operation; taking a position disrupts the equilibrium that keeps discomfort and conflict at bay. Coerced or pushed, they go stubborn and control through nonaction: "I've gone along with you as far as I can. This was your idea not mine. Now I don't like it, and it's your fault." Subtext: "I'm merged with you. Come up with something I like." Fear of separation and being on their own; a "fine line" between merging and feeling the life overlooked; chronically submerged, opinions muffled by louder voices. The most intimate task: supporting decisions that come from their own being. [p.254]

**Signals Nines send** [pp.255-256]
| Signal | Content |
|---|---|
| Positive | Support the lives of others as their own; caring, attentive, aware of what is essentially good about others; giving, compromising, adaptive, receptive; productive and happy on track; welcome a safe structure; "a bulwark in other people's lives"; nonjudgmental regard. |
| Negative | Passive aggressive, stubborn, missing the point, "out of it"; dispersed energy makes "everything" seem equally important so you feel unimportant; seem to agree when they may not; give what is familiar, not what you need; spacing out into TV, food, energy-draining habits. |
| Mixed messages | Secondary interests bury priorities; apparent full agreement may be a peripheral interest or not wanting trouble; **self-depreciation**; outer compliance with deep inner anger over missed opportunities. Overt: "It's not important." Covert: "I'm angry about being overlooked." Help by setting boundaries on your own interests and helping them refocus. |
| Inner signals | Confused by choosing; pulled into others' lives, then must extricate; go along, later resist; overinfluenced; thoughts: "I can see what she wants and why she wants it," "Blend in. Don't make waves. This will pass," "It's not worth it. Wait it out. The right time will come." Hard to get enthusiastic without nearing a stand and anger in your own defense; helps if significants note time is passing and meet regularly to remind of priorities. |

### Nine at work [pp.256-261]
**In the workplace (list)** [pp.256-257]: relaxes without friction; wants the "job family" to get along; flourishes with positive support but avoids self-promotion ("Wants recognition but will not ask"); wants procedures, lines of command and rewards well defined; "No sudden surprises, please"; can go on automatic and produce a great deal of work; energized by routine and others' enthusiasm; wants a structure to support decisions; goes by the book; cautious with risks and "avoids risks that raise hopes, for fear of disappointment"; forestalls decisions by gathering information; unessentials get done first; strategic deadlines give "magnificent last-minute saves"; overwhelmed by too much to do; ambivalent about authority (can't set priorities and get going, yet stubborn about taking directions); first expresses anger covertly by ignoring the problem or blaming the structure, mismanagement or others.

**Leadership style** [pp.257-258]
- Torn between points of view; examines alternatives at length; misses opportunities for precise directive leadership; global rather than specific goals; listens overlong, gathers too much information, apportions a little to everybody; reports come from every part and factions war if leadership doesn't narrow the vision; unwilling to fill others in until the plan is complete; assumes others share the global perspective; imprecise about carrying out the plan; do not move quickly; want to repeat the known once a workable solution is in place.
- Conflict is very difficult: they fill the breach rather than harass an employee, withdraw when clarity is most needed, and evasive action invites accusations of selling out. Works best with self-motivated employees who fill in specifics; fails where clarity or quick shifts are needed. Energy dies for new directions but is there for routine implementation; Palmer compares this mentality to utility companies and the U.S. Postal Service; "appropriate for the long haul"; administrative levels have this quality; "apportion a little bit to everybody and strive for consensus."

**Classic conflicts: two phases** [pp.258-260]
1. **Ambivalence:** hard to know where they stand; factions feel unheard, whoever gets the Nine's ear gets heard; Nines inadvertently create conflict by withholding information while pondering, then delivering the final decision without explanation (pink slips in the mail rather than difficult meetings); "by not saying anything, Nines hope to avoid saying no"; others feel neglected, angry and manipulated into being the aggressive force.
2. **Digging in:** passive-aggressive tactics (slowing, fulfilling only the job description: "I'll do only as much as I'm paid for"); if they cannot choose, the position is "I want it all. No compromise"; a stubborn Nine is "very slow and very angry," and can outwait the opposite side "sometimes for years."

**Employee participation** [pp.260-261]: respond to structure and clear incentives; sensitive to being overlooked and overshadowed by self-promoters; gravitate to systems that let them coast on known procedures; can take risks, decide and be creative inside an organizational system more easily than attend personal priorities; need to see advancement possible; "take on the shape of their environment" (internalize the climate of opinions, satisfactions, frustrations; merge with dissatisfaction as well); conflict is disruptive; attracted to benign environments; ambivalent about authority (want structure, stubborn about being told); a fair internalized structure "serves as a rudder"; agreeable if heard.

**Team building** [p.261]: natural team players if conflict is minimal; proud of group victories; feel another's triumph as their own; back a star who credits the team; reach high goals merged with others' ambitions; often more creative on a team than alone; "a project's glue," steady in difficulty because they stick to routine under pressure. In conflict their mediation capacity emerges; they become sounding boards, relay unifying facets of opinion, can become the hub of negotiations; sound them out when difficulties arise. They want consensus; when negotiations falter they become discouraged and see disagreement instead of unity; a neutral third party consulting them can draw out "a fountain of information."

# PART III: THE DIRECTORY OF RELATIONSHIPS [pp.263-323]

## Compassionate Understanding [pp.265-266]
- Two common questions: "Which types are best for me to relate to?" and "How can we mobilize the most successful team?" The "best match" approach (dating services, personnel questionnaires) is "usually less effective than it seems." On the job many learn to test well and act like high-performance Threes, with a mask; teams get stuck on misperceptions of each other.
- In intimacy, feelings about types are shaped by the few people who raised, loved or disliked us; we generalize one exemplar to everyone with that worldview.
- **Oral tradition panels:** a panel of one type reports radically different preferences toward another type. Example: Fours asked about Eights say "I marry them, I fear them, I love them, I hate them, I avoid them, or I compete with them"; all true from personal history with the good or bad side of a Boss.
- Conclusion: do not categorize types by compatibility; "Your best match in intimacy and team building is actually any psychologically mature person."
- The directory records "the most often reported findings from Enneagram students who practice self-observation": the concerns that emerge in any committed partnership, not the chemistry that bonds a team or enlivens a marriage.
- The compulsion or bias of type isn't always in force; under pressure the **"veil of illusion"** (bias of type) dominates perception.
- **Technique:** both partners work their side at once: "One works on change, while the other works on acceptance." (Threes shrink schedules; partners accept that Threes put their hearts in work.)

## Reading the Directory [pp.267-268]
- Interactions are presented without regard to gender and culture. The same mental and emotional habits are acted out differently by culture (a southern belle's Four-ness versus a corporate-trained Four). Palmer notes Argentina producing many males who favor an emotionally expressive side, and Gypsy culture valuing women's organizational strengths and earning power, reinforcing aggressive or "masculine" behavior; cultural overlay affects self-presentation but "not the basic preoccupations that underlie type."
- **The Work and Family Platform:** love and work are different expressions of the same outlook, so crossovers exist; each person shows only a few of the type's focal issues; which show at work or home is individual.
- Source: thousands of reports from oral-tradition classes; couples format has a panel of one type opposite spouses and significants; the conversation shows "typical projections, blunders, and transformations"; such events can be "the raw material, the compost, the energy source for personal transformation."
- **Format:** couples are described as peers; work interactions as manager-employee. Each pair appears once (One with Two, not Two with One).

## Interactions Between the Types

*Format in each entry: the couple, then the work partnership. Palmer's line/arrow references (security/risk points and wing points) are kept.*

### Double One: double Perfectionist [pp.268-272]
**Couple**
| Theme | Content |
|---|---|
| Lifestyle | Strive for a perfect lifestyle (food, health, child-raising); practical families built on responsibility and pride in earned achievement; projects bond them but "teamwork favors function over feeling"; "No time for hugs. Lots to do."; love shown through effort. |
| Criticism | They understand criticism: critical of a loved one = investment; criticism as a sign of trust, silent resentment as a sign of distrust. **Fight pattern:** slow buildup, short explosive tussle, long silent standoff, gradual reengagement as forgiveness takes place. |
| Safe fights | Anger about safe topics (neighborhood politics, saving the whales) opens the conduit for personal matters; long-standing One couples say deeper intimacy depends on fighting, "showing anger is an act of trust" ("We fought, we said everything that we were afraid to say, we still love each other, and nobody died"). |
| Difficulty | Outside projects pull both out; chores and children; mutual blame ("This isn't fair"); being dumped with unexpected duties is irritating to a dedicated worker; **a household job sheet** with clear division of labor helps (fair play). |
| Story | "The Party Will Be Late": a One husband (electrician, remodeler) and wife with a three-year-old, graduate theses, a garden; a birthday party requires furniture, which requires finishing the trim, then tile; both home constantly but see each other "mostly in passing." [pp.269-270] |
| Insecurity signals | "Who is better?" comparison from the childhood belief that being perfect earns love ("Am I good enough to be loved?"); "comparison attacks" signal insecurity; **"You go first!"** means "I'm afraid to initiate change/make a mistake," and can be a disguised compliment ("I'm afraid to fail in your eyes, because I'm not lovable unless I'm perfect"). |

**Work partnership**: Ones seek each other out (relief at another who cares about quality control, "another Atlas carrying the world on his back"; fun to debrief and pick apart presentations). **Liability:** a "liaison of superiority," an exclusive club that props itself up by putting others down. Positive: shared hard work, earned advancement, professionalism. Control issues: manager does "what's right" for the business, employee wants what's right for staff; manager's raise contingent on "continued good performance"; both feel right; withdrawing into stony nonproductive silence and the letter of the law; "each waits for the other to 'break' by showing anger"; helps to loosen the system so each saves face, respect for the loser. Mutual blind spot: detail control (small matters magnified into obstacles); decisions hard because both see all points but have trouble initiating; broad solutions have too much room for error; managers repeat what worked; outside counsel and a roster of consultants help; employees blank out with contingencies (performance anxiety) and need a rule for red alerts and memorized who/what/when/how.

### One with Two: Perfectionist-Giver [pp.272-274]
**Couple**: attraction of differences (practicalities versus feelings and style); One flattered by emotionally flamboyant Giver; Twos initiate (regardless of gender), cutting through perfectionist criticality and social anxiety; Ones' needs "shine like a beacon to Twos"; Ones are guilty about having needs, Twos aren't guilty about meeting them. Twos like steady, dependable mates who show love in responsible action; a One is an anchor in emotional turbulence. **Friction:** Ones work overtime, Twos push for "juice"; "Long days and no hugs make Perfectionists feel pure and drive Twos crazy"; from the Two side the One's pleasure in a job well done is "grim and punitive." **Meet at Four:** mutual understanding of disappointment and depression (Ones depressed when work fails; Twos saddened when secure because then they feel their needs); wish to shield each other from shame; "Ones respect people who struggle, and Twos are helpful when they see pain." Over time the shoulds deaden spontaneity; a threatened Two asks "Where do I stand?" "Don't I come first?" and fights for attention (pride inflates when the partner cares enough to react); "the Giver looks unstable and the One looks rigid"; "Ones impose structure to fend off chaos, and Twos fight the rules as a way of getting back." Ones under pressure bury themselves in work ("A stiff 'Good morning' over faultlessly buttered toast, no telephone call during the day, and lots of overtime"). Remedies: Twos should know work suppresses Ones' anger, take some responsibility, agree to reasonable guidelines; Ones should understand the need for attention and make gestures of affection, accept help ("Ones commonly feel criticized when help is offered"; they see offers as pure generosity or manipulation: "It's not either/or. It may be a little of both").

**Work partnership**: Givers work for love; if work doesn't win approval, they change jobs. They may collude to put One in charge and Two as helper ("power behind the throne"); One needs to be right, Two to be liked, so Twos are uneasy challenging Ones. One managers focus on skills and procedures, long-range planning, precise details, little attention to staff feelings; Giver employees notice but may not confront; best use shows Twos confident enough to challenge the manager's thinking. Two managers emphasize image, appearances and an inner circle of favorites; One employees dislike social posturing and favoritism, want a fair authority acknowledging merit unasked. One employees are threatened by manager's moods (Givers forget bursts of temper, Ones believe words spoken in anger and worry weeks later). Two manager repair: "I may have overreacted."

### One with Three: Perfectionist-Performer [pp.275-278]
**Couple**: energetically well matched (active, status and social image, identity through work; sports, family events, house projects). Weekend away helps because relating via activity slides into compulsive doing that blocks intimacy. **Different relating:** Ones review the day ("Listen to what happened to me today"); Threes relate to results and move on once the job is done. **Image:** both care what others think; Ones compare themselves and distinguish real accomplishment from image, "Ones rarely puff out their image"; Threes project an exciting public facade; Ones ask "Is this honest or deliberate deception?" **"Threes want to look good to people, while Ones want to look right."** Playing to the crowd raises fears of dishonesty elsewhere; Ones want absolute honesty; Threes may keep seeking the way to impress. **Fight:** Threes retreat when image is attacked, Ones pursue when angry (Ones put the focus of anger on the table; if Threes retreat Ones pick a fight about chores). Peril: Three unable to self-reflect and One in angry pursuit; Three should see themselves as 50 percent of the problem. Ones should stay on a single area; Threes dislike "negative" emotions and discussion spreading to past sins. Ones have **total grievance recall** ("You did it once and you might do it again. Have you really changed?"); Threes: "OK, I admitted I was wrong. I paid my dues. Now why can't you forgive and forget?" Threes should deal with Ones' anger early, while attached to a specific issue; discussions must be scheduled, not evaded by pleading work; Ones should remember facades may be a different way of meeting the world rather than a lie.

**Work partnership**: **"Threes get the job done, but Ones do it right"** (quantity versus quality). Threes take the most efficient route, shortcuts, spotty directions, details later; this drives Ones up the wall. Story: a One nurse among Three doctors who arrive "at sixty miles an hour," spot-check, order, exit; she observed for twelve hours; she learned to do it her way with minimal input ("they just want it done and don't care about the way it's done"). [p.277] Three managers run several projects, initiate, delegate, move on; Ones "backtrack in search of a plan. No plan equals full stop." Three managers: "Employees are responsible for small matters ... I'm on the front line, I represent the business." Underappreciated Ones slow down; standoff of principle-plus-data versus "something that works"; neither admits error because both are identified with professional profiles; "two angry people who are both right." Resolution: both committed to excellence; Threes bluntly informed "This isn't working"; "Employee input day," "Employee recognition week" with a star by the One's name; Ones should phrase suggestions in efficiency, profitability, competitive edge. One managers controlling, want detail certainty (Three employee bends rules); Ones focus on character, honest effort and fair return; Threes want instant success, respond to competition, bonuses, titles; bulletproof guidelines get ignored or finessed. Works with Three as point person in the field, rapid decisions; Three's input valuable to the One manager.

### One with Four: Perfectionist-Romantic [pp.279-282]
**Couple**: volatile, potential for deep self-understanding; share the One-Four line; Romantics act out "improper" emotions that frighten Perfectionists (shame, depression, envy, competition, despair on the way to joy and creativity); Fours dramatize emotional needs Ones have suppressed; may introduce Ones to "a life of feelings rather than a life determined by right-or-wrong thinking." Ones: "Pull yourself together. There's work to be done." Cycle: One's criticism reinforces Four's lack of worth; Four retaliates ("You're unfeeling," "You're cold," "You can't be touched") implying superiority. Interrupting: Fours see why Ones control "bad" emotion; Ones see the integrity of deep connection, that rules cannot govern matters of the heart. **High side:** Four's emotionality loosens Ones' shoulds; Ones say they "found" their life direction or creativity at their risk point of Four; true companions in emotional pain; ideas of right and wrong become meaningful to Fours when deeply felt. Fours are attracted to One's steadiness; afraid of abandonment, they sabotage on realizing commitment (disappear, fight, depression); Ones hold ground and remember fights; Romantics respect those who don't respond to sabotage ("proof" that partners won't abandon). Disadvantage: shared dissatisfaction (Fours suffer from what's missing, Ones see flaws); animate by pleasure in the present moment.

**Work partnership**: One's perfect standards, Four's unique standards; bond in excellence; jointly critical of substandard performance; Fours less volatile at work. One managers' order feels like a container for feelings; trouble if distant and rule-bound (Fours dismiss as emotionally dishonest, "rules more important than people"); Fours need special treatment, otherwise dismiss the rules (a "reckless, delinquent side"); need personal recognition from an acknowledged authority and genuine interest in their project. Fours secure at One: clarity of purpose, creativity perfected and made practical; or disaster: low-functioning picky judgment, "Wham. Suddenly whatever the One manager proposes isn't good enough," bitterness, public scene, insulted by pay scale, projects and decor; One managers retaliate; mediated fights can be healing (Ones angry without guilt; Fours' flaws exposed without abandonment). Four managers: Three-like precision (competitive) or office centerpiece with moods affecting pace; erratic supervision makes Ones anxious; Four managers are relieved of supervisory details when Ones keep the calendar in order.

### One with Five: Perfectionist-Observer [pp.283-284]
**Couple**: look-alike pair (independent, work alone, value emotional control); pragmatic, not romantic (livelihood, practical projects, organized family life). Tension from lack of information: Fives' blank affect and Ones' worry scheme; Ones interpret silence as judgment; "Neither partner wants to get angry, so important topics are neglected"; tension eases when Fives speak or fight (anger shows investment; emotional distance vanishes when something matters). Fives find Ones' intensity attractive, "a wake-up call to life," and position themselves as counselors and advisers. Ones find a steady, knowing presence and absence of judgment. Couple tends to be "somewhat dry"; long-term couples say moving into anger and sexuality helped.

**Work partnership**: Fives come alive with intellectual curiosity and can be actively critical, a bond; One managers relieved by meticulous Five employees; "joined in their powers of criticism," thrifty, good at clearing inefficiency and waste; tug-of-war over information (Ones want more, Fives give less; Five needs to know exactly how much is required; manager should not call for further improvement past the standard). Five managers set clear objectives, are methodical; formal job contact; first defense is withdrawal; Five managers must initiate conversation because silence reads as criticism to Ones. Product: well-researched, well-documented work ("Fives value knowledge for the sake of knowing, and Ones enjoy building an impeccable case").

### One with Six: Perfectionist-Trooper [pp.284-287]
**Couple**: bond through shared vision requiring hard work; underdog causes coincide with Ones' dedication to perfection; "dedicated to a dream." Both seen as negative thinkers (Ones preoccupied with error, Sixes with doubt) yet see "the beauty of the human dilemma and the creativity that springs from pain"; hang in during trouble; unusual trust; each sees the other's fear of success. **Mutual projection:** both feel guilty if they can't perform, both procrastinate (One afraid to err, Six doubts success); "Why don't things move faster?" "Look how he keeps stalling." "Is this deliberate?"; afraid to argue (anger frightens Sixes, Ones think anger wrong). Without a reality check: One sees Six as holding out, Six feels blamed; One takes superior stance ("I'm right; and it's true, you aren't good enough"); Six may "look bad as a repulsion technique" hoping the One leaves in disgust for a guilt-free exit. Remedy: "nip projection in the bud"; a good talk; disclosure; irritation can boil over without warning.

**Work partnership**: Ones rise through effort and are the most rule-bound; Sixes are ambivalent about hierarchy; under perfectionist managers Sixes bend rules and encourage others ("roam in packs"). Successful One managers reassure even when unnecessary because Sixes read lack of praise in the worst light; manager's anger about traffic is registered and referred to the Six's worst case; Sixes build a case and spread the alarm ("Can this manager be trusted?"); don't entrench in right-or-wrong; reframe or offer a face-saving solution ("That was an experimental phase ..."). Faced with uncertainty a Six "will either fall at authority's feet or go for authority's throat." Both are alert to hidden intentions; dispel rumors and loopholes. Troopers manage well short-term, waver long-term, deadlining, stop-start style; strong at groundbreaking, trouble expanding; Ones rally around a deadline given inspiring example. Weekly informal roundtable check-ins "clear the air" and serve as reality testing ("always surprised to discover that their project is still safe"). Product: airtight, well-documented analysis, excellent troubleshooters.

### One with Seven: Perfectionist-Epicure [pp.287-290]
**Couple**: share a line (each sees a major aspect of self); Sevens admire One's clarity, discipline, follow-through, which can ground "monkey mind"; Ones are attracted to Epicurean spontaneity (an evening by the fire "takes on theatrical proportions"; holidays become heroic events); Ones welcome their Seven security point as happiness and freedom, yet see Epicures as "flakes." Story "Seven Heaven" [p.288]: Perfectionist mother visits her Seven son in a hill town in the Himalayas (no running water, heat, flies, no stove); he is enraptured: "This is the best restaurant in town. I can eat at the best place in town every night." **Dynamics:** Ones push for product, Sevens want fun; spontaneity can be "the right thing" if lifestyle says so; more common is "parallel play" under agreements on money, time, family. Pinned into practicalities, Sevens fight back; stressed Sevens at One fight limits ("Limits are for lesser beings," "Rules are for inferior minds"); Ones' black-or-white thinking makes Epicures look selfish and bad. "In a fight, Ones get rigid and Sevens evade. Sevens try to demean the problem, and Ones move in to control." Sevens use Ones' fury to obscure facts and Ones can be baited into offensive, giving the Seven an excuse to disappear; Epicures should come halfway and face the anger. "Ones on vacation will naturally look and act like Sevens"; the trick is to bring some vacation home.

**Work partnership**: Seven as inventor, planner, meeter and greeter; One provides structure, follow-through, control. Different thinking: One right-wrong and logical; Seven interlocking concepts; Perfectionists can't follow multioptional thought, Sevens feel limited by linear approach. One managers pointing out small mistakes make Sevens retreat behind excuses ("I meant it to look that way," "The timing wasn't right"); Ones see lies instead of "process thought." Manage Sevens with a light touch, reasonable deadlines, evaluate results only. Seven managers leave "a swath of unanswered questions" on vacation; "clear memo" that's sketchy; Ones think "This is mismanagement. This is abusive to staff" and follow the letter of the law rather than assist; Epicures should spend a day producing exact instructions while Ones expose loopholes, after which Ones shape the details so the manager can take a real vacation.

### One with Eight: Perfectionist-Boss [pp.290-292]
**Couple**: "If there's such a thing as Enneagram opposites, this is it": both anger types, black-or-white thinkers, convinced they're right; fireworks. Ones are mesmerized by the Eight's force and sexuality; Eights are attracted to the discipline and good intentions of Ones, whose moral stance "looks truthful to the somewhat lawless Boss." Direct anger is a blessing to Ones (someone who won't judge anger as wrong), but goaded Ones retaliate in force. Good at neutral decisions, harder on personal ones; Eight and One are wing points of the self-forgetting Nine; both reactive rather than proactive; prodding polarizes. Story "Why Ones and Eights Fight" [p.291]: Ones are right because they're good (guilty if wrong); Eights are right because they're bad ("It's good to be bad ... Bad is lawless and free and exciting ... being bad doesn't make you wrong"). Supervisory Ones make Eights lawless; clash can heal: Ones teach limits and boundaries, Eights teach Ones to go for what they want; anger frees Ones from prissiness and gives permission for irreverence and humor; "It looks a lot worse from the outside." Combination: Ones disciplined, Eights have juice, a potential for focused power or a conflict cycle.

**Work partnership**: **"Eight is the partnership's id and One is the superego."** Anger accentuated at work without the outlets of a good fight or sex. Supervision styles: "one right way" versus "my way or the highway"; One managers should evaluate on performance only; an Eight employee who produces should be left alone. Eight managers control by physical presence; Ones out of touch with their aggression: "The boss is angry, so the boss is bad"; "Eights get angry and blurt whatever they think at the moment. Ones control themselves until they're in the right." When id and superego agree: obsessive workers, practical results; Eights use Ones as guides ("Is this appropriate? Are we pushing too hard?") and take worry from Ones ("I'll take charge. We'll take a chance.").

### One with Nine: Perfectionist-Mediator [pp.292-294]
**Couple**: look alike; bracketed in the same category in some psychological systems; both repress anger and are obsessive ruminators; important decisions slow ("a conspiracy to postpone difficulty"); Ones preoccupied with details, Nines wait for someone else to initiate; both want a peaceful, comfortable, household routine; Mediators' agreeableness reduces Ones' anxiety about being right; Nines find structure in a Perfectionist's "correct" worldview. **Difference when action is called for:** Ones go into fast forward when committed; Nines sit on the fence even when they agree. Ones can jump-start but if "what ought to be done" is the priority, Nines think "This is someone else's idea." Nines in distress go to "field focus" (all options the same), "vague-out and go numb" under pursuit ("Be explicit. What do you want?"). Bitter fights about small matters as vents. **Anger is healthy for this couple:** people know what they don't want when angry; "Not that" eliminates an option. Help: Mediators see good intentions in One-like anger; Perfectionists see anger won't work: "You can't push Nines into commitment, but they will merge and join with a loved one's needs."

**Work partnership**: obsessive quality ideal for setting up and consolidating systems; endurance; strengthen known procedures; dislike rapid change; master specific areas; "detailmongers"; both procrastinate, neither likes risk. One managers must remember positive reinforcement (Nines respond to encouragement, Ones focus on flaws); underappreciated Nines control by silence and slowing ("make the manager dance"); balance feedback; "unconditional positive support." Nine managers set up systems and coast; a One employee's critical faculties rope in details; Ones narrow tasks when Nines are overwhelmed.

### Double Two: double Giver [pp.294-297]
**Couple**: "a rare sighting"; with two Givers no one receives; each embarrassed to be the center. They succeed by helping the partner succeed and share glory "from behind the scenes," spared public failure. "But double Two is a setup": each thinks "I'm being forced to go first"; dependency exposed makes them angry; each wants the other to be the mover; stalemate and boredom; neither knows who to be without the catalyst of another's need. Tempers explode as repressed needs surface; they unify around a common venture (business, family problems) and look outside the home for inspiration. They need to bring attention back to themselves ("Having needs equals being rejected" is the old formula); learn to be alone; relating is not denying needs: "Only half a relationship can be built on serving a partner's potentials; the other half is developing yourself." Often friends, less often mates; as friends they can encourage each other to receive.

**Work partnership**: often do well; serve a task "as if it were a third party" (a task has needs, identity, emotional juice); compete to be indispensable, works if roles equally important; "two good promoters focused on a common effort." Two managers are personable, want to be liked; lead aggressively for a popular decision; manipulative and vengeful if made the target of hostility (going over heads, seducing the power elite; employees put down peers to impress). Clear boundaries and public, objective advancement conditions remove the indirect approach; need much support to enter public competition, then hold position with pride. Sensitive to criticism ("You can't be indispensable if you make mistakes"): sidestep ("It wasn't my fault"); reassure about the future ("Our relationship is not at risk"); separate worth from merit; respect them as people, not performers. Pride inflates ("running the show from a corner of the room") and deflates when not asked ("Why wasn't I called?"); involve in specific work; if excluded, expect a move to the low side of Eight. Twos play favorites ("Approval is not extended to proletariats"); "Getting close to power is their way to control."

### Two with Three: Giver-Performer [pp.297-299]
**Couple**: Twos give attention, Threes expect it; support the Performer's needs; **"Givers work to be loved, and Threes love to work."** "Successful love": Giver interprets family feelings, emotional center of the home. Common complaint: late hours, falling asleep at home; Two waits by the phone ("Where's the attention? I'm not needed here."). Performers put romance on the schedule; Twos state needs; Threes are not deliberately neglectful ("They do to others what they do to themselves"). In a fight Twos feel superior because they're more emotional, Threes "because of all that I've done"; Twos read effort as affection. Reassured, Twos demand less time; easy if Three focuses on feelings and Two doesn't compete with a job.

**Work partnership**: high-powered team that produces and promotes; Three as manager; Two backs if attention is met; if the Three is self-promoting, the Two finds another candidate; Givers soften the manager's image (Twos don't see image as deceptive). Neglected Twos may initiate an exposé ("They only look good because you don't know them"); Three managers should show interest and hold private talks; "Givers won't ask and Performers don't remember." "Twos don't work well in a vacuum. They work for the person, not for the task." Two managers want to be liked and struggle with competitive Threes who go around authority and want the job; Twos must set limits and cap goals; apportion a small domain; Threes may learn the Giver's people skills from example.

### Two with Four: Giver-Romantic [pp.299-301]
**Couple**: "like a dance," push-pull: each pulls back when the other is available, pursues when the other disappears ("You come forward, I go backward. You go backward, I want you again."). Each appreciates the other's capacity for feeling; romantic love without embarrassment; dance of emotional distance because each fears commitment. Share the Two-Four line: Twos at the security point feel free to seek authentic commitment, to be dramatic and selfish; see an exaggeration of their emotional story in Fours, who "make emotion public." Fours are stressed reaching out and feel deprived meeting others' needs; see Twos' flirtation and flattery as lacking depth. "A Two's power is in pursuit": if Four rejects, Two pursues; if Four turns available, Two is frightened away. Dance ends in commitment. Two fears being ordinary ("Will my Four abandon me if I am myself?"); Four should question the urge to criticize ("Why do I see the flaws when my Giver is with me?").

**Work partnership**: emotional tone matters; attention to people even in a factory; knowing each other's personal lives is "a motivation"; workplace as belonging. Two managers' persona turns Fours off ("sucking up," "How tasteless to be so needy," "Why does he have to be liked?"). Feeling criticized, Two managers look for a need to fill; Fours see focus on flaws. Competitive Fours: give own territory, delegate, fall back; single out for unique responsibility; Two's need to be indispensable and Four's need to feel unique become competitive unless Four is respected in a special area. Four managers enjoy status; Twos help the manager of distinctive flair; Fours reward the "understanding"; ignored Twos retaliate by pulling out ("suddenly nothing seems to be on schedule"), typical revenge supporting someone else's work; "Romantic managers see employees as working for them, but Twos don't work for, they only work with." Make the Giver feel essential and the manager's moodiness impersonal.

### Two with Five: Giver-Observer [pp.301-304]
**Couple**: attraction of opposites; "Five is the most contracted Enneagram type, and Two is the most extended toward others." Twos are drawn to the self-possession and quiet; "someone at home"; Fives' relative immunity from what others think; Fives are drawn to the Two's open-hearted generosity and engagement. Two is the couple's socialite, speaks at parties; the afternoon with the couple is easier than alone with the Five unless interests coincide. "Twos and Fives can look like they're from different species": Twos "go toward people to interact and socialize, while Fives move away to analyze and think." Tug-of-war: Two pulls for contact, Five contracts. If separation solidifies each sees the other as "the case" (the Two reads withdrawal as emotional deprivation; Twos are attracted by the challenge of opening someone set against emotion; the Five reads the Two's oscillations as instability). **They join at Eight:** a powerful aggressive meeting when Five feels secure enough to fight; Two should pull back so Five can come forward. Observers learn to stay in the room; shouldn't dismiss spontaneous feelings as trivial; set a system for the heart (a time set for future discussion reassures Givers).

**Work partnership**: Two's people orientation plus Five's abstract isolated work; works with either as boss; best when focus is on task. Two managers may intrude on Five privacy, draw them into the social scene, worry that Fives are unhappy; focus on projects; give specific tasks, separate offices; Fives mind the shop while the Two extends operations; the Two protects the Five from dealing with people. Five manager: door ajar but "an aura of unavailability"; place the Giver as special representative (natural link between public and power); semiformal meetings with each staffer for opinions; learn to tolerate group meetings; employees' "Where is leadership when you need it?"; the Two may run interference if the Five disappears under fire, but if the Five disappears from the Two the abandoned Giver "may protest loudly."

### Two with Six: Giver-Trooper [pp.304-306]
**Couple**: Two moves toward Six to disarm fear of intimacy; Six doubt as a catalyst for the Giver who likes overcoming obstacles ("Believe in me. Believe in us."); Twos support the Six's underdog goals so the Six can trust the intentions. Harder: making the Trooper personally successful; Sixes may see Twos' help as self-serving ("whether the Two in fact stands to benefit"), feel set up as vehicle for the Two's ambition, sabotage by failing expectations; soothed yet doubting flattery; Two becomes critical of "stop-start progress." Reduce mistrust: Six separates personal goals and makes them priority, remembers to be physically demonstrative, accepts honest affection; Twos discern altruistic versus self-serving help ("giving to get"), find their own professions; Sixes can be unusually loyal supporters.

**Work partnership**: another partnership of differences; Twos attracted to power, Sixes ambivalent about authority; natural alliance in an underdog context (turnaround, cause under fire); conflict in "regular" settings with self-interest and competition; both falter as long-term leaders (Six stress at the performance point Three; Two wants to be liked). Six managers want loyalty and are disturbed if the Two alters to please a powerful rival ("Just where does this employee's loyalty lie?"); both believe themselves targets of free-floating animosity; the Six seeks the source of difficulty to eliminate it, the Two tries to make friends. Two employees misread Six hesitancy as lack of commitment ("for Six trust is a very slow build"). Troopers want to be egalitarian and may not cultivate Givers with personal attention ("Twos must feel that they wield private influence in order to feel safe"). Loyal Sixes see trouble from far off and Two managers use political and people skills to ward it off; if seen as self-serving, a behind-the-scenes coalition may expose the authority.

### Two with Seven: Giver-Epicure [pp.306-309]
**Couple**: "a classic fun couple"; Givers want to help Epicures realize plans; the Seven's vision is the focal point of emotional life; shared hope of a bright future. Both have broad interests (Twos' many selves, Sevens' multioptional thinking); drawn to the best entertainment; Givers see the Seven's potential and hidden pain, fascinated by sunny exterior and dissatisfaction, read gluttony for experience as a search for emotional depth; Twos may feel called to make Sevens productive and heal fears; get adventure and attention. Both optimistic; Sevens self-directed; wise Twos pursue the interests of their many selves rather than wait at home; given freedom, Sevens can face the C word. Naturally seductive; each considers potential other partners; ground in commitment and decide how much outside attention each needs. Both wary of prolonged contact (Sevens feel limited, Twos feel exposed); Seven escapes into pleasant activity, Two pursues; attention crisis: "The Two sees a superficial lightweight, and the Seven sees an emotional drag"; "This was never meant to be." They collude in optimism; help: focus on real feelings rather than "intimate ideas"; move from fascination to emotional depth.

**Work partnership**: popular; Seven ideas, Two makes them happen; danger is that neither leads to a conclusion (Epicures best at initial stages, Givers best supporting a strong leader with tangible results). A Two with a Seven boss may try to manifest all options when the Seven was thinking out loud; neglected Twos manipulate a better deal elsewhere ("Twos do not waste time on unappreciative people"); narcissistic Seven and unhappy Two means no grand scheme succeeds; Twos "work the system" to find a graceful exit. Two managers should protect Sevens from boredom (hard workers while learning or challenged); may fear authority is threatened ("How will it look to superiors if this employee gets away with something?" "How can I control my employee and still be liked?"); either befriend and ally or withdraw; make consequences clear: "Seven-like charm, rationalization, and reframing all disappear in the face of a deadline."

### Two with Eight: Giver-Boss [pp.309-312]
**Couple**: aligned around the seduction of Two and the power of Eight; "The Giver moves toward others by trying to please, and the Boss moves against them to uncover the truth." Both want to be central (they meet at Two); Twos adjust their feelings to others' needs, Eights insist on their own; Eights sexually expressive, Twos equate sexuality with love. Common: Two tries to soften Eight, Eight holds out, an attraction that can last years; the self-referencing Eight gets attention, the Giver focused on the other prevents own needs from emerging; in return the Boss gives energetic protection and forceful leadership. A secure Eight shifts to Two (not "turn into"): acts out need for attention by controlling the Giver's life or supporting goals; less commonly surrenders control; Eights "in Two" are extremely generous, putting the Giver in the position of having to receive, which may feel like domination. If Twos find their own agendas, Eights can be second in command; if the Two fears exposure or the Eight dominates, Twos "explode, fight for freedom, and shoot across the diagram to their own stress point of Eight." Open hostilities can draw the couple closer. Another outcome: Two cut off disappears into the arms of friends or another relationship, feels used, wants revenge; war with an Eight ex-partner. **Twos in Eight can be deadly manipulators** (indirect influence, social pressure, presenting the Eight as "the case," loud public complaint if pride injured). A crisis: Eight feels betrayed by manipulation, clamps down, belligerent: "You're dishonest. Don't mess with me."

**Work partnership**: key word is trust; near-synchronous action; Eight as the heavy, Two as liaison with staff; good cop-bad cop. Two managers must win an Eight's support; Eight employees focus on the Two's popularity image ("Is leadership genuinely honest, or is this manager self-serving?"); favoring those "worth it" looks dishonest; a perceived injustice leads to eliminating controls; clear limits and consequences for both management and staff; document agreements (without which the Eight "may feel too threatened to cooperate fully": "Maybe there's a hidden agenda here. Let's test it out"). Eight managers are loved or hated by Twos; loved, they get loyal support; Two employees should provide "overkill reportage" (Eights imagine escalating conspiracy when information lags); give the Two a search-and-discovery mission to keep the Giver indispensable and the Eight in control; if the pair doesn't get along, a Two withholds contact and information and shelters in a small, personally chosen clique.

### Two with Nine: Giver-Mediator [pp.312-315]
**Couple**: can look alike (both merge with others' agendas and emotions, focus on fulfilling needs); motives differ: **Nines want a reason for living, found through a mate; Twos try to find identity through others**; each affected at a nonverbal level. Givers help Nines find purpose, esp. if the Nine can excel in an area for Two's pride; Twos attracted to Nines' gentleness; Nines enjoy affection; either may use sex to wake up to contact; sexuality as metaphor: Two asks "What do you want?" and the Nine answers "I want you as you really are"; the Nine helps the Two tell the adjusted self from the authentic emotional self, the Two energizes the Nine erotically. **Crisis:** the Two becomes indispensable and the Nine feels controlled and suspects filling Two's unrecognized needs; stubborn refusal; Nines "hold back their own potential as a way of getting even"; Twos get bored and furious; Twos resent taking the rudder when they prefer to be inspired and led; painful cycle: "Nine withdraws in the face of Two's demands, causing Two to pursue more forcefully, causing Nine to entrench in stubborn silence." If Nine is stubborn, Two demands freedom; fearing separation Nines wake up and may become possessive; if the Nine moves forward the Two falls back with loud demands for freedom; the Nine can withdraw attention, making the Two frantic. Ease: Givers support real needs; Nines turn attention to themselves; Twos know Nines take time and "The most important decisions are last"; Twos initiate a question, negotiate a deadline, leave Nines alone.

**Work partnership**: "a jump start to a source of potential energy"; common in marriage and business; Nine holds the agenda buried under secondary commitments ("It matters too much. It's too much to hope for. It's too important to complete."); Two's magic is meeting needs and drawing people out; the Two structures the Nine's potential. Different rhythms: Two impatient with the time over simple decisions; Two employees infuriated by repetitious meetings. Both merge so they have nonverbal understanding and care about the feeling tone; Nines absorb distress and hear all views to defuse conflict; Twos spot potential winners and place them strategically. Two managers rely on developing key people over meticulous structure and a "well-knit community"; value that Nines rarely compete with the boss; Nines loyal to benign authority and motivated by public credit. Two managers have favorites; Nines relate to authority through structure and may resent inequality; if chosen, the Nine tunes out the problem; if not, unexpressed anger affects supervision; apologies and a productive routine restore. Nine managers are effective with a clear course; Two employees supply key information, funnel opinions through the office pipeline, point to the politically expedient course, chart the Nine's self-advancement.

### Double Three: double Performer [pp.315-318]
**Couple**: "an endangered species" (few Threes in Enneagram classes; many in business circles but few long-standing couples). Threes are attracted all around the diagram (similar high-energy types like Twos, Sevens and Three-like Fours; or dissimilar highly emotional partners); in Palmer's area the most reported Three pairing is **Three with Five** (balance of opposites). Double Threes: everything seems possible; many projects; impatient and competitive in domestic projects, so they keep separate elaborate professional connections; activity-related social life; "relate through shared activity rather than hanging out and 'being.'" Trick: support each other's accomplishments without sliding into **parallel play** or a "picture-postcard life with all the appearances of happiness and prosperity but without the inner intensity of strong emotional connection"; "relationships can look great and feel empty"; growing indifference toward building for the future ("Yet another challenge? Do we have to mobilize again?"). **Emotional deadening** takes the edge off enjoying the fruit of their efforts; need to hurry to the next task; same feeling as Sevens at midlife ("Haven't we been here before?"). The need to turn inward appears as weariness with external life; projects seem superficial; a double Three relationship can either compound the worldview in which feelings are incompatible with getting the job done or provide "an educated source of help for the turn inward."

**Work partnership**: valued in go-ahead phases; sell and promote; "personal enthusiasm that can be a company's best advertisement"; a new, potentially profitable project is Three territory. Difficulties when forced to outperform each other (merit systems pit them); psychological well-being depends on winning; more vulnerable to professional loss; covert job cultivation, "jump ship," hoarding and trading contacts; win-at-all-costs confused with marketability. Helps: their own domain; model efficient cooperation; attach status to a group win. Invincible when goals align ("can-do pair"); think alike (blessing and bane); reframe signs of difficulty; seek short-term gains; chafe in consolidation; contraction and downsizing read as failure; at a disadvantage in turnarounds, unpopular decisions, long-term planning. Three managers must model the image expected (Threes take on the look of the environment; if cooperation is called for they cooperate, if personal achievement is rewarded they compete). Clearly demarcated openings for rising; public recognition while downplaying competition; systems reflecting participation over competition.

### Three with Four: Performer-Romantic [pp.318-321]
**Couple**: both focused on image and regard (respect for achievement versus distinctiveness); present well in public ("successful elegance"). Three is drawn to Four's inner drama as counterpart to public recognition; Four is challenged by Three's worldly focus; Three's task orientation is irritating yet attractively distancing; Fours never get enough attention. Story [p.319] (Three with Four): driving into a Santa Fe sunset, the wife sobs ("There'll never be another like this, and soon it will be gone") while the Three plans second jobs "which will buy us a piece of the ridge." **Major difficulty:** Four's moods and Three's suspended feelings; "Four longs, while Three promises more time and then reneges"; unavailability can magnetize; Fours go into high drama or depression, the demand Threes detest; "Fours overvalue emotional life, and Threes undervalue it"; a Four competing for attention and a Three over-scheduling. Help: enter the other's worldview; Threes demonstrate commitment by working "for us," Fours learn to tell numbing from commitment; Threes should not trivialize Four's yearning; learning the full range of emotions "could be the task of a lifetime ... deeply satisfying instead of merely successful."

**Work partnership**: juxtaposition of differences, supportive or disastrous; task focus bypasses tender feelings; Fours produce work "elegantly successful rather than successfully slick"; works when Four's unusual, slightly controversial contribution succeeds with Three's support; collapses when they compete. Three managers "do for show"; Fours see doing something significant versus what looks good to the crowd (an awards dinner turned photo opportunity; meetings scheduled with no time for emotional fallout); Three managers should pencil in "Don't yell" and "Ask permission." Threes love creative productivity and are captivated by those insisting on authentic work; Fours hold out for years rather than compromise; Three willing to submit to numbing drudgery to rise. Romantic managers see Threes' overt competition as gauche ("They may be smart, but they don't have style," "How tacky to be so pushy"); a blocked Three makes an end run to the power elite. Third-party mediation: Three respects social boundaries, reaffirms interest in the task, reaffirms the Four's seniority; clear chain of command.

### Three with Five: Performer-Observer [pp.321-323 and continues in part 3]
**Couple**: Threes are extroverted, most Fives introverted; the Performer's self-image depends on recognition, the Five needs privacy. Threes move toward the emotionally retracted partner and "commandeer the mechanics of a relationship" (brochures, itinerary, reservations: "All the Five has to do is pack and go"). Very common in love and business. Threes show affection by giving time and energy, which fits a reclusive partner. Saying yes to commitment is difficult for Fives: "It's so easy to detach and let go that a Five's continued presence is de facto commitment"; they "initiate" by positioning to be drawn out; Threes should hold back to respect boundaries. Observers show availability nonverbally (sharing information, household maintenance, presence). Three is typically the pair's **social secretary** (filters messages, consults the Five in private, conveys decisions); home life as parallel play with designated meeting times. Unexpectedly the Five often objects to the Three's absences; once attached, Observers get territorial "within the security of the castle walls" and, Eight-like, limit outside interests; cyclical crisis: Fives sulk and withhold comfort, presence and sex; Threes bury themselves in work, which "has the adverse effect of increasing the distance." Helps: negotiate mutual commitments; both have intimacy issues (**Threes afraid to be seen separately from their achievements, Fives afraid of being drained by other people's needs**); Fives forthcoming if they know what is expected, Threes express through action; talk easier during a golf game or hike; Threes show love as tangible success "for us," Fives by giving up private time and space; Fives learn to enjoy social life, Threes be touched by silence.

**Work partnership (range ends here, mid-section):** "one of the most frequently reported business partnerships"; the Observer is typically the analyst while the Performer handles public matters; Fives often produce the basic concept and theory, amplified and sold by the energetic Three. *(Continues in part 3 of the extraction.)* [p.323]
