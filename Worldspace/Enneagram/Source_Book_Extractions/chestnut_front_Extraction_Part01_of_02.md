## Overview

Chestnut presents the Enneagram as a map for self-knowledge built on one premise: people live in a "waking sleep" of automatic habits formed in childhood as defenses, and growth begins by observing those habits from an inner witness. The Introduction argues for this premise and positions the Enneagram as an "owner's manual" for the self. Chapter 1 sets out the structure: personality as a "false self" plus Shadow, three centers of intelligence, nine types with their passions, three instincts producing 27 subtypes (with one "countertype" per type), and a three-step growth path (self-observation, self-inquiry, self-development) plus two "Enneagramatic paths" (the Inner Flow along the arrow lines, and the Vice-to-Virtue vertical path). Chapter 2 gives the "perennial wisdom" background: a seed-to-oak parable, a three-step transformation path mapped onto Points 3, 6 and 9, the symbolism of the circle, triangle and hexad, nine as a boundary number, parallels in Kabbalah, the Odyssey and Dante, and the lineage from Gurdjieff to Ichazo to Naranjo. She follows mainly Claudio Naranjo's descriptions of types and subtypes.

## Introduction: Self-Awareness and the Enneagram [pp.6-17]

**Epigraphs** [p.6]: William James (the faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention is the root of judgment, character and will); Victor Frankl (between stimulus and response there is a space, and in it the power to choose).

### The problem and the two goals [pp.6-7]
- People ask why they do what they do, how to have more fulfilling relationships, how to get better results and live more satisfyingly. Behavior change is central to growth and very hard because of unconscious habit.
- The one capacity needed: creating "mental and emotional space" inside oneself to observe what one is doing and why, seeing thoughts and feelings "in action instead of just being absorbed by them."
- **Two goals of the book:** (1) show how ancient wisdom teachings illuminate the idea that **self-knowledge** is the key to growth; she defines self-knowledge as "the capacity to observe, think about, and own your thoughts, feelings, and actions"; (2) provide a map and instructions, an "owner's manual for your own self," for expanding self-knowledge toward positive change.

### "The Common Truth of the Human Condition: We Are Asleep" [pp.7-10]
- Each person is unique with great potential but lives in a "waking sleep" due to early childhood programming. The knowledge needed has been encoded for centuries or millennia in teachings, philosophy, myths and symbols, and the Enneagram is a wisdom tradition "rediscovered in the last fifty years or so."
- To change, one must first know how one truly operates now. A deep unconscious pattern is the false belief that we already know ourselves well enough; she argues we do not, and that thinking we do is part of the problem.
- **"Sleep"** = the unexamined belief that we have relatively unlimited freedom, when in fact we respond in predictable, repetitive, programmed ways "much like uniquely specialized machines," unable to grow past the programming without conscious understanding of it. [p.7-8]
- Both Western psychology and Eastern spiritual traditions recognize this state. Signs: repeating comfortable habits, going on autopilot, entering a room and forgetting why, reading a page without taking it in, conversing while the mind drifts, being unable to feel emotions after something significant. "Perhaps it's more accurate to say that our habits are the show." [p.8]
- **Psychological account:** as a survival mechanism the psyche "goes to sleep" (dissociates) from painful experience. It is impossible to be aware of everything, and learning to minimize pain and danger is advantageous, but if the ways we do this are never examined as adults we fall asleep to who we are.
- **How it starts:** human babies have the longest dependency of any mammal, so children have wired-in defenses against psychological and emotional threat. Early, sometimes life-saving, coping strategies harden into **patterns** of thinking, feeling and behaving, which act as **"organizing principles"** (beliefs about how the world works and how one must act to survive or thrive) and then into invisible automatic **habits** that direct attention and adaptive strategies. [p.8]
- Examples of strategy: a child under pressure to "be good" becomes "perfect" to avoid criticism; another hides vulnerability, exerts control and seems fearless. Together these strategies make up the organizing principles of the personality. [p.8-9]
- The patterns trigger automatically even when the original threats are long gone. The misalignment between ingrained habits and the yearning to live authentically and spontaneously is a source of suffering, dissatisfaction and unhappiness; outdated coping strategies become "unseen prisons." [p.9]
- **Author's own example [p.9]:** growing up she focused on relationships, making others happy to feel safe, being charming and pleasant to avoid criticism and rejection. This put her to sleep to her own feelings, needs and desires (asserting them risked conflict). A graduate-school roommate observed she never got angry; she realized it was true. She had lost touch with the natural flow of her emotions and with knowing what she needed.
- Early self-protective habits are "self-limiting strategies based on an overly narrow perspective" of one's capacity. Noticing, studying and understanding them is the "liberating 'self-work.'" Sometimes seeing a habit and why it developed is enough; stubborn ones need work over time. First steps: understand that we all fall asleep and notice how you go to sleep to yourself. [p.9-10]

### "Another Common Truth of the Human Condition: We Can Wake Up" [pp.10-12]
- The waking sleep is the starting point of waking up. Ability to wake up is inherent in being human; many ancient traditions say becoming aware of who we are is the purpose of human life. It requires consistent effort; laziness risks falling asleep again.
- **Self-observation** begins with perceiving one's own unconsciousness: looking for automatic patterns builds an objective interior capacity for awareness separate from them. One learns to recognize "when you have gone on automatic, or when your 'program' is running" (her cousin Chris Fasano's phrase: you "catch on to your own game"). It creates separation between patterns and consciousness so one can watch thoughts, feelings and actions without "being one" with them and without judging them as good or bad. [p.10-11]
- The entry point: "catch yourself snoozing," slow down, observe with distance, and learn "to pay attention to what you are paying attention to (and not paying attention to)." [p.11]
- **Meditation:** psychology and spiritual teaching both point to simple meditation as the best way to build self-reflection and conscious intention, mainly by noticing and consciously stabilizing or shifting the "focus of attention." Self-inquiry: "What am I paying attention to and why?" [p.11]
- **Concentration:** the "faculty of fixing maximum attention on a minimum of space" (traditional formulation). Stilling automatic thought builds a **"zone of silence"** to support self-observation. [p.11]
- **Practice of self-observation:** put attention on present-moment thoughts, feelings and actions without judgment, and bring focus back repeatedly when it wanders; a mindfulness exercise. The **"attentional muscle"** strengthens like a muscle through repetition. [p.11]
- This develops an **"inner witness"** or "witnessing consciousness," a key ingredient of meditation and many forms of psychotherapy; simple yet extremely difficult. **"Self-remembering"** (remembering to pay conscious attention to yourself) never becomes habitual and takes practice. [p.11-12]
- Fields converging on self-awareness as vital ingredient in development: psychotherapy, spirituality, philosophy, brain science, leadership training, business development. What was once the territory of spiritual directors and psychotherapists is now mainstream. [p.12]

### "The Enneagram as a Map for Developing Self-Awareness" [pp.12-16]
- **"Know Thyself"** (inscribed on the Temple of Apollo at Delphi): the cornerstone of Western wisdom, treating study of the self from inside as hand in hand with study of the outer world. [p.12]
- **The Enneagram** ("a drawing of nine"): a nine-pointed star in a circle that provides a framework for, among other things, a personality type system of **twenty-seven distinct character "archetypes."** [p.12]
- On the surface: nine interconnected types, each connected to four others on the diagram. The symbol has "ancient and mysterious origins" and is said to model fundamental laws of the universe in mathematical terms. [p.13]
- **Archetype (boxed sidebar) [p.13]:** models or prototypes that help discern universal patterns. Jung: "typical modes of apprehension," "patterns of psychic perception and understanding common to all human beings." Jung took the concept from the ancient Greeks (archetypal principles; "clarifying universals in the chaos of human life"). Carolyn Myss: personal archetypes are "the psychic lenses through which we view ourselves and the world around us."
- The teaching's core echoes the world's oldest mystical, psycho-spiritual and philosophical traditions: an "inner witness" yields self-knowledge, which makes a higher state of consciousness possible. The Enneagram shows the witness what to pay attention to and gives a method for change. [p.13]
- **Structure named at p.14:** three "centers of intelligence," nine personality "types," twenty-seven "subtypes." Personality is "made up of patterns," invisible because we have been doing them so long, "in the same way a fish doesn't 'notice' the water he's swimming in."
- **Mechanism of use:** coping strategies fall into a finite number of categories. Detailed descriptions of the typical thoughts, emotions and behaviors of nine types (and three subtypes each) show how habits are enacted. Finding yourself in one of the 27 gives access to information to recognize patterns. [p.14]
- The Enneagram is **not static**: a "dynamic map of change" showing how habits develop and how one can free oneself over time; recognizing one's type lets one "catch yourself in the act." [p.14]
- **Case: Max [pp.14-15].** A lawyer in his late forties with anxiety and self-doubt, unable to leave a failing marriage; analytic talent fed imagining negative scenarios; belief in his own essential "badness" made him imagine others condemning him. Chestnut recognized him as a **"Self-Preservation Six"** (ongoing fear and uncertainty, circular thinking of questioning and doubt). Key inner conflicts identified: (1) false belief he was bad and would be rejected despite evidence; (2) sense that acting decisively on his own behalf would bring bad things; (3) mental habits that kept him in fear (trusted his analytical mind but doubted everything, so never felt safe). Central issue: no strong sense of inner authority (his father threatened and abused him and did not protect or support him), leaving a defensive strategy "run amok." He improved by learning to see his thinking from a larger perspective and using practices that worked against the tendencies.
- **False self vs. true self:** personality is a "false self," necessary up to a point for safe interaction, but the means by which we lose touch with the "true self," buried as the false self comes forward. The false self is "the problem" (we identify with it as all we are) and also "the vehicle for getting back to the true self": seeing through its automatic functioning, we can let it drop or work with it consciously, becoming less fixated and more creative and flexible. [p.15-16]

### Conclusion and acknowledgment of sources [pp.16-17]
- **Lineage:** Oscar Ichazo developed the Enneagram in Chile in the 1960s as part of a larger program of human transformation. Claudio Naranjo, psychiatrist, learned it from Ichazo in 1970 and translated the types into Western psychological language, refining it while teaching from the 1970s in Berkeley. The Enneagram "leaked out" from Naranjo's original student group and spread worldwide. The book focuses mainly on **Naranjo's descriptions** of types, subtypes and the path of conscious development.
- Further foundations: an ancient wisdom tradition, her psychotherapy training, her own self-work, and her most direct Enneagram teachers **Helen Palmer and David Daniels**.
- Hope: to translate the tradition and integrate it with modern insight; the work is "a great adventure of the kind immortalized in epic poems and classic myths." [p.16-17]

## Chapter 1: The Enneagram as a Framework for Understanding the Multidimensional Nature of Personality [pp.18-38]

**Epigraph** [p.18]: David Whyte, "What to Remember When Waking" (excerpt: we are "not a troubled guest" on earth; "What shape waits in the seed of you / to grow and spread its branches / against a future sky?").

### What is Personality? [pp.18-19]
- "Personality" = the part of character that develops to interface with the outside world, shaped by innate qualities and early experience with parents, caregivers, family and friends, and the physical, social and cultural environment.
- Most people equate personality with identity. Per the Enneagram, as in Western psychology, you are more than your personality: it is a **"false self"** that lets the vulnerable "true self" adapt, fit in and survive; a "defensive" or "compensatory" self whose coping strategies developed to fulfill needs and reduce anxieties.

### Personality as Ego and Shadow [p.19]
- Jung: as light illuminating an object casts darkness, the conscious brightness in how you see yourself creates a **"Shadow"** aspect, an unseen blind spot. Attributes judged "bad" (anger, jealousy, hatred, inferiority) get relegated to the Shadow; people who focus on negative traits may conversely hide "positive" qualities in the Shadow. The Shadow is everything we refuse to acknowledge about ourselves that still affects behavior.
- Repressing it creates unintended problems because it influences us unseen. Developing the true self requires knowing, accepting and integrating all parts, including Shadow elements.

### The Enneagram's 360-Degree View [pp.19-20]
- It describes each type's conscious patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving together with their repressed Shadow aspects, making it a tool for the hardest part of consciousness work: "realizing, owning, and accepting your blind spots."
- **Richard Rohr anecdote:** at the first meeting of what became the International Enneagram Association (Stanford, 1994), Franciscan priest Richard Rohr likened the Enneagram to the "Wheel of Fortune" (life's inevitable ups and downs). Western culture is oriented to the "ascent," so has trouble with the "descent." Because the Enneagram lets us see dark sides without judgment, it helps us accept and manage the descent.
- Without recognizing and accepting the whole self, including Shadow, growth stops. The Enneagram shows the "good" and "bad" parts of habitual programming so we can compassionately address the disowned and "fixated" (stuck) parts.

### The Enneagram Personality Map [p.20]
- It describes twenty-seven "false selves" systematically. Level 1: **center of intelligence** (body/physical, heart/emotional, head/intellectual). Level 2: each center divides into three **types** (nine total). The types and connecting lines draw the Enneagram diagram. Level 3: each type divides into three **subtypes** by relative emphasis of three basic instincts: **Self-Preservation, Social Interaction, Sexual (or One-to-One) Bonding.** Result: 27 subtypes, "unique personality types based on how each of the nine types are shaped by the three most central instinctual drives that we all share."

### The Three Centers [pp.20-21]
Humans are "three-brained beings" functioning through three centers of intelligence, three modes of perception, processing and expression: moving/sensing (kinesthetic), feeling, thinking. Each has positive uses and misuses.

| Center | Points | Function | Good use | Misuse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Body (also "motor"/"gut") | 8, 9, 1 | Active part in all physical movement; the "instinctive center" corresponds to instinctual functions. Motor center activated when thought initiates movement. | Impulse from the gut center can be a solid guide to right action | Impulsive behavior or inertia |
| Heart / emotional | 2, 3, 4 | Regulates feeling: experience and expression of emotions; connects to others through empathy | Empathy | Oversensitivity, insensitivity, emotional manipulation |
| Head / intellectual | 5, 6, 7 | Regulates thinking: thoughts, beliefs, cognitive activity | Dispassionate analysis and reasoning | Paralysis through overanalysis |

(Chestnut labels the body center types as "the motor center (Points 8, 9, and 1)" within the body center; the body center "includes" the motor center and the instinctive center. [p.21])

### The Nine Personality Archetypes [pp.21-27]
Each type has a habitual **"focus of attention"** (its most prominent patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving) and a central motivating **"passion"** or **"chief feature."**

**The Passions (boxed sidebar) [pp.21-22]:** one of nine per type; the central emotional-motivational issue. They are emotional, often unconscious drivers based on an implicit view of what you need to survive and how to get it. Because they spring from a sense of lack, they create a basic dilemma or trap around which personality organizes itself while striving for a need that never gets fulfilled. They motivate action out of hunger yet obstruct real fulfillment, because we only obtain what we really need by transcending the personality's limited purview; only becoming conscious of motivations breaks the cycle. ### The Twenty-Seven Subtypes [pp.28-35]

- **Definition:** three versions of each type, according to how the type's passion combines with one of **three instinctual biases or goals shared by all social creatures**: Self-Preservation, Social Interaction, Sexual (One-to-One) Bonding. Passion plus dominant instinct yields a more specific focus of attention reflecting "a particular insatiable need that drives behavior." [p.28]

**The three instincts ("Our Animal Drives") [p.28]:**

| Instinct | Focus | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-Preservation** | Survival and material security: safety, enough resources, avoiding danger, basic structure and well-being | Beyond basics, emphasis on other kinds of security depending on type once mixed with the passion |
| **Social Interaction** | Belonging, recognition, relationships in social groups: "get along with the herd" (family, community, groups); also power or standing relative to others in the group | Meaning depends on type |
| **Sexual Bonding** ("One-to-One") | Quality and status of relationships with specific individuals: sexual connection, attraction, bonding; well-being through one-to-one connection | Meaning depends on type |

All three operate in everyone, but usually one dominates; when it serves the passion, it yields a more nuanced character, the subtype. [p.28]

**The Countertypes (boxed sidebar) [p.29]:** in each type, two subtypes go with the flow of the passion's energy and one is "upside-down," going against the main energetic direction of the passion. This "counter-passional" subtype is the **countertype**. Best-known example: the "counter-phobic" **Sexual Six**, a Six who is unafraid; the passion is fear, but this subtype goes against fear by being strong and intimidating.

**The Subtypes in Brief [p.29]:** the passage highlights two things: the movement from center to passion to instinct that defines each personality, and the countertype of each triple. Each is developed in the type chapters.

**Subtype table.** Each subtype carries a name or theme word (the author's subtype labels). "(CT)" marks the countertype.

### Charting Your Personal Growth Path [pp.35-37]

- Once you know your type and subtype, "Now what?" The Enneagram is "a model of transformation that indicates a path for growth." The sequence: first remember to observe what we do, then inquire into why and how, then actively work against old habits and toward higher aspects, an ongoing learning process aimed at conscious choice. [p.35]

**Three tasks (the structure used in the last sections of each type chapter):**
1. **Self-observation:** the path to dis-identifying from personality. It is "about creating space"; witness habitual patterns as they happen. In the type chapters she highlights specific patterns to observe. [pp.35-36]
2. **Self-inquiry and self-reflection:** understand the root causes and consequences of observed patterns. She identifies each type's **"key issues"** and offers ideas about **"what to do."** [p.36]
3. **Self-development:** actively working to achieve change; awareness of central patterns, sources and consequences lets one make new choices. [p.36]

**Two "Enneagramatic paths" of transformation** offered in each chapter's self-development section [p.36]:
1. **The Inner Flow:** the change process following the arrow lines within the diagram.
2. **The "Vice to Virtue" path:** the vertical path of understanding one's Vice (the unconscious operation of the passion) to move toward embodying the higher Virtue (the antidote to the passion). She also gives each subtype specific tasks on this journey toward the true self. [p.37]

**Inner Flow, Chestnut's own theory [pp.36-37].** (The diagram on p.36 shows the nine points on the circle with the arrow lines of the inner triangle 9-3-6 and hexad 1-4-2-8-5-7; it is shown only as a figure.)
- The arrow lines point in the direction of each type's specific path of psychological and spiritual growth, and away from characteristics and experiences repressed in childhood (to which we periodically return for security).
- **The point ahead of our core point** along the arrow lines represents key challenges to master to become more whole.
- **The point behind our core type** along the arrow lines represents issues from the past to re-integrate, reclaiming what was disowned in childhood to ground and support forward movement.
- She reinterprets the traditional "stress" or "defensive" point (the point ahead, where we go in distress) as an opportunity for growth: she names it the **"growth-(through)-stress"** or **"stress-growth" point**, and invites consciously manifesting its high-side qualities. The traditional "security" or "heart" point (behind our core point) she calls the **"child-heart" point**: parts of ourselves left behind in childhood, occasionally returned to under stress or security as a resource, which can be consciously re-owned. [p.37]

**Chapter 1 Conclusion [pp.37-38]:** the personality is a necessary survival mechanism, not all of who we are; if we are honest about what we do, avoid knowing we do, and the adaptive strategies that limit us, we can take part in what Cynthia Bourgeault calls "the dance of self-manifestation," the "cosmic task entrusted to us as human beings." Recognizing the Shadow, consciously bearing suffering we defend against, and accepting all of who we are opens endless possibilities. Chapter 2 tells the story of the wisdom tradition behind the Enneagram.

## Chapter 2: The Enneagram as a Universal Symbol of an Ancient Teaching: The Perennial Wisdom View of the Human Purpose [pp.39-56]

**Epigraph** [p.39]: Cynthia Bourgeault, *The Wisdom Way of Knowing*: Wisdom is an ancient tradition "at the headwaters of all the great sacred paths," flowing from wisdom schools like an underground stream, giving guidance and occasional "sharp course corrections" to human history.

### Opening: crisis of meaning and the perennial wisdom [pp.39-40]
- Humanity suffers a "crisis of meaning": more money, status or inner peace leave us empty; we struggle to connect to truths that let us know, love and accept ourselves and others and understand our place in the human collective and universe.
- The wisdom behind the Enneagram rests on a universal understanding of human purpose and possibility found in the root teachings of the world's major spiritual traditions and many ancient philosophies. Religious studies scholar **Huston Smith** calls it the "forgotten truth" of "human unanimity"; it begins with "Know Thyself." Smith argues this perennial wisdom gives a "pattern" or "model of reality" we need to orient ourselves; the Enneagram is a visible geometric model of the unity beneath the world's religions, "a viable pattern for our time." [pp.39-40]
- **"Ancient wisdom behind the Enneagram"** in her usage = the **perennial philosophy** as **Aldous Huxley** defines it: a metaphysical philosophy and practical psychology that "finds something in the soul similar to, even identical with, divine Reality." The Enneagram is one of the forms in which it is encoded; using it reduces anxiety and increases meaning. [p.40]

### The Perennial Wisdom Teaching Expressed by the Enneagram [pp.40-44]

**Parable of the acorns (from Bourgeault, quoted at length) [pp.40-41].** A kingdom of modern, Westernized, midlife acorns busy with self-help (seminars like "Getting All You Can out of Your Shell," woundedness groups for acorns bruised in the fall from the tree, spas for oiling shells, "acornopathic therapies"). A knotty, capless, dirty stranger arrives, points up at the oak, and says, "We...are...that!" Asked how to become that tree, he points down: "going into the ground...and cracking open the shell." The acorns reply: "Insane...Totally morbid! Why, then we wouldn't be acorns anymore."

**Her reading [p.41]:** before self-development we are the seeds of what we may become. To transform from "acorn-self" to "oak tree-Self" we traverse our "underground territory," allow defenses to crack open and break down, and integrate disowned feelings, blind spots and Shadow traits to shed the limiting outer shell of personality. Nature brings us part of the way; full potential needs conscious effort, and the Enneagram can guide it. "Knowledge is a function of being," so improving the world requires advancing capacity for consciousness.

**The Vision: the wisdom view of humanity and the universe [pp.41-42]**
- Each human has a **"divine spark,"** an inherent capacity for a higher state of being interconnected with the living universe. Human life, nature and the universe are ordered by the same laws, principles and patterns; seeing them brings wholeness within and unity with the natural world.
- As we grow we identify with a **"personal ego,"** both a false (acorn) self necessary for survival and a vehicle for self-work toward the larger (oak tree) Self. A useful separation can be created between false self and the rest of consciousness by a concerted effort to know our depths. Through the space between the pure awareness of the inner witness and the habits of the false self we **"dis-identify"** with personality programming and make more conscious choices directed by and supportive of the higher Self.
- Huxley's perennial philosophy begins with the Sanskrit **"That art thou"** (central to Hindu thought): "That" can be called divine source, the Tao, or the Absolute Principle of existence. Human purpose: each person discovers this higher Self and finds out (and becomes) "Who we really are." The roughed-up acorn was saying this.

**The Way: the wisdom view of the process of transformation mapped by the Enneagram [pp.42-44]**
- The wisdom way to freedom, peace and wholeness starts with active effort to know yourself, requiring discipline, hard work and sustaining motivation.
- **Three basic steps mapped on the inner triangle (3, 6, 9) of the Enneagram diagram [p.42]:**

| Step | Point | Core theme |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Three** | Dis-identification from the Personality through self-observation |
| 2 | **Six** | Surrendering to the fear and emotional suffering associated with loosening ego defenses (put down defenses, integrate the Shadow) |
| 3 | **Nine** | Actively working toward transcendence and union (rise above the personality's limited view, merge with greater wholeness and peace) |

1. **Dis-identification (Three) [pp.42-43]:** meditative practice stills the mind to create inner "space" to watch yourself, separating the witnessing consciousness from observed habits. Over time the hold of old patterns loosens and different choices become possible. It demands **humility** because the Personality "wants to stay in charge"; like a machine or sleeper it can only grow to a point. Challenging the Personality's control starts building "a conscious center of gravity" within, "the nucleus of your higher or 'greater' or essential Self."
2. **Surrendering to fear and suffering (Six) [p.43-44]:** as you challenge the personality's dominance, fear arises (fear is what keeps personality patterns in place). The step is willingness to feel this fear rather than retreat to old patterns, and to experience the emotional territory under the defenses consciously, thereby integrating the Shadow (disowned, warded-off feelings). Some level of "conscious suffering" is necessarily part of growth; like the acorn, one must break open. Fear can take many forms including resistance to change; it must be respected, but also surrendered to, faced and worked through. Recognizing patterns and feeling what personality avoided releases the energy spent maintaining defenses. Hence the tradition says growth is hard work requiring effort and humility; fortitude opens "a whole new way of being."
3. **Transcendence and union (Nine) [p.44]:** with ongoing self-observation, self-study and self-development one gradually attains greater freedom, connectedness, balance, wholeness and creativity of living from the higher Self, a more conscious, integrated state available to all who dis-identify and face fear and pain; through meditative practice and conscious self-work one merges with something larger. Reflecting the "high side" of the Nine point: union with the true Self, with others and with the natural world. Defenses block pain but also positive experiences (receiving love, true union); loosening them brings receptivity to love and the ability to call on the strategies and strengths of all nature's archetypes instead of a single way of doing things.
- Next chapters will show how the Enneagram symbolizes this path through message and mathematical-geometric structure. [p.44]

### The Enneagram as Universal Symbol: Ancient Wisdom Expressed through Mathematics and Sacred Geometry [pp.44-45]
- Ancient philosophers saw arithmetic and geometry as expressing archetypal principles, clues to a deeper understanding of the universe; numbers were studied for recurring meaningful patterns in nature, not as "servants of commerce." **Pythagoras and Plato** believed numbers and shapes symbolized divine principles; math was central to "the deep cosmic canon of [the] design" of nature. In ancient Greece math was the foundation of all other fields (philosophy, art, psychology) because symbolic mathematics gives "a map of our own inner psychological and sacred spiritual structure." The Enneagram's power comes from this tradition: geometry as sacred, revealing archetypal patterning that structures growth of living things (flower petals, fern unfurling, sea shell dimensions, the psyche). [pp.44-45]

### The Symbolic Meaning of the Enneagram [pp.45-46]
- "Enneagram" = "drawing of nine"; a nine-pointed star inscribed in a circle; exact origin unknown, thought to be thousands of years old.
- **Three symbolic forms (illustrated on p.45 as a separate circle, triangle and hexad figure):**

| Form | Symbolizes | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Circle** | "The law of one" / unity | Eternity |
| **Inner triangle** | "The Law of Three" / trinity | Creation |
| **Hexad** | "The Law of Seven" | Steps in a process, as in the octave of the musical scale; cyclic, ordered functioning |

- The symbol is structured by the combination of the Law of Three and the Law of Seven inscribed in a circle (unified whole). Together they say the universe is perpetually developing and an essential unity lies behind the multiplicity of forms.
- **Gurdjieff** (one of the main sources on the Enneagram): "for the man who is able to make use of it, the enneagram makes books and libraries totally unnecessary." He told students everything can be read in it; a man alone in a desert can trace it in the sand and read the eternal laws of the universe. He described it as one of many symbols designed by ancient spiritual masters to communicate "objective knowledge" of humankind and the universe across generations. [p.45]
- Gurdjieff claimed modern people lost knowledge known long ago; those who had it transmitted it in stories, parables and diagrams like the Enneagram, which could pass without alteration from school to school and era to era. [p.46]

### The Circle and the Law of One [pp.46-47]
- For ancient mathematical philosophers unity was "a philosophic concept and a mystic experience expressible mathematically" in the circle. The circle around the diagram represents unity, wholeness and the natural order.
- "All is one" is shown by the fact that any number multiplied or divided by one stays itself: "Unity always preserves the identity of all it encounters." The Law of One as circle was a metaphor for cosmic creation from a dimensionless center (point or source) expanding equally in all directions.
- The circle also conveys **enclosure**: boundaries around a process or realm. The Enneagram's circle encloses the developmental process of an individual; inside is the finite nature of the Personality, outside is "the infinite" beyond the comprehension of the conditioned Personality. [pp.46-47]

### The Inner Triangle and the Law of Three [pp.47-49]
- Epigraph from *The Theology of Arithmetic*: "The triad is the form of the completion of all things."
- **Law of Three:** three forces, an **active**, a **passive** and a **neutralizing (reconciling)** force, must enter into every kind of creation. Example: sailboat (boat = passive, wind = active, sail = reconciling). Physicists call this "action, reaction, and resultant." [pp.46-47]
- **Gurdjieff:** every phenomenon manifests three forces; one or two alone cannot produce anything. When something is stuck and not coming to completion, a third force is often lacking; until we transcend ego-based viewpoint we usually cannot perceive the third force. [p.47]
- **In psychological growth:** change is hard (quitting smoking, resolving conflict, losing weight, changing a relationship dynamic) because we react in a habitual, mechanical way (action and reaction). Bringing in a "third force" lets us shift. Michael Schneider: "opposites are balanced by a third, mediating element that reconciles a conflict, healing the split of polarity and transforming separate parts into a complete and successful whole." A properly chosen third factor synthesizes opposites. [p.47]
- The triangle is the geometric representation of the Law of Three. **Trinities across traditions [p.47]:** Hindu Brahma (Creator), Vishnu (Preserver), Shiva (Destroyer); Christian Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit); Buddhist **three poisons** (ignorance or delusion, craving, aversion), which she parallels to the three core points: ignorance/unconsciousness = Nine, aversion = Six, craving/desire = Three. Other "threefoldness": birth, life, death; three dimensions of space and three of time (past, present, future); the three primary colors.
- **Threefoldness within the Enneagram [p.48]:** three "brains"/centers marked by the three points of the inner triangle; three types per center; three subtypes per type; 27 = "three threes" or three cubed (3 x 3 x 3) for the whole "psychological Enneagram." Also, three main types usable for change: the core type and the two connected to it by the inner flow arrow lines.
- **The inner triangle as the three forces needed to bring anything into being [p.48]:**

| Point | Force | Why (her reasoning) |
|---|---|---|
| **Three** | Proactive / advancing force | Threes focus on actively "doing" |
| **Six** | Resistant force / "antithesis" / resisting force | Sixes resist through questioning, doubting and testing |
| **Nine** | Reconciling force | A key Nine trait is bringing different ideas together to create harmony and consensus |

### The Hexad and the Law of Seven [pp.48-49]
- Epigraph: Tao Te Ching Chapter 1, "Origins" (trans. David Burke): those free of the passions observe the signs of the making; those who crave the passions are blinded to the signs and see only effects. [pp.48-49]
- The Law of Three shows how something new comes into being; the **Law of Seven** shows how things happen as a process or sequence of steps. A "law of order" distinct from the "law of creation." Law of Three = something created purposely or intentionally; Law of Seven = a cyclical process of transformation. Ancient mathematicians took seven as symbolic of transformation. Heraclitus: the only constant is change; no one steps twice into the same river. [p.49]
- The movement along the arrow lines (the hexad lines) indicates specific steps in a cycle of transformation, comparable to the organic unfolding of nature as in "The Way" or Tao of the Tao Te Ching. **Personality** as represented by the Enneagram is a form of getting stuck or **"fixated"** at one point along this natural path of change: a defense that protects us but also resists the natural flow or rhythm of life. [p.49]
- (The diagram on p.49 is a hexad figure; not described in text.)

### The Significance of Nine [pp.49-51]
- Epigraph: Michael S. Schneider: "Nine represents the boundary between the mundane and the transcendental infinite."
- The circle of nine points is a boundary between the finite, mundane nature of human life (as experienced by the Personality) and the infinite beyond human consciousness. Nine is the final number in base-10 with a specific identity, symbolizing highest achievement in an endeavor, "the utmost boundary." The ancient Greeks called nine **"the horizon"**: it encloses or binds the essential elements of any event into a whole. **A. G. E. Blake:** the apex point 9 is unique because it "represents the beginning and end of the cycle...that which gives the form of the whole. It is the source of the purpose that has to be realized." [p.50]
- **Correlations with classic texts [p.50-51]:**
  - **Kabbalah:** the "Tree of Life" has ten Sefirot (numbers representing divine principles); nine correspond directly to the nine Enneagram archetypes. The tenth, Keter, is placed above the diagram because it reflects the Messiah's soul and does not relate to the human Personality.
  - **Homer's *Odyssey*:** structured by the nine "lands" Odysseus visits on his way home; each presents obstacles to overcome. The journey of homecoming is a metaphor for the inner journey to the true self. She states the lands and their characters parallel exactly the issues and traits of the nine personalities, visited in the same order as they appear counterclockwise around the Enneagram. In the *Odyssey* and *Iliad* a person's main identifying characteristic usually leads to their undoing, mirroring the Enneagram's view that each type's greatest strength can also be its fatal flaw. (The type chapters each open with a corresponding Odyssey episode; Chapter 3 begins with the Lotus-Eaters for Nine.)
  - **Dante's *Divine Comedy*:** versions of the nine passions appear in the "sins" or unconscious patterns that bar humans from Heaven. Threefold structure (Inferno, Purgatory, Paradise) and nine levels in the sections of the underworld's moral geography parallel themes the Enneagram maps: **dis-identification (Inferno), purification through conscious suffering (Purgatory), transcendence (Paradise).** The Inferno shows Dante the Pilgrim learning about "sins," the unconscious drives of the passions, portrayed in the "underworld" (the unconscious or Shadow). In the type chapters, Inferno examples illustrate each passion's character and effect when we do not become aware of it; the punishments are exacted in the underworld of our unconsciousness when we fail to face the Shadow. [pp.50-51]

### The Modern Rediscovery of the Ancient Wisdom Encoded in the Enneagram [pp.51-55]
- Chestnut first encountered the Enneagram in 1990, amazed at how precisely it described her. Learning its origins deepened her personal understanding. [p.51]
- **J. G. Bennett** (a Gurdjieff student and modern source): this "cosmic secret of perpetual self-renewal" was discovered more than four thousand years ago in Mesopotamia by a brotherhood of wise men and passed down. [p.51]
- Rediscovery through **three key individuals**, each presenting Enneagram ideas to small student groups in the context of psychological and spiritual growth: **G. I. Gurdjieff** (Russia and Europe, from the early 1900s); **Oscar Ichazo** (Chile, 1960s); **Claudio Naranjo** (Berkeley, California, after briefly studying with Ichazo in 1970). [p.51]

**G. I. Gurdjieff [pp.52-53]**
- Taught the Enneagram symbol and a practical self-work called **"The Work"** or **"The Fourth Way"** from the early 1900s. Presented a system of "hidden" knowledge from many sources, primarily esoteric Christianity. He did **not** connect the Enneagram symbol to psychological types, beyond three types of individuals tied to the three centers of intelligence.
- Born in Russian Armenia around 1866; his father a Greek bard, possibly linked to "an oral tradition stretching back to mankind's past." As a young man he traveled widely (Middle East, Asia, Egypt, North Africa) contacting secret societies and hermetic organizations (religious, philosophical, occult, political, mystic). Began teaching in Russia in 1914.
- Controversial; part of his method was obfuscating through his self-presentation to challenge ordinary thinking; he purposefully triggered students' habitual Personality reactions to show mechanical, automatic functioning.
- Portrayed humankind as in a waking sleep; the Fourth Way offers a path out of mechanical functioning through self-knowledge. Quotation: "It is possible to stop being a machine, but for that it is necessary first of all to know the machine...When a machine knows itself it is then no longer a machine...It already begins to be responsible for its actions." [p.52]
- Work on self requires strenuous effort because the mechanical state makes falling back asleep likely. Personality = the ways we buffer ourselves against the reality of our lives, maintaining an illusion about what we can "do." Through active self-observation and intentional **self-remembering** one could evolve past the mechanical state. [pp.52-53]
- Each person has a **"chief feature,"** also called the "passion," central to the Personality: "an axle around which the 'false personality' revolves." Each person's work is "struggling against this chief fault." Because people have different chief features there cannot be one general set of rules for growth: "What is useful for one is harmful for another. One man talks too much; he must learn to keep silent. Another man is silent when he ought to talk and he must learn to talk." [p.53]

**Oscar Ichazo [pp.53-54]**
- The Enneagram of psychological types as widely used today comes from Ichazo. Originally from Bolivia; first taught his program of psychological and spiritual development in Chile in the 1950s and '60s. Taught the core ideas of the "Enneagram of personality" under the name **"Protoanalysis,"** meant to bring about transformation by methods based on science, reason and rationality.
- Cites few sources other than saying he developed it himself; references Aristotle and neo-Platonism; thought influenced by studies in the near and far East, especially Afghanistan; says the knowledge behind his **Arica School** came "from many sources."
- Protoanalysis is based on the questions: What is humankind? What is the Supreme Good of humanity? What is the Truth that gives meaning and value to human life? He calls it scientific because it asks no faith, but studies, measures and analyzes the psyche. 1978 interview: Arica provides a new theory of the psyche and a method for making the psyche "fulfilled and clear," to handle problems faster, easier, clearer. [pp.53-54]
- **"Enneagram of Fixations"** (as distinct from the many other "Enneagrams" in the Arica work): groupings of psychological traits resulting from some early injury to the psyche and a corresponding compensation. **Fixations** = points where "our psyche has been wrongly predisposed," focused on a lack (unmet need or trauma) that colors and defines a type. [p.54]
- Arica teaching: each individual has a core type or "fixation" in **each of the three centers**, so personality is described as a **"Trifix"**, a triad of types, one per center, by a specific formula. (Chestnut's example: her Trifix is 2-7-1.) [p.54]
- Like Gurdjieff, Ichazo calls fixated ego behavior mechanical: "the work of an automaton whose behavior is dramatically obvious and repetitive." [p.54]

**Claudio Naranjo [pp.54-55]**
- Chilean-born, American-trained psychiatrist; learned the model from Ichazo at Arica, Chile, in 1970. Afterward founded in Berkeley a development group called **SAT (Seekers After Truth**, an allusion to Gurdjieff's work). He asked members to keep the Enneagram confidential; it leaked out and reached the mass market when the first books describing the personality types appeared in the late 1980s. [p.54]
- Well placed to bring it to a wider audience: a key figure in the 1970s US human potential movement; worked with **Fritz Perls** (founder of Gestalt therapy, emphasizing present-moment and bodily awareness). Already knew psychoanalytic psychology, existential therapies, **Karen Horney's** personality theory, Gurdjieff's Fourth Way, Sufism and Buddhist meditation. He developed the Enneagram further, communicating it through existing psychological and spiritual lenses and weaving it into a more refined description of the personalities based on Ichazo's "Protoanalytic" process. [pp.54-55]
- Like Ichazo, he did not teach the Enneagram as a stand-alone tool but as one piece of a wider program of self-work. Through his writings he presents it as an overarching theory integrating many Western psychology and Eastern spiritual trends. **This book is based on Naranjo's interpretation of the types and subtypes**; most published Enneagram content derives most directly from Naranjo even though he credits Ichazo as source. [p.55]

### Conclusion: Where We Are and Where We Wish to Be [pp.55-56]
- The same wisdom appears throughout history: philosophy on eternal questions, Eastern texts on humanity's relation to the "ground of being," epic poetry portraying estrangement from self as the start of a journey of homecoming, insights of Western psychology.
- **The Enneagram as a model of wholeness:** each point describes individual personalities and also certain universal archetypal elements. Each point expresses a **range** (a vertical dimension): a higher aspect when fixated aspects are loosened and transcended, and a lower side of more unconscious, automatic, "fixated" functioning.
- **Growth** per the perennial philosophy and the Enneagram: observing ourselves, suffering through fears and disowned Shadow aspects, and working to manifest higher potentials. The following chapters describe each archetype and its "journey of homecoming," tracing the development of the Personality, the three subtype forms and the path toward liberation, "a collective effort to increase the level of human consciousness." [pp.55-56]
