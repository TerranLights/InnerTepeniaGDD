## Overview of this range

After the Point One chapter the book has no further teaching chapter. It ends with a very short conclusion to Chapter 11 and then a single substantial **Appendix, "Help with Identifying Your Enneagram Type: Discerning the Difference Between Pairs of Types"** (pp.393 to 430). This is the real content of the back matter. It takes every one of the 36 possible pairings of the nine types and, for each pair, states what makes the two types look alike and what actually distinguishes them. The distinctions are drawn on recurring axes: how each type relates to anger and other feelings, whether attention is self-referencing or other-referencing, attitude to authority and rules, relation to work and play, how conflict is handled, how each defines quality or "good," what each avoids, and how each treats boundaries, commitments and time. Following the appendix are the notes (pp.431 to 441), the reference list (pp.442 to 445), acknowledgments (pp.446 to 447), an author biography (p.448) and a contents listing (p.449).

Terminology the appendix uses and takes for granted from the body of the book: "core type," "wings," "arrow lines," subtypes (Self-Preservation, Social, Sexual; the Six subtypes are also called phobic, counterphobic [Sexual] and Social), "centers"/"triads" (body/gut, heart, head, also called mental types and "fear types"), "self-referencing" versus "other-referencing," "leaking" of repressed anger, "intellectualize," "equalize authority" (Sevens), "image management," "go along to get along" (Nines).

## 1. Chapter 11 closing: "Conclusion" [p.392]

The author places a three-sentence conclusion at the end of the Point One chapter:

- The One point stands for "the energy of anger in the service of virtue" and for the ways we try to control the "beast within" to cope with a world that requires us to meet standards in order to be loved.
- Its growth path shows how to transform anger into energy that helps us manifest our ideals.
- In each of the three Type One subtypes we see a specific character who shows what is possible when "righteous anger" turns into "calm acceptance" through "the alchemy of self-observation, self-development, and self-knowledge."

Context noted only so the conclusion is understood (not extracted): immediately before it, the chapter's closing growth section has a "grit, grind, and grace" frame for self-work and a short paragraph of advice for each One subtype on the path from the vice (anger) to the virtue (serenity) [pp.391 to 392]. In compact terms: Self-Preservation Ones are told to slow down and trace where worry comes from, challenge beliefs in their own "badness," and relax their need to control; Social Ones to remember that there is no ultimately right or perfect way and that there are many good ways, so they need not prove worth through teaching; Sexual Ones to look at the motives beneath the wish to perfect others and to know themselves first before offering their love of "the right way." ## 2. Appendix: Help with Identifying Your Enneagram Type: Discerning the Difference Between Pairs of Types [pp.393 to 430]

### 2.1 Introduction to the appendix [p.393]

Why typing is hard (the author's reasons):
- Everyone has blind spots that make it hard to recognize oneself in a type description.
- Inner experience of who we are often differs from how we are viewed from outside.
- We may relate to several types because we do have experiences of several types, not only the core type: overlapping traits with the **wing** types (on either side of the core type), with the types we connect to by the **arrow lines**, and with the types of our parents.
- We hold a vision of who we would like to be and be seen as, which differs from how we actually are now.

Method she recommends: often one cannot be sure at once but can narrow to two or three types; the material is meant to show similarities and differences between specific pairs. Step one is recognizing yourself in the pattern of one of the nine types; step two is exploring the three subtype versions in each type chapter. A "big advantage" of Naranjo's subtype descriptions is that they give more specific, nuanced information for describing your own habits.

The appendix is organized as 36 pairs, in order: One with Two through Nine; Two with Three through Nine; and so on, ending with Eight and Nine. Each pair has a "look-alike" paragraph and a "differences" paragraph; they are given here in compact form. Subtype exceptions the author flags are included.

### 2.2 Quick axes that recur across pairs (author's recurring distinctions, gathered for use)

| Axis | How the appendix uses it |
|---|---|
| Source of judgment | Ones judge by an internal standard; Twos, Threes and Fours are more affected by how others see them (Fours also by an inner sense of the special/authentic); Fives by objective analysis; Sixes by doubt; Sevens by what feels good/possible. |
| Self- vs. other-referencing | Other-referencing: Twos, Nines (Threes read others to build an image). Self-referencing: Fours, Fives, Sevens. |
| Anger | Ones leak it (resentment, irritation); Eights express it freely and widely; Nines have little contact with it; Twos feel it when needs go unmet; Threes when blocked from a goal; Fives mainly when boundaries are crossed; counterphobic Sixes use it to face threats. |
| Authority | Ones obey; Eights rebel openly; Sixes question or rebel (Social Six excepted); Sevens "equalize"; Nines go along (with quiet passive resistance); Threes work with it if it does not block goals; Fives follow if they choose, otherwise resist quietly; Twos seek good relations with it; Fours can be nonconformist. |
| Work and play | Ones: work before play. Sevens: planning for play first. Threes: can be workaholics. Eights: overwork and forget limits. Fives: conserve energy. Nines: inertia, distraction. |
| Conflict | Eights engage easily; Fours can engage or even create it; counterphobic Sixes move toward it; Twos, Nines and phobic Sixes avoid it (Nines most). |
| Vulnerable feelings | Eights deny them; Fours feel them regularly; Threes and Fives avoid or detach from them; Twos can express them (sometimes manipulatively). |

### 2.3 Ones paired with each other type

**Ones and Twos [pp.393 to 395]**
- *Look alike:* both have rules they expect others to follow and become upset and reactive when others do not; both are dutiful and want to be seen as "good" and "responsible"; both can be self-critical and critical of others.
- *Differences:* Ones have far more rules, over a wider range of behavior (work style and product, organization, conduct in many situations, dress codes); Twos' rules concern how people should treat one another. The One's inner critic is active "eighty to ninety percent of the time or more"; the Two's criticism is less frequent and triggered by distressing events such as rejection or feeling one failed others. "Good" for a One means doing everything right with few mistakes, and "responsible" means keeping commitments, delivering on time, being punctual; for a Two "good" means thoughtful, considerate, selfless, and "responsible" means being available whenever others need them. Ones speak definitively and offer opinions and judgments, using words like *should, ought, right, wrong, appropriate* constantly; Twos speak softly, ask questions, draw people in, give advice, and make people feel important. Ones can be warm but rarely as consistently warm and empathic as Twos.
- *Key test:* Ones look inward to judge whether they did well; Twos are more affected by how others perceive them and read nonverbal cues and reactions closely.

**Ones and Threes [p.395]**
- *Look alike:* both task-focused, wish to excel and be seen as competent, both emphasize tasks over relationships and organize work toward objectives.
- *Differences:* the One's drive comes from inner satisfaction at having done the task as well as possible; the Three's from needing to have succeeded in others' eyes (salary, raises, office trappings, the responses of important people). Ones seek self-respect by their own standards; Threes seek admiration using external reference points. Ones enjoy structuring work in refined detail; Threes treat objectives as items to check off and devise the most efficient plan, less structured than a One's. Ones may procrastinate for fear of error; Threes want the fastest efficient route and worry less about mistakes. Quality: Ones define it as the best job they can do with no errors, even if no one else would notice; Threes define it as meeting customer expectations and going slightly beyond. For a Three (except the Self-Preservation Three) perfection on every task wastes resources and "good enough" is good enough; for a One, "good enough" is rarely good enough.

**Ones and Fours [pp.395 to 396]**
- *Look alike:* both take work seriously and want to do the best job; both idealistic and appreciate quality; both base judgments on an internal sense of the ideal; both self-critical.
- *Differences:* Ones focus on structure, process, details; Fours on relationships, people and their own creative expression. Ones aim at perfection by their own standards; Fours value creativity, authenticity and aesthetics over a specific ideal of perfection, and are far more conscious of how things look to others. The One's critic gives running commentary on how things could be more perfect, often with little emotion beyond mild irritation (grammar errors, misalignment); the Four feels something fundamentally flawed within, notices what is missing in a situation and in the self, and reacts more deeply to what is "not good enough." Ones tend toward black-and-white, "one right way"; Fours see many ways to approach a project. Fours attend to the emotional quality of interactions and connection; Ones to the structure of relationships and shared tasks. Emotional tone: Ones are reserved, controlled, concise and precise, sometimes annoyed at rule-breakers; Fours show varied, prominent moods, empathy and sometimes drama.

**Ones and Fives [p.397]**
- *Look alike:* both reserved, logical, task-focused, serious or withdrawn at times; value independence and self-sufficiency (Fives need more privacy); both seek knowledge (Fives because knowledge is power, Ones to be competent, informed and correct); both excel at objective analysis (Ones to be responsible and prevent mistakes; Fives because they detach from feelings when analyzing); both understand boundaries; diligent and practical; both use internal standards.
- *Differences:* Ones are more rules-based; Fives value simplicity and conserving resources over a set of rules. Ones are much more self-critical (a critic commenting on nearly everything) and more judgmental of others, openly angry or irritated when people do not do things "the right way." Ones' feelings leak out more; Fives keep a calm, unruffled reserve even under stress and rarely share emotions, especially at work. Ones experience some version of anger fairly regularly; Fives automatically detach.

**Ones and Sixes [pp.397 to 398]**
- *Look alike:* both strong analytical thinkers who worry about things going wrong (Ones about mistakes; Sixes about many things in general). Ones respond by trying to be perfect; Sixes catastrophize and imagine worst cases. Both are uncomfortable with success and create problems in completing tasks (Ones because nothing is ever perfect and they constantly criticize themselves; Sixes because they doubt themselves and think success makes one a target). Both are often activists (Ones from responsibility to improve the world; Sixes from identifying with underdogs and being sensitive to unjust use of authority).
- *Differences:* Ones worry about being wrong by their own standards; Sixes about danger and external threats. Ones are self-critical and judge others; Sixes doubt themselves and others. Ones try and fail to be perfect; Sixes try and fail to find certainty (or find it in a specific authority). **Authority is the stark contrast:** Ones obey authority and follow rules; Sixes are suspicious and may rebel and most question rules (exception: the Social Six, who adheres to an outside authority and may follow its rules strictly). Both procrastinate: Ones want more time to perfect; Sixes are stalled by doubting. Trust: Ones give people the benefit of the doubt unless they break rules; Sixes mistrust at first until trust is earned, then are very loyal.

**Ones and Sevens [pp.398 to 399]**
- *Look alike:* both quality-oriented (Ones in standards of work; Sevens in seeking the best of everything, especially recreationally); idealistic and visionary; high energy; both can be perfectionistic (Ones more consistently; Sevens can drop the effort if it gets onerous); intellectual, analytical, like problem-solving; both sensitive to criticism (the One shows it more).
- *Differences:* for Ones work comes before play; for Sevens planning for play and pleasure is primary (though Sevens can be dedicated and make work enjoyable). Ones look for ways to improve things and so can seem less than optimistic; Sevens are relentlessly optimistic and want to focus on the positive, so a One's attention to errors seems too negative to them. Ones like structure and can work within limits and in defined authority structures; Sevens dislike limits and hierarchy and tend to equalize authority. Ones excel at managing project details; Sevens find them tedious. In relationships Ones can be critical or inflexible but are committed to self-improvement, listen to feedback and work at the relationship; Sevens bring positive energy and fun but can feel challenged when difficulties must be worked through.

**Ones and Eights [pp.399 to 400]**
- *Look alike:* high-energy, hardworking, like control and order; both get angry; both think in black-and-white or all-or-nothing terms; both concerned about justice and fairness and work hard for a cause; both can overwork and neglect their own needs.
- *Differences:* Ones believe showing anger is wrong and hold it back, but it leaks as resentment, irritation, annoyance or passive-aggressive behavior; Eights feel and express anger readily and do not think it wrong. Ones get angry mainly when people break rules or behave badly; Eights over a wider range of reasons. Ones control through rules, structure, standards; Eights exercise power directly. Eights think big-picture and dislike detail; Ones excel at and may enjoy detail. Ones aim at perfection; Eights can be satisfied with "good enough." Eights go with impulses, can be excessive, dislike inhibition; Ones over-control impulses and delay pleasure. Eights are "under-social" (do not mind going against convention); Ones are "over-social" (almost always observe conventions). Ones are very self-critical; Eights criticize themselves little and act quickly, feeling freer to use power and will. Ones apologize if they think they erred (and value apologies); Eights rarely feel apologetic. Ones obey authority; Eights do not like being told what to do and may rebel. Speech: Ones are polite and restrained, using *should, ought, must*; Eights are direct, abrupt, intimidating, even profane.

**Ones and Nines [pp.400 to 401]**
- *Look alike:* both appreciate structure and process at work; both make good mediators (Nines see many sides and want harmony; Ones have standards of fairness and can be objective, discerning judges); both have difficulty noticing and asserting their own needs and wants; both can be perfectionistic (Ones more); both can work within and respect existing authority (Nines sometimes rebel passively if they feel controlled).
- *Differences:* Ones are opinionated and often believe in the one right way; Nines have trouble locating their own opinion because they are so attuned to others' perspectives, and do not assert a position. Ones see black and white; Nines see many shades of gray. Both want to avoid conflict, Nines more; a One who feels strongly may be unable to stop arguing. Ones do things their way; Nines adapt and prefer to follow another's agenda. In getting tasks done Ones rely on internal standards of the ideal; Nines are oriented to what others think and want and seek consensus on what the standards should be. Ones observe rules and will confront those who do not; Nines are easygoing and unlikely to confront.

### 2.4 Twos paired with each other type

**Twos and Threes [pp.401 to 402]**
- *Look alike:* both manage image and presentation to please or attract others; both competent doers with active energy; both want others' approval; both can be confused about who they really are because so much energy goes into maintaining an image; both avoid emotions (Threes because feeling gets in the way of doing; Twos because it gets in the way of forging connections).
- *Differences:* Twos focus on meeting others' needs and being friendly and accommodating; Threes on achieving goals and success to win admiration. Twos are relationship-oriented; Threes task-oriented. Threes are motivated by the good feeling of reaching a goal and appearing successful; Twos by earning affection and being indispensable. Twos numb feelings less completely and feel and express more emotion than Threes. Threes are competitive and value winning; Twos value aligning with others over coming out on top. Anger: Twos when unacknowledged needs go unmet; Threes when someone blocks a goal. Threes can be workaholics; Twos work hard but also prioritize relationships and pleasure. Threes are efficiency-focused and may be unable to be present to listen when goal-fixed; Twos tune into people, even at their own expense, and may abandon their own goals to support others'. What each avoids most: Twos avoid rejection, working behind the scenes at positive connection; Threes structure goal-directed activity to avoid failure. So Twos are less direct and assertive; Threes more driven to win and reframe failure as learning.

**Twos and Fours [pp.402 to 403]**
- *Look alike:* both aware of image and how others perceive them (Twos want to seem likable and friendly; Fours special and unique); both self-critical about not being good enough to earn love; both feel emotions fairly readily (Twos sometimes repress; Fours may overdo or dwell in some feelings to avoid others); both prioritize relationships and connection and have great empathy.
- *Differences:* in a joint project Twos are optimistic, upbeat and supportive; Fours focus on what is missing. Twos neglect their own needs; Fours have more access to their own needs and wants and prioritize them. Twos are other-focused; Fours self-referencing. Twos adapt their presentation to what they think others want; Fours value authenticity and alter less. Twos avoid conflict for fear of destroying connections; Fours can engage in conflict when needed, valuing truthful expression over accommodation. Mood: Twos upbeat and positive; Fours can dwell in melancholy and sadness.

**Twos and Fives [pp.403 to 404]**
- *Look alike (they are "in some ways opposites"):* both can withdraw when feeling vulnerable (Fives more often; the Self-Preservation Two withdraws more than other Two subtypes); both need alone time (more regular for Fives; Twos feel it after being around people a lot or after development work reveals self-neglect). In self-work Twos need to learn to be alone more, Fives to be with others more. Both value independence, a way of life for Fives, an unconscious defense for Twos against feeling dependent on others' approval.
- *Differences:* Twos feel emotion frequently; Fives detach, appearing reserved, unemotional, analytical; Twos take an intuitive, feelings-based approach, Fives an objective, intellectual one. Twos seek close relationships; Fives value privacy and space and avoid others' emotional needs. Fives believe they are easily drained by interaction; Twos feel energized and affirmed by contact. Twos give generously, even too much; Fives withhold, fearing people will take too much time and energy. Twos struggle with boundaries and saying no; Fives set firm boundaries and say no easily. Twos see themselves as high-energy; Fives sense limited energy and conserve it.

**Twos and Sixes [pp.404 to 405]**
- *Look alike:* both worry and are fearful, from different sources: Sixes about overall safety and bad things happening; Twos about being perceived positively, being rejected, and the safety of specific important people. Both read people well, for different aims: Sixes look for hidden agendas, trustworthiness, threats; Twos gauge moods and needs to create rapport. Both work hard so things go well (Sixes as troubleshooters anticipating problems; Twos to please and look competent). Both have difficulty deciding (Twos do not know what they need or want because their attention is on others; Sixes doubt themselves and fear choosing wrong).
- *Differences:* Twos manage image to please; Sixes think less about image. Twos want to be seen and appreciated; Sixes would often rather hide, since being noticed is vulnerable. Sixes are suspicious of or rebellious toward authority; Twos seek good relationships with authority. Sixes catastrophize far more; Twos are usually optimistic. Twos avoid conflict; Sixes, especially counterphobic ones, may move toward conflict, particularly to challenge an abusing authority.
- *Subtype note:* Twos and **Self-Preservation Sixes** share warmth and energy invested in friendships and trying to avoid showing aggression (both can when reactive). Twos attract friends to be liked, affirmed and seen as indispensable, and so friends will meet their needs as they meet others'; Self-Preservation Sixes form alliances for safety against outside threats.

**Twos and Sevens [pp.405 to 406]**
- *Look alike:* both upbeat, energetic, fun-loving, positive (Twos because people like happy people; Sevens because "negative" emotions feel threatening); both hedonistic, but with different aims (Twos want positive shared experiences to build relationships and to indulge in response to feelings of deprivation; Sevens seek pleasure defensively to avoid discomfort, pain, anxiety). Both enjoy relating to people and may idealize those they like (Twos for affirmation of likability; Sevens for stimulation).
- *Differences:* Twos attend to others' moods and needs; Sevens to their own needs and wishes. Twos adapt and abandon their own needs to strengthen bonds; Sevens do what they want and rarely give up needs to please someone (Social Seven excepted). Twos manage image to attract; Sevens do not focus as much on how they are perceived. "On a fundamental level, Twos are motivated by pleasing others; Sevens are motivated by pleasing themselves" [p.406]. Twos are feeling-oriented; Sevens mental and thinking-oriented. Sevens struggle to focus on tedious tasks; Twos focus more easily, especially if seen and evaluated. Sevens want many options and feel limited without them; for Twos many options make deciding harder.
- *Subtype note:* **Social Sevens** look most like Twos: more service-oriented, they will sacrifice their own needs for the group, seeming friendly, outgoing and generous. They are still distinguished from Twos by knowing their own needs and wants and tending to avoid difficult feelings.

**Twos and Eights [pp.406 to 407]**
- *Look alike:* Social Twos can look like Eights, and especially female Social Eights can look like Twos. Both are protective (Twos of important others; Eights of the weak and vulnerable); both can be impulsive, self-indulgent, hedonistic and excessive in eating, working and giving (Twos because they often do not know what they need and overcompensate for feeling deprived; Eights because of big energy and appetites and dislike of limits); both forget their own needs (Twos by abandoning themselves for others; Eights by taking on more and not recognizing limits); both like control (Eights to order things and move forward; Twos to appear competent and impress).
- *Differences:* Twos watch their image closely; Eights may say they do not care what others think. Eights feel and express anger relatively easily and confront; most Twos repress anger and avoid conflict for fear of losing connection. Eights attend to power and control and who holds it; Twos to needs and feelings. Eights step into leadership readily; Twos are more comfortable as support, "the leader's right-hand or the power behind the throne." Eights dominate and impose will; Twos adjust to what others need (though sometimes in a prideful "I know best" way). Eights deny vulnerability; Twos express vulnerable feelings such as hurt or sadness and may unconsciously use vulnerability to manipulate.

**Twos and Nines [p.408]**
- *Look alike:* both focus on others and neglect their own needs; both overadjust (Twos to be liked; Nines to blend and reduce tension); both good mediators who see others' views more clearly than their own; both appear likable, friendly, caring; little or no contact with anger (some Twos feel it when unexpressed needs go unmet); both avoid conflict (some Twos can be more open to it when emotion drives them); both can be passive-aggressive out of fear of breaking connections.
- *Differences:* Twos focus on feelings and feel them readily; Nines on keeping an energetic harmony. Twos feel a wider range of stronger emotions; Nines are steadier. Twos move actively toward others, reading needs and preferences; Nines seek connection less actively and read needs less. Twos are selective about relationships, more drawn to some people; Nines are "democratic." Twos have more energy and a faster pace; Nines are relaxed. Both abandon themselves: Twos *repress* needs and feelings; Nines *forget* or avoid attending to their desires and agendas. Twos often have a clear agenda (especially for connection) while not attending to their own needs; Nines tune out their own agenda.

### 2.5 Threes paired with each other type

**Threes and Fours [pp.409 to 410]**
- *Look alike:* both focus on how others perceive them (Threes build an image of success by external standards in specific contexts; Fours communicate an image based on their own unique sense of what matters); both in the heart triad, fundamentally oriented to feelings and emotional connection; both can prioritize relationships and want approval and recognition; both can be intense, creative, hardworking, competitive.
- *Differences:* Threes focus on tasks, goals and work, looking for the shortest, most efficient path; Fours on feelings, self-expression and emotional connection, with a nonlinear, organic, creative approach. Threes numb feelings to get things done; Fours believe all feelings should be felt and authentically expressed. Threes pursue success as defined by the group; Fours pursue ideals of love and emotional depth to feel special and unique. Threes value material signs of success (nice clothes, cars); Fours attend to inner feeling and values. Threes focus on goals; Fours on what is missing. Threes conform to whatever image others will admire, even losing sight of who they are; Fours value authentic self-expression. Threes identify with an idealized image of success and often seem genuinely confident; Fours identify with a deficient self-image and sense they are flawed. Threes watch competing, winning, avoiding failure; Fours authentic connection and aesthetics. (The Sexual Four may be as competitive as a Three, but from an emotional wish to prove themselves worthy or superior, motivated by anger or unconscious envy [pp.409 to 410].)

**Threes and Fives [p.410]**
- *Look alike:* both value emotional control and avoid attending to emotions (Threes numb out to protect tasks, goals and image; Fives detach and think/analyze); Fives find comfort in the mental realm, Threes in doing and performing. Both can seem unavailable (Threes overidentify with image and cannot live from the real self; Fives withdraw to avoid draining entanglements). Both value independence and self-sufficiency.
- *Differences:* Threes depend on others' approval and admiration; Fives pride themselves on independence and objectivity and do not evaluate themselves by others' perceptions. Threes build an admirable image; Fives do not. At work Threes are oriented to doing and goals; Fives to observing, thinking, analyzing, developing knowledge. Threes expend large energy and can be workaholics and bring work on vacation; Fives conserve energy, are economical with time, energy and effort, and avoid draining tasks and relationships. Threes compete, sometimes to win at all costs; Fives, who can seem aloof, disengage if an effort is not worth the cost.

**Threes and Sixes [p.411]**
- *Look alike:* Threes and some Sixes (especially counterphobic) are hardworking, assertive, forward-moving; both specialize in reading people (Threes to learn what others value and craft a successful image; Sixes to answer an inner sense of threat by looking for hidden agendas); both can be personable and friendly (Threes seeking approval; Sixes wanting to know allies); both practical and solution-focused (Threes seeking the most efficient path; Sixes anticipating problems and dangers).
- *Differences:* Threes move quickly; Sixes can procrastinate from fear of doing it wrong. Threes appear confident; Sixes waver through doubt and may be paralyzed by overanalysis and worst-case imagining. Threes do whatever it takes for success and like recognition; Sixes usually fear success and may self-sabotage, believing success leads to exposure and then attack. Threes do not slow down to think about what could go wrong; Sixes always do, which makes them skilled troubleshooters. Threes work well with authority unless it interferes with goals; Sixes are suspicious or rebellious toward authority.

**Threes and Sevens [pp.411 to 412]**
- *Look alike:* much energy, work hard on what interests them; both charming, engaging, attractive (Threes to gain approval, admiration, cooperation; Sevens as a first line of defense, to defuse negativity and set an upbeat mood); both optimistic and confident about goals (Threes to project achievement; Sevens from habitually positive framing and belief in endless possibility, as a way of avoiding difficult feelings); both avoid negative feelings that would slow them down (Threes because emotions interfere with doing and looking good; Sevens fearing being stuck in anxiety or sadness).
- *Differences:* Threes excel at focusing on and completing tasks; Sevens are easily distracted. Threes put effort into cultivating image and managing perceptions; Sevens less so. Threes are other-oriented, relying on approval to affirm self; Sevens are self-referencing. Threes prioritize work; Sevens pleasure, fun and recreation. Threes work well within authority structures if they support progress; Sevens dislike hierarchy and equalize authority to avoid limits. Sevens plan for the future; Threes attend to the present and today's tasks.

**Threes and Eights [pp.412 to 413]**
- *Look alike:* hardworking with great energy; both can overwork (Threes driven to finish and reach goals; Eights wanting big accomplishments and forgetting physical limits); both feel and express anger when necessary but for different reasons (Threes when obstacles come between them and goals; Eights more frequently and about more things: someone hurting those they protect, impeding forward progress, giving orders, being unjust, injuring them); both direct, assertive and results-oriented; both enjoy leadership (Threes like a say and the image-enhancing effect of high status; Eights want control and the power to set the agenda); both have difficulty expressing vulnerable emotions and may see them as weakness (Threes because feelings interfere with progress; Eights to maintain strength and control).
- *Differences:* Threes cultivate an image to gain admiration; Eights pay little attention to image. Threes work for goals in the service of success and looking good; Eights are motivated by power, control and satisfying physical needs. Threes find the most efficient route and judge their impact on others well; Eights can have difficulty knowing how much force to apply and have a blind spot about their impact. Threes work within structures if not impeded; Eights rebel against authority and break rules if it suits them. Truth: for Eights "truth is what they say it is"; for Threes (whose fixation is "deceit" or "self-deceit") truth is relative and adapted to the circumstances and audience [p.413]. Eights usually know who they are (identity, power, strength); Threes can be confused about identity and believe they are their image.

**Threes and Nines [pp.413 to 414]**
- *Look alike:* both optimistic, upbeat, likable; both hardworking and practical (Threes more excessively); both depend on external support for identity and direction (Threes read what is viewed as successful and design an image; Nines reference others and go along, disliking conflict and lacking a clear inner agenda); both can be hard to contact (Threes absorbed in to-do lists and image; Nines forget themselves and merge). Nines may realize later that they did not want the plan but did not know it at the time.
- *Differences:* Threes focus on tasks and goals; Nines on comfort and harmony. Threes are fast, decisive, forward-moving; Nines slow and tend to sit on the fence. Threes can be workaholics; some Nines (especially Social Nines) can be very hardworking, but many struggle with inertia, indecision and distraction by lesser tasks and others' goals. Threes can engage in conflict to remove an obstacle; Nines avoid it. Nines stay in the comfort zone; Threes endure discomfort for goals. Nines dislike being the center of attention; most Threes enjoy the spotlight and may seek it.

### 2.6 Fours paired with each other type

**Fours and Fives [pp.414 to 415]**
- *Look alike:* both introverted and withdrawing (Fives regularly, to conserve energy and avoid being drained or invaded; Fours periodically, for deeper contact with inner experience); both can intellectualize (think to disconnect from feeling), though Fours have far more contact with emotions; both self-referencing and introspective.
- *Differences:* Fours are among the most emotional types, in contact with deep feelings much of the time; Fives are among the least emotional, habitually detaching. Fives avoid deep connection and limit emotional entanglement; Fours seek deep emotional connection. Fives keep feelings private and value self-sufficiency; Fours share feelings and value emotionally authentic relationships. Fives evaluate with a detached, objective view; Fours' strength is emotional intuition. Fives enjoy lots of private time; Fours can enjoy alone time but are sensitive to abandonment and loss. Fives are reserved, withholding and intrusion-sensitive; Fours dramatic, romantic, passionate. Fives are drained by others' needs; Fours are empathic in the face of them. Fives minimize needs and desires; Fours dwell in desire and longing and feel lack acutely, whereas Fives detach from pain and cope by hoarding, economizing and conserving.

**Fours and Sixes [pp.415 to 416]**
- *Look alike:* both intuitive and good at reading others (Sixes to detect intentions and protect themselves; Fours through emotional intuition and empathy); both good troubleshooters (Fours see what is missing; Sixes anticipate what could go wrong); both can challenge authority and conventional ways (Fours as nonconformists with original perspectives and depth; Sixes as contrarians suspicious of those with power); both hold negative feelings about themselves (Fours feel defective or missing something; Sixes doubt, question, blame themselves); both can get stuck (Fours by self-criticism and attachment to emotions, believing things hopeless; Sixes by overthinking, "analysis paralysis," doubt, fear of success).
- *Differences:* Fours care how they are perceived and want to be seen as unique and original; Sixes care less about image and identify with the underdog and "everyman/everywoman." Fours live from emotions; Sixes dwell in their heads. Sixes' commonest feelings are fear, doubt, worry; Fours' are sadness and melancholy. Sixes search for certainty and fail to find it or cling to something for it; Fours focus on what they lack that others have (often an unattainable love) and think they could finally be happy if they got it. Fours' chief aim is to feel loved and appreciated for who they are; Sixes' is to feel safe.

**Fours and Sevens [pp.416 to 418]**
- *Look alike:* both very idealistic (Fours about love and connection; Sevens in a wider array of imaginative realms); both seek intense, stimulating experiences (Fours because they live from feelings and relish deeply felt emotion and passionate connection and dislike the mundane; Sevens to keep mood up and avoid less positive, empty, boring or unpleasant states); both averse to the everyday and ordinary; both value creativity and self-expression (Fours to be seen as special and for aesthetics; Sevens as natural visionaries with many interests); both self-referencing (Fours on feelings and moods; Sevens on thoughts, plans and desire for amusement); both sensitive to criticism (Fours as a blow to an already diminished sense of self; Sevens as an interruption to a youthful focus on the positive).
- *Differences:* Sevens are relentlessly optimistic; Fours can seem pessimistic, drawing attention to what is missing. Sevens dwell in positive feelings and have trouble staying with difficult ones; Fours are comfortable with a wide range of emotions and feel disappointment or melancholy regularly and comfortably. Sevens reframe negatives into positives; Fours are irritated by being told to "look on the bright side." Fours' comfort with feeling makes them good supporters of the suffering, and they can find richness in suffering as a valuable part of human experience; Sevens find others' pain hard to be with. Fours seek deep connection based on authentic feeling; Sevens can be hesitant to commit deeply because they dislike being limited. Fours value authenticity and depth; Sevens prioritize charm and a positive, fun-loving presentation (which Fours may find superficial or insincere).

**Fours and Eights [pp.418 to 419]**
- *Look alike:* both will engage in conflict and confront (Eights more regularly); both feel and express big emotions (Eights mostly anger; Fours a wider range, especially melancholy); both drawn to intensity and passionate (Fours far more likely to feel vulnerable feelings); both can be impulsive and justified in breaking rules (Eights because they are bigger than the rules; Fours because they put their inner experience and needs over rules); both work hard and are deeply involved (Fours seeing work as self-expression and collaborative artistry; Eights wanting big impact, power, and to mentor and protect the people they work with).
- *Differences:* Fours feel a wider range of emotions; Eights feel more anger and impatience; Fours more melancholy and sadness. Eights dislike vulnerability and deny vulnerable feelings; Fours feel them regularly and can find comfort in a deep experience of their vulnerability. Eights struggle to recognize physical limits, dependency needs and softer emotions; Fours (possibly except some Sexual Fours) know their limits, dependency and softer feelings. Fours put much more effort into getting their physical and emotional needs met. In love Eights express through protection and power; Fours through feeling and desire for connection. Eights rebel against authority more regularly. Eights see the big picture and strategize; Fours attend to the creative process and being appreciated for unique contributions. With others Eights are assertive, even aggressive and dominating (Sexual Fours may be too); Fours seek emotional connection. Eights misjudge their impact; Fours are emotionally intuitive and sensitive to how they affect others. Eights communicate directly; Fours descriptively about their emotional experience. Eights pay little attention to inner processes; Fours are very introspective.

**Fours and Nines [pp.419 to 420]**
- *Look alike:* both prize relationships and connection; both can lose themselves by merging with loved ones (Nines more; Fours sense their independent needs more easily); both understand others deeply (Fours via emotional intuition; Nines seeing others' perspectives more clearly than their own); both can feel overlooked, unimportant and misunderstood (Fours: misunderstood or "not good enough"; Nines: overlooked and not heard, because they do not take strong positions); both fear not belonging (Fours feel like misfits; Nines wonder if they belong to the group).
- *Differences:* Nines are other-referencing; Fours self-referencing. Fours feel more emotions more deeply; Nines are steady and even. Nines are drawn away from their priorities to substitutes and others' agendas; Fours know and pursue their needs. Nines work for harmony; for Fours it matters less and they may create or contribute to discord to push for authentic communication. Nines avoid conflict; Fours can engage or create it. Nines often do not state preferences (not knowing them, valuing others' opinions more, or avoiding conflict); Fours believe their opinions matter and say them. Fours do not always adapt and often voice disagreement; Nines overadjust, thinking a failure to adapt will break the connection, so they struggle to say no, set boundaries and assert themselves; Fours do so more easily.

### 2.7 Fives paired with each other type

**Fives and Sixes [pp.420 to 421]**
- *Look alike:* both reserved and withdrawn; more phobic Sixes in particular resemble Fives (introverted, seeking security by moving away from people); Fives keep distance to avoid being depleted, Sixes because others may be dangerous; both slow to trust, both with safety and security concerns (phobic Sixes more actively fearful; Fives avoid fearful situations before they occur); both vigilant about boundaries and angry when they are challenged (Fives to prevent intrusion and energy-draining interaction; Sixes fearing attack or shaming); both analytical thinkers who intellectualize, thinking about feelings but having trouble feeling them.
- *Differences:* counterphobic (Sexual) Sixes look very different, being much more extroverted. Sixes have more apparent issues with authority: suspicious or openly rebellious; Fives can follow authority if they choose and, if not, go against it quietly. Sixes focus on questioning and doubting in the quest for certainty; Fives on accumulating knowledge, reducing needs, and economical use of time and energy. Fives value emotional control; Sixes do not prioritize it the same way. Fives are objective, having detached from emotion; Sixes can have trouble distinguishing intuition from projection and may confuse what they perceive with what they fear. Fives withdraw so as not to be pressed to meet others' emotional needs; Sixes do not fear meeting others' needs and can be generous with people they trust.

**Fives and Sevens [pp.421 to 422]**
- *Look alike:* both mental types living in their heads, in different ways: Fives gather and compartmentalize information (knowledge is power); Sevens plan and interrelate ideas nonlinearly, finding parallels between dissimilar things. Both have active imaginations and enjoy learning; both guard against social over-commitment (Fives fearing drain; Sevens wanting options); both intellectualize.
- *Differences:* Sevens live in the future in fantasies and plans of pleasure; Fives do not. Sevens reframe negatives into positives; Fives are removed and objective. Sevens want multiple options and limitless opportunities; Fives focus on conserving energy and resources against depletion. Sevens struggle with commitments because being depended on feels constraining; Fives can commit precisely because they protect private space and boundaries, making social promises carefully and to few people. Sevens are gregarious. For feelings, Sevens seek excitement to avoid frustration, discomfort and sadness; Fives simply detach and move to thoughts. Sevens handle fear by charming and disarming others; Fives detach and withdraw from intrusive interactions.

**Fives and Eights [pp.422 to 423]**
- *Look alike:* both express anger if boundaries are challenged (for Fives nearly the only occasion of open anger; Eights get angry more often and over more); both have great difficulty experiencing and especially expressing vulnerable emotions (Fives detach and withdraw from situations that inspire them; Eights deny vulnerability and overcompensate by showing strength).
- *Differences:* Eights are extroverted with "big" energy; Fives are introverted, reserved, low-key. Both like control: Eights take it overtly, actively, aggressively; Fives quietly with less energy. Eights are excessive; Fives minimalistic, conserving, economical. Eights cannot always tell objective truth from their own version; Fives are good objective analysts. Eights are impulsive and act before thinking; Fives think a great deal before acting and suffer from thinking too much without acting. In relationships Eights make clear where they stand; Fives are hard to read and may withhold their thoughts and feelings even from close others. Eights rebel against any inhibition of their capacity for pleasure or power; Fives minimize and inhibit their own needs and desires and can feel depleted by life and relationships, even forgoing relationship pleasures if the cost in time, space or emotional energy is too high. Most Eights are energized by relationships, especially physical intimacy or the promise of it.

**Fives and Nines [pp.423 to 424]**
- *Look alike (especially to observers):* both reserved and withdrawn in energy (Nines do not withdraw from others so much as forget themselves and neglect their own agendas for harmony); both good mediators (Nines see all sides; Fives are objective analysts); both dislike conflict and can be passive-aggressive (Nines from not feeling anger directly; Fives to avoid open emotion or an energetically costly situation); both distance themselves from their own inner experience (Fives by detaching from emotion; Nines by forgetting preferences and opinions); at work both like structure and regularity, want to be consulted and may need time to reflect, and dislike being controlled.
- *Differences:* Nines merge with others and find comfort in harmony; Fives withdraw, fearing depletion. Nines are other-referencing; Fives self-referencing. Nines are overly adaptive; Fives under-adaptive. Nines often do not know what they want, avoid stating it, and later may resent having gone along; Fives nearly always know what they want and prevent interference. Nines seem friendly, affable, easygoing; Fives aloof and reserved. Nines often do not perceive their need for boundaries and so do not set them; Fives prioritize boundaries. Nines may say yes and mean no; Fives say no when they mean no. Nines may have a hard time separating from others; Fives separate easily, sometimes to a fault, since withdrawal is a primary self-protection. "For Nines, paying attention to others' agendas gets in the way of knowing their own; for Fives, paying attention to their own agenda makes it hard to let in and make room for the agendas (and feelings) of others" [p.424].

### 2.8 Sixes paired with each other type

**Sixes and Sevens [pp.424 to 425]**
- *Look alike:* both mental, thinking-oriented types, thinking about different things: Sevens plan future activities, new ideas and synthesize information; Sixes think about what might go wrong, and think contrarily, questioning ideas heard from others to find truth. Both quick thinkers with good imaginations (Sixes imagine worst cases; Sevens highly positive ones). Both are "fear types," though possibly not actively aware of fear. **Sevens and counterphobic Sixes can look very much alike:** both move toward threats to face danger, the Seven with charm and an engaging presentation, the counterphobic Six with strength and willingness to intimidate. Both can get caught in thinking and not act (Sixes in doubt; Sevens distracted by new ideas and options or unwilling to be limited).
- *Differences:* Sevens are very optimistic; Sixes describe themselves as realistic but may look pessimistic to others. Sevens reframe positively; Sixes focus on what might go wrong in order to prepare. Managing fear: Sevens move toward its source with charm and pleasantries to disarm it with "soft power"; Sixes watch vigilantly to see threats coming. Counterphobic Sixes move toward threatening situations with strength; phobic Sixes withdraw; Social Sixes obey one kind of authority or another to cope with anxiety. Sevens focus on positive possibilities and fun to avoid pain; Sixes can hardly avoid pain and discomfort given self-doubt, questioning, and detecting danger. Sixes seek certainty and rarely find or keep it. Sevens are playful and adventurous; Sixes careful and strategic. Sevens plan for fun; Sixes prepare for problems, and actually seek out problems to solve as a way of feeling safe. Sixes question and rebel against authority; Sevens equalize authority, denying hierarchical power and befriending both superiors and subordinates. Sevens expect success and look confident; Sixes expect things to go wrong and may seem worried, even paranoid. Sevens have trouble with commitments, fearing limits; Sixes are loyal and committed once they trust.

**Sixes and Eights [pp.425 to 426]**
- *Look alike (phobic Sixes look quite different; counterphobic Sixes a lot like Eights):* both can appear strong and intimidating and move "fearlessly" toward threats head-on. But Eights truly have little or no fear, while counterphobic Sixes act against threats to quell a deeper, not always conscious fear (the "fight" of "fight or flight"). Eights and all Sixes tend to rebel against authority; both protect those they care about (Eights the weak and vulnerable; Sixes underdogs and underdog causes); both can be hardworking and practical (Eights more prone to overwork and moving big things quickly; Sixes cautious and slowed by overanalysis).
- *Differences:* Eights feel little fear or vulnerability, based on denial of vulnerability and overcompensating confidence in strength; phobic Sixes feel fearful and vulnerable much of the time and stay vigilant. Eights rarely self-doubt; Sixes continually do. Sixes overthink and may be paralyzed; Eights act quickly without thinking and get impatient when slowed; Sixes procrastinate fearing bad outcomes. Sixes are slow to trust and inspect people for hidden agendas; Eights generally trust people who seem competent until trust is broken. Eights and counterphobic Sixes confront directly; the phobic Six would rather avoid conflict but can engage if necessary or provoked.

**Sixes and Nines [pp.426 to 427]**
- *Look alike:* both loyal, caring, supportive; both procrastinate (Sixes from fear things will go wrong and fear of success; Nines when they struggle to access and stay with their own agendas, sometimes passively resisting what others want to avoid conflict; Sixes block their own progress by questioning, overanalyzing, doubting); phobic Sixes and Nines both avoid conflict (Nines more extremely); both self-effacing and humble; both dislike the spotlight (Sixes because even positive attention exposes them to attack; Nines because they do not place themselves at the center of their own attention).
- *Differences:* Nines merge and trust easily; Sixes stand apart and are suspicious at first until they gather information. Nines go along to get along and overadjust to avoid discomfort and separation; Sixes are mistrustful and may question or test people before going along. Nines are easily distracted from their agenda; Sixes stay alert to potential threats. Nines see many viewpoints and mediate; Sixes are contrarian: they see one side and then the opposing, countering whatever view is put forward rather than holding many equally plausible views. Nines dislike conflict and lack contact with their anger; counterphobic Sixes may get angry and move toward conflict to deal with a potential threat. Sixes are somewhat to very anti-authoritarian; Nines cooperate with authority, at least outwardly.

### 2.9 Sevens with Eights and Nines; Eights with Nines

**Sevens and Eights [pp.427 to 428]**
- *Look alike:* both visionary thinkers who see the big picture and future possibilities; both can engage in conflict (some Sevens more comfortable with confrontation than others); both uninhibited, self-indulgent, excessive in pursuit of pleasure and appreciate intense experiences; both dislike being limited or controlled; both rebellious (Eights openly and straightforwardly, "the best defense is a good offense"; Sevens through a charm-based, diplomatic approach, soft power and keeping multiple options, with charm as the first line of defense); both break rules if it suits them; both overbook (Sevens because they struggle to say no to exciting possibilities; Eights from wanting to do everything and forgetting physical needs and vulnerability); both avoid or deny softer, vulnerable feelings (Eights deny vulnerability; Sevens avoid pain and discomfort).
- *Differences:* Eights can be rebellious toward authority but can also work with a good authority they respect and enjoy leading at times; Sevens equalize authority, making friends with bosses and subordinates to deny a vertical power structure. Attention: Eights focus on power and control; Sevens on planning and play. Both have access to anger but Eights are more likely to express it. Eights are direct and move things forward strongly and forcefully; Sevens get distracted, especially by tedious or routine work. Eights make order and push projects to a conclusion; Sevens prefer the idea stage to implementation and have trouble following through [pp.427 to 428]. Sevens intellectualize to move from feeling into thinking and find difficult feelings uncomfortable; Eights move into action without thinking things through. Eights deny softer feelings or project them onto those perceived as weaker and then protect them. Analyzing: Sevens reframe negatives into positives; Eights face negatives and see issues as "all or nothing" or "black and white."

**Sevens and Nines [p.428]**
- *Look alike:* friendly, optimistic dispositions; personable, affable, like being around people; both want to be liked and make it easy for others to like them; both keep things positive and avoid conflict if possible (many Sevens can do conflict if necessary; most Nines prefer not to); both struggle to keep focus on the task (Sevens distracted by more interesting things; Nines by others' agendas, environmental claims and inessential tasks).
- *Differences:* both are mainly (if unconsciously) concerned with avoiding uncomfortable feelings, but Sevens pursue excitement, indulgence and fun, whereas Nines neglect themselves and forget their opinions and desires to avoid anger and discomfort. Sevens are fast-paced, high-energy; Nines relaxed, often with inertia and indecision. Sevens are self-referencing, focused on their own agenda; Nines other-referencing, with no clear direct experience of their own desires. Nines merge and go along; Sevens' own clear agenda usually takes priority in a conflict. It is easy for Sevens to know what they want; hard for Nines, for whom "it's easier to know what they don't want than what they do want." Nines typically do not state preferences (which they often do not know) and then can resent those whose agenda they passively followed.

**Eights and Nines [pp.429 to 430]**
- *Look alike:* both dislike being controlled by others but respond differently: Eights openly rebel, fight, potentially overpower; Nines are passive, seeming to agree while passively resisting ("saying 'yes,' but acting out 'no'"). Both belong to the **"self-forgetting" triad** of types and can forget their own needs and wants (Eights through excess and overwork and denying physical vulnerabilities, taking on too many responsibilities; Nines by focusing on others and losing conscious contact with their emotions and priorities); both can enjoy and seek worldly comforts and pleasures.
- *Differences:* Eights' primary attention is on power and control; Nines' is on creating harmony and avoiding conflict. Nines unconsciously avoid their own anger, which might put them at odds with someone; Eights have easy access to anger, may feel it often and engage in conflict easily. Eights are highly opinionated and direct; Nines often do not know their position because they attend to others' perspectives (an opinion risks conflict). Nines see everyone's point of view, making them excellent mediators who help create consensus; Eights see their own view most clearly and in black and white and want their own way. Nines struggle with boundaries and saying no; Eights assert will and reject requests easily. Eights are often seen as intimidating; Nines as likable, approachable, friendly. Eights have a big impact on others; Nines struggle to make an impact and are harder to contact interpersonally. Eights like to break or make their own rules and rebel against authority; Nines like structure and work more easily with authority figures. Both avoid a realm of inner experience (Eights deny vulnerable softer emotions; Nines avoid or forget their anger and preferences) but Eights are far more open in expressing themselves and acting forcefully in the world to get what they need and want.
