# Triads, Wings & Instinctual Variants

**Source:** *The Wisdom of the Enneagram* — Don Richard Riso & Russ Hudson, Part I: "The Inward Journey,"
Chapters 5–6 (of 6; Chapters 1–4 are covered separately in `Foundations_and_Practice.md`). This is the
structurally load-bearing half of Part I — Triads/Centers, Hornevian Groups, Harmonic Groups, Wings, Instincts,
Levels of Development, and the mechanism behind Integration/Disintegration. Distilled into original wording,
not reproduced verbatim, per this project's standing book-mining practice.

**Cross-reference:** the Integration/Disintegration *numbers* (which type moves toward which under stress vs.
security) are already canon at `Enneagram_Dynamics.md`, sourced from *Personality Types*. This file doesn't
re-list that table — see the Directions section below for what Chapter 6 adds on top of it instead.

## Chapter 5 — The Triadic Self

### The Three Centers (Triads)

The nine types cluster into three Centers of three types each — **Instinctive** (8, 9, 1), **Feeling** (2, 3, 4),
and **Thinking** (5, 6, 7), also called the gut/body, heart, and head Centers. This is the same three-way split
the Timeline repo's lookup table already calls "Major Theme."

The book is explicit about a common misreading: being in the Feeling Center doesn't mean a type has *more*
feelings than other types, and being in the Thinking Center doesn't mean more intelligence. Each Center is
instead the function (instinct, feeling, or thinking) the ego has formed most rigidly *around* — making it, if
anything, the component of the psyche *least* able to function freely and spontaneously in that type.

| Center | Concerned with | Issues with | Seeks | Underlying feeling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instinctive (8, 9, 1) | Resistance & control of the environment | Aggression & repression | Autonomy | Rage |
| Feeling (2, 3, 4) | Love of false self / self-image | Identity & hostility | Attention | Shame |
| Thinking (5, 6, 7) | Strategies & beliefs | Insecurity & anxiety | Security | Fear |

**Instinctive Center detail — ego boundaries.** Eights, Nines, and Ones each build a "wall" between self and
not-self to manufacture a false sense of autonomy, but point the wall differently: **Eight** directs it outward,
against the environment (expansive, "nothing's getting the upper hand on me"). **One** directs it inward,
against their own unwanted impulses (holding back what they don't want to feel). **Nine** — the Center's
midpoint type — holds boundaries in *both* directions at once, which the book flags as the direct explanation
for why Nines report chronic fatigue: maintaining two fronts simultaneously is exhausting. All three carry rage
underneath; **Eight acts it out, Nine denies it, One represses it.**

**Feeling Center detail — self-image direction.** Twos, Threes, and Fours each substitute a self-image for
authentic heart-contact, but present it differently: **Two** presents the image outward, to others (caring,
loving, indispensable). **Four** presents it inward, to themselves (a private story of being uniquely
different). **Three** — the midpoint type — presents it in both directions simultaneously, needing external
affirmation *and* running constant internal "self-talk" to sustain the same picture. All three carry shame
underneath; **Two becomes "ultra-good" to outrun it, Three becomes flawlessly outstanding to outrun it, Four
dramatizes loss and victimhood to outrun it.**

**Thinking Center detail — direction of flight.** Fives, Sixes, and Sevens have each lost the "quiet mind" —
the source of felt inner guidance — and each flees the resulting anxiety differently: **Five** flees inward,
retreating from the world into the mind until they feel they have enough stockpiled to safely reengage. **Seven**
flees outward, into activity and anticipation, to outrun the inner world (grief, pain) rather than the outer one.
**Six** — the midpoint type — flees in both directions at once, oscillating: anxious energy launches them
outward like a Seven, then fear of consequences snaps them back inward like a Five, over and over ("bouncing
around like a Ping-Pong ball").

### Personality vs. Essence, by Center

A compact contrast table the book uses to summarize each Center's "asleep" (personality-driven) state against
its "awake" (Essence-connected) state:

| Center | Asleep (personality) | Awake (Essence) |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking | Mental chatter, figuring it out, strategies/doubt, anxiety and fear, anticipation (future-oriented) | Quiet mind, inner guidance, knowing/clarity, support and steadiness, open to the present |
| Feeling | Self-image, stories, emotionality, holding on to moods, adapting to affect others (past-oriented) | Authenticity, truthfulness, compassion, forgiveness and flow, inner-directed |
| Instinctive | Boundaries, tension/numbness, defending, dissociating, irritation (resistant to the present) | Connected with life, relaxed/open/sensing, inner strength, grounded, acceptance |

### The Hornevian Groups (Social Style)

Named for Karen Horney. Cuts across the Centers to group types by *how* they pursue their Center's underlying
need — **Assertive** (Horney's "moving against people"): 3, 7, 8. **Compliant** ("moving toward people," i.e.
toward the superego's expectations): 1, 2, 6. **Withdrawn** ("moving away from people"): 4, 5, 9. This is the
exact axis the Timeline repo's lookup table already calls "Hornevian Group," and it's the same axis Group I of
the Riso-Hudson QUEST tests (see `Foundations_and_Practice.md`).

- **Assertive (3, 7, 8):** ego-expansive under difficulty — build up, reinforce, or inflate the ego rather than
  withdraw. Walking into a room, the automatic (if unconscious) read is "I am the center; something's going to
  happen now that I'm here" — vivid and immediate for Seven and Eight, but covert for Three, who needs external
  admiration to actually feel it and works subtly to secure that admiration.
- **Compliant (1, 2, 6):** answer to their own superego rather than to other people directly — "How do I meet
  what's expected of me? How do I stay a responsible person?" Often become authority figures themselves,
  especially One and Six. Their private sense of being "better than" others is subtle and duty-shaped: One
  ("if I were in charge, this wouldn't be such a mess"), Two ("these poor people need my help"), Six (via group
  affiliation and loyalty rather than personal virtue).
- **Withdrawn (4, 5, 9):** respond to stress by moving away from engagement into private inner space — Nine's
  "Inner Sanctum," Four's "Fantasy Self," Five's "Inner Tinker Toy." The automatic room-read is "I'm not part of
  what's going on here, I don't fit in" — most acute for Four and Five; Nine may stay and even participate while
  remaining privately disengaged.

**Cross-tabulated with each Center's core want**, the Hornevian style becomes the *strategy* for getting that
want met — **Assertive types demand it, Compliant types earn it, Withdrawn types withdraw to get it (on their
own terms)**:

| | Demands (Assertive) | Earns (Compliant) | Withdraws for (Withdrawn) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** (Instinctive) | 8 | 1 | 9 |
| **Attention** (Feeling) | 3 | 2 | 4 |
| **Security** (Thinking) | 7 | 6 | 5 |

### The Harmonic Groups (Coping Style)

A third cross-cutting grouping — how each type copes *when it doesn't get what it wants*. This is the axis
Group II of the QUEST tests, and the "Harmonic Group" field in the Timeline lookup table. Notably, each of the
three types positioned on the Enneagram's inner equilateral triangle (Three, Six, Nine) has two "look-alike"
partners in its Harmonic Group that people frequently self-mistype as — Nines often misread themselves as Twos
or Sevens, Threes as Ones or Fives, Sixes as Fours or Eights — because the shared Harmonic coping style produces
real surface resemblance despite no direct line connecting them on the symbol.

**Positive Outlook Group (9, 2, 7)** — respond to conflict/difficulty by reframing it positively, staying
"morale-builders," and avoiding the dark or painful:

| Type | Emphasizes | Avoids seeing | Problem with needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Positive self-image: "I am a caring, loving person"; focuses on their own good intentions | Their own neediness, disappointment, and anger | Overemphasis on others' needs; neglect of their own |
| 7 | Positive experiences, enjoyment, activity, excitement, fun | Their own pain and emptiness; their role in creating suffering for self/others | Overemphasis on their own needs; easily feel burdened by others' needs |
| 9 | The positive qualities of others and their environment; idealizes their world | Problems with loved ones or environment; their own lack of development | Overwhelmed by both their own needs and others' — doesn't want to deal with either |

**Competency Group (1, 3, 5)** — cut off feeling, solve problems logically, expect the same objectivity from
others; differ mainly in their relationship to rules/systems (One works inside them, Five works outside them,
Three wants both):

| Type | Emphasizes | Manages feelings by | Relation to systems |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Being correct, organized, sensible; standards, self-improvement, knowing the rules | Repression/denial — feelings get channeled into doing things perfectly; also held as physical rigidity | Wants to work *with* the system; a "good boy/girl," irritated by rule-breakers |
| 3 | Being efficient, capable, outstanding; goals, pragmatism, self-presentation | Repression/denial — attention stays on tasks and staying active; looks to others for feeling cues | Wants to work with the system, but also likes being outside it — bending rules, finding shortcuts |
| 5 | Being the expert, having deep information; process, objective fact, detachment | Splitting off/abstracting — stays cerebral, as if feelings are happening to someone else | Rejects the system; wants to work alone, outside it, little patience for rules/procedure |

**Reactive Group (6, 4, 8)** — react emotionally and openly, want a matching emotional response from whoever
they're in conflict with, and tend to actually clear once they've been allowed to vent:

| Type | Seeks | Fears | Deals with others by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | A rescuer — someone to understand and support their life and dreams; wants to be seen | Abandonment — that no one will care enough, or that they won't find/become themselves | Limiting access, playing hard to get, holding on to supporters |
| 6 | Both independence and support — wants someone reliable, but also needs to be "the strong one" | Being abandoned and unsupported, but also becoming too dependent on others | Being committed and reliable while also guarding their independence — engaging but defensive |
| 8 | Independence and self-reliance — wants to need others as little as possible | Being controlled or dominated — fears intimacy and the vulnerability of trusting/caring too much | Keeping their guard up, not letting others get close, toughening against hurt |

**Quick reference, one line per type** — the book's own compressed illustration of each group's default
response to a problem:

- *Positive Outlook:* Nine — "What problem? I don't think there is a problem." / Two — "You have a problem. I
  am here to help you." / Seven — "There may be a problem, but I'm fine."
- *Competency:* Three — "There's an efficient solution — let's get to work." / One — "I'm sure we can solve
  this like sensible, mature adults." / Five — "There are hidden issues here; let me think about it."
- *Reactive:* Six — "I feel really pressured, and I've got to let off some steam!" / Four — "I feel really hurt,
  and I need to express myself." / Eight — "I'm angry about this and you're going to hear about it!"

## Chapter 6 — Dynamics and Variations

### The Wings

Each type has two adjacent types on the Enneagram circle; one of the two typically dominates as that type's
**wing**, blending with and flavoring the base type without changing which base type it is. Riso-Hudson name
all 18 resulting wing subtypes (two per base type):

| Type | Wing A | Wing B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1w9 — The Idealist | 1w2 — The Advocate |
| 2 | 2w1 — The Servant | 2w3 — The Host/Hostess |
| 3 | 3w2 — The Charmer | 3w4 — The Professional |
| 4 | 4w3 — The Aristocrat | 4w5 — The Bohemian |
| 5 | 5w4 — The Iconoclast | 5w6 — The Problem-Solver |
| 6 | 6w5 — The Defender | 6w7 — The Buddy |
| 7 | 7w6 — The Entertainer | 7w8 — The Realist |
| 8 | 8w7 — The Independent | 8w9 — The Bear |
| 9 | 9w8 — The Referee | 9w1 — The Dreamer |

The book's own metaphor for how to think about wing variation: the nine types are a color wheel, not nine
walled-off boxes. Calling someone "a Six" is like calling a color "in the blue family" — real, useful, but not
yet specifying whether it's teal, navy, or powder blue. The wing is what starts specifying the exact shade.

### The Instinctual Variants

A third lens, independent of both type and wing: **Self-Preservation, Social, and Sexual** — the three
instincts every person has, ranked in a personal stack (dominant, secondary, least-developed) that holds true
*regardless* of Enneagram type. Combined with the two-wing lens, this produces 6 variations per base type and
54 total across the system. This is the same Self-Preservation/Social/Sexual triad this project's own Doll
notes already track as "Subdivisions."

- **Self-Preservation:** preoccupied with physical safety, comfort, and resource security — food, money,
  housing, health. Entering a room, notices the room's comfort (lighting, temperature, seating) first. Healthy:
  earthy and practical, competently handles life's logistics. Unhealthy: either self-neglect (poor eating/sleep)
  or compulsive over-acquiring/hoarding. When this instinct is the *least* developed of the three, basic
  self-care (eating, sleeping, money management) simply doesn't occur to the person naturally — often to the
  real detriment of their career and material stability.
- **Social:** preoccupied with belonging, acceptance, and standing within a group — not simply "likes parties,"
  but a survival-based need to be seen as valuable within a community. Entering a room, immediately reads the
  power structure and social politics. Healthy: a kind of contextual intelligence — sees their own efforts in
  the group's larger frame. Unhealthy: becomes antisocial and distrustful while paradoxically unable to
  disengage from social ties. Ironically tends to *avoid* one-on-one intimacy even while highly social. Least
  developed: little natural investment in sustaining community ties, frequent friction with allies/family who
  expect that investment.
- **Sexual:** a constant search for intense connection — not necessarily literal sex, but any high-charge
  experience (an intense conversation, a ski jump). The "intimacy junkies" of the three variants. Entering a
  room, scans for the most magnetic person present, regardless of that person's social usefulness. Healthy:
  wide-ranging, exploratory engagement with life. Unhealthy: scattered attention and lack of focus, sliding into
  either promiscuity or a fearful, avoidant stance toward intimacy — equally intense either way. Least
  developed: struggles to get deeply excited about anything, tends toward routine, and can feel disconnected
  even from close spouses/friends/family despite being socially present.

### The Levels of Development

The system's "vertical" axis, layered on top of the nine "horizontal" types — Riso's own core original
contribution (1977). Each type has **nine Levels**, grouped into three ranges of three: **healthy (1–3)**,
**average (4–6)**, **unhealthy (7–9)**. Health here is explicitly a measure of *freedom and identification*, not
of mood — someone can be having a great day while solidly entrenched at an unhealthy-adjacent Level, and someone
doing real inner work can feel difficult emotions from a healthier, more grounded place.

**The bandwidth / center-of-gravity model.** Picture a peg in one of nine holes (the person's habitual "center
of gravity" within their type) with a rubber band attached, stretching upward when relaxed and secure, downward
under stress. Mood swings move the rubber band; *real* growth or decline is the peg itself moving — which
happens slowly, usually only through sustained work or major life events. Two people of the same type can have
very different bandwidths (say, Levels 2–5 vs. Levels 5–8) and read as strikingly different personalities day
to day despite sharing a base type.

**The average range** is where most people spend most of their time, and where four of the book's diagnostic
tools live:

- **The Wake-up Call** — the earliest warning sign that a type is sliding from healthy into average-range
  fixation:
  | Type | Wake-up Call |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Feeling personally obligated to fix everything themselves |
  | 2 | Believing they must go out to others to win them over |
  | 3 | Beginning to drive themselves for status and attention |
  | 4 | Holding on to and intensifying feelings through imagination |
  | 5 | Withdrawing from reality into concepts and mental worlds |
  | 6 | Becoming dependent on something outside the self for guidance |
  | 7 | Feeling something better is available somewhere else |
  | 8 | Feeling they must push and struggle to make things happen |
  | 9 | Outwardly accommodating themselves to others |
- **The Social Role** — a family-systems-theory-style fixed role the person starts playing once they're
  identified with getting others to affirm their self-image (echoes "Family Hero," "Lost Child," "Scapegoat"
  archetypes); detailed per-type versions live in the individual type chapters (Part II), not summarized in
  this system-level chapter.
- **How each type manipulates others**, once locked into that Social Role:
  | Type | Manipulates by |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Correcting others — insisting they share their standards |
  | 2 | Finding out others' needs/desires — creating dependencies |
  | 3 | Charming others — adopting whatever image "works" |
  | 4 | Being temperamental — making others "walk on eggshells" |
  | 5 | Staying preoccupied — detaching emotionally |
  | 6 | Complaining — testing others' commitment |
  | 7 | Distracting others — insisting others meet their demands |
  | 8 | Dominating others — demanding others do as they say |
  | 9 | "Checking out" — passive-aggressively resisting |
- **The Leaden Rule** — the inverse of the Golden Rule: *"Do unto others what you most fear having done unto
  you."* At the bottom of the average range, each type starts inflicting its own Basic Fear onto others as a
  (false) way of feeling lifted above it:
  | Type | Inflicts on others, out of fear of it themselves |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Points out others' evil/corruption/defectiveness |
  | 2 | Makes others feel unworthy of love/attention |
  | 3 | Makes others feel valueless — arrogance, contempt |
  | 4 | Treats others disdainfully, as if they have no significance |
  | 5 | Makes others feel helpless, incompetent, stupid |
  | 6 | Undermines others' support systems, isolates them |
  | 7 | Causes others pain, makes them feel deprived |
  | 8 | Makes others fear being harmed/controlled, via intimidation |
  | 9 | Makes others feel disconnected, by "tuning out" |

**The Red Flag** — the last, more serious warning before a fall into the unhealthy range; a *realistic* fear
that, if actually heeded, can still arrest the slide:

| Type | Red Flag fear |
|---|---|
| 1 | That their ideals are actually wrong and counterproductive |
| 2 | That they're driving friends and loved ones away |
| 3 | That they're failing — their claims are empty and fraudulent |
| 4 | That they're ruining their life and wasting their opportunities |
| 5 | That they'll never find a place in the world or with people |
| 6 | That their own actions have harmed their own security |
| 7 | That their activities are bringing them pain and unhappiness |
| 8 | That others are turning against them and will retaliate |
| 9 | That they'll be forced by reality to deal with their problems |

**The unhealthy range** is reached one of two ways — a major life crisis without the tools to absorb it, or
patterns of severe childhood harm that never allowed healthy coping to develop in the first place. There's a
"shock point" between average and unhealthy that acts as a brake — most people function in the average range
for years without crossing it.

**The healthy range and the Level of Liberation.** Even at Levels 2–3 the ego is still present, just "worn
lightly" and expressed constructively. At **Level 1**, the book states plainly, a person actually achieves their
Basic Desire — the thing the whole personality structure was built to chase turns out to already be available
once identification with the ego loosens enough.

### The Directions of Integration and Disintegration — the Mechanism

The numeric Integration/Disintegration chart itself is already canon at `Enneagram_Dynamics.md` — this chapter
supplies the *mechanism* behind those numbers, plus three ideas not yet captured in that file:

**Disintegration acts out at the same Level, not a jump to a different type's identity.** A person doesn't
leap from their own average-range behavior into their disintegration-point type's *unhealthy* extreme — they
act out at roughly the same Level of health they're already at. An average-range Two under stress starts
behaving like an *average* Eight, not a pathological one. This also explains real-world mistyping: someone under
sustained stress or trauma can start reading, even to themselves, as their disintegration-point type rather than
their actual base type.

**Disintegration is a pressure-release valve, not pure pathology.** Acting out toward the disintegration point
gives temporary relief and can actually slow a *worse* descent into one's own type's unhealthy range — it
doesn't solve anything, but it isn't purely destructive either. This is consistent with, and gives the
underlying reason for, `Enneagram_Dynamics.md`'s existing "Reversal Note."

**The Security Point — a concept not yet in `Enneagram_Dynamics.md`, worth adding there.** Distinct from real
integration, a person can temporarily display *average-range* (not healthy-range) behaviors of their
Integration-direction type specifically when they feel unusually safe and secure in a given relationship or
situation — not as growth, but as another escape valve, just one triggered by felt safety instead of stress. The
book's own examples: an average One who feels completely secure with someone may loosen into average-Seven
scattered enthusiasm; a secure Five may act out average-Eight forcefulness. This is a *different* mechanism from
real Integration (see below) — it requires no conscious work and produces no lasting change, just a
situation-bound loan of the other type's average traits. **Possible use for this project:** this is a clean,
book-sourced mechanic for writing a companion's specific "off guard / comfortable" behavior around the
player in high-trust scenes, distinct from both her baseline and her true late-arc growth.

**Real Integration requires conscious choice and isn't costume-wearing.** Unlike disintegration (automatic,
compulsive, stress-triggered), moving toward the Integration point only happens through deliberate inner work —
and importantly, it is *not* imitating the target type's surface behaviors. The book's own example: an Eight
moving toward Two doesn't start "acting like a Two" (baking cookies, people-pleasing) — that would just add a
new mask. Real integration is the Eight's *own* blocked capacity for openness and care unblocking, from the
inside, as her own defenses relax — it happens to look Two-like from the outside because Two's healthy
qualities are what was missing, not because she's performing another type. **Direct consequence for companion
writing:** a companion's Integration-point growth beat should read as *more fully herself*, not as her briefly
borrowing another character archetype's mannerisms.
