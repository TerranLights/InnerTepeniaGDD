## Overview

Sixes build their whole strategy around a single, universal childhood fear that most people repress but Sixes
never fully stop feeling: the fear of being genuinely unable to survive without support. Both books trace this
to an early, ongoing experience of unpredictable or unreliable authority — an unstable, absent, or frightening
figure the child couldn't fully trust but also couldn't do without — leaving a lasting, deeply ambivalent
relationship to authority itself: wanting a good one to lean on, while remaining permanently suspicious that
any given one might not actually be trustworthy. What makes Type Six genuinely hard to summarize in a single
description is that this ambivalence runs all the way through the personality — Sixes are, as both books note,
a real bundle of opposites: brave and fearful, compliant and rebellious, trusting and doubting, sometimes in the
same conversation. The three subtypes resolve this tension in three structurally different directions (see
`Subtypes.md`), which is part of why Type Six can look less like one consistent character than like three
distinct responses to the same underlying fear.

**In the stories both books use as touchstones:** Homer's *Odyssey* gives Sixes the Laestrygonian disaster —
the single most catastrophic episode of the entire journey. A seemingly peaceful harbor turns out to house
giant, monstrous inhabitants who attack without warning or explanation, devouring one man and then bombarding
the fleet with boulders; only Odysseus's own ship, powered by sheer terror, escapes, while the other eleven are
destroyed. The Laestrygonians function as an oversized picture of the type's unhealthy extreme — so certain
that any approach signals attack that they never pause to actually investigate whether a threat is real before
reacting to it. Dante's *Inferno* gives Sixes the Vestibule — the outer ring reserved for those who were too
fearful to ever truly commit to anything, forever chasing a banner that "would not ever take a stand," stung
endlessly by hornets and wasps. Dante's judgment is severe: these souls "had never truly lived" — a stark image
of what happens when doubt itself becomes the permanent resting place, and the risk of trusting or acting is
never actually taken.
