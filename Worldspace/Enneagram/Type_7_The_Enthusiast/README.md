## Overview

Sevens build a life around staying one step ahead of pain — not through avoidance exactly, but through
constant forward motion toward whatever's stimulating, promising, or new. Both books trace this to an early
disconnection from steady nurturing (often the mother), followed by the child's own quiet decision to take care
of themselves rather than wait to be cared for — a decision that shows up later as genuine self-sufficiency,
real optimism, and a real gift for finding the good in almost anything, alongside a much harder-to-see cost:
very little practiced tolerance for sitting still inside a difficult feeling long enough to actually process it.
The underlying mechanism both books converge on is that Sevens don't so much repress pain as *outrun* it —
keeping the mind busy enough, and the options open enough, that a difficult feeling rarely gets the chance to
fully register before attention has already moved somewhere more promising.

**In the stories both books use as touchstones:** Homer's *Odyssey* gives Sevens Aeolia, the floating island of
Aeolus, Master of the Winds — a place of continuous feasting and festivity where life is, by design, easy and
carefree. Aeolus even hands Odysseus a literal solution to danger: every wind but the favorable one, sealed
safely away in a bag. But Odysseus's exhausted crew, suspecting the bag holds treasure being withheld from
them, opens it out of envy while he sleeps — unleashing a storm that blows the fleet straight back to where they
started, and gets them permanently ejected from the one truly carefree place in the whole story. The lesson
both books draw is pointed: no one gets to keep a private, permanent Aeolia, and grasping too hard for the
comforts of life is exactly what tends to cost them. Dante's *Inferno* gives Sevens the Gluttons — punished not
with more excess but with its precise opposite: pressed together in freezing, filthy rain and hail, howling
with hunger in the muck. The dark side of an appetite that never gets satisfied, Dante suggests, isn't more
indulgence — it's an emptiness no amount of consumption was ever actually going to fill.
