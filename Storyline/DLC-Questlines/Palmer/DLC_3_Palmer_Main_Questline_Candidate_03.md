# DLC 3 (Palmer Subnet) — Main Questline Candidate #3: "Whose Name Comes Back First"

**What this is:** a third prospective But/Therefore chain for DLC 3's main questline, produced by running
`../DLC_Main_Questline_Design_Method.md` against Palmer subnet, built around Esperanza's own established
birth registry and its diaspora's living relationship to it. **Not canon, not chosen, not the only
candidate** — the 3rd of the "at least 10" the method calls for. Uses the Linear Escalation Chain schematic
(`../But_Therefore_Quest_Design_Method.md` §5a).

**Uses 1 of Palmer subnet's 8 cities (Esperanza, as ruins and archive)** — its diaspora, per the city's own
established facts, is carried forward primarily by long-lived robot caregivers rather than human bloodlines,
referenced through that already-established mechanism rather than new invented material.

---

## Inputs actually used (Step 1 of the design method)

- **Esperanza's own Full Extrapolation, Section I** — the birth registry — the founding document recording
  every child born under Esperanza's own founding compact — partially survives the war: fragments, not the
  whole. What survives is treated with even greater reverence specifically because it's incomplete; the
  diaspora would want desperately to know whether their own family's names made it through, giving any
  future recovery of additional fragments real, specific emotional stakes rather than being a historical
  curiosity.
- **Esperanza's own Full Extrapolation, Section II** — the founding compact was never a single formal
  document; it lived in the birth registry itself, oral tradition, and lived practice — meaning the registry
  isn't merely a record of the compact, it's one of the compact's actual living expressions.
- **Esperanza's own Full Extrapolation, Section IV** — "being from Hope" as a human genealogical identity
  fades within a couple of generations, consistent with Tepenia's established fact that human memory dies
  with the generation that held it; the actual durable carriers are the long-lived robot caregivers and
  teachers — Caregiver-Teacher Alanna Whitcombe-Reyes, proposed by name — who lived through Esperanza's whole
  history and later relocated to Concordia.
- **Esperanza's own Cross-Reference Synthesis, Finding 3** — Esperanza's compounding loss for Tepenia's
  wider expansion effort; this chain doesn't use this material directly, but keeps it in view as the reason
  the city's destruction still resonates beyond its own diaspora specifically.
- **Explicitly excluded:** any claim about the registry's total contents or a full reconstruction of who
  specifically was recorded in it; this chain treats the registry as genuinely, permanently incomplete,
  consistent with Section I's own framing.

---

## The Chain

**SETUP** — Esperanza's diaspora, organized in part through its own surviving robot caregivers — the actual
durable carriers of "being from Hope" identity — wants additional fragments of the founding birth registry
recovered from the city's ruins. For specific families, this isn't academic: it's the only way to learn
whether their own name made it into the record their whole civic identity is built around. The player is
asked to help search the ruins for whatever fragments remain.

**BUT** — The registry's own partial survival isn't evenly distributed. Some sections of the ruins are more
thoroughly destroyed than others, meaning the search itself will confirm some families' presence in the
record while, for others, either leaving their fate exactly as uncertain as before or — worse — confirming
that their own section was among what's actually lost. The recovery effort isn't neutral good news waiting
to happen; it's an uneven process that will resolve some people's grief and deepen others', entirely by the
accident of where the war's damage physically landed.

**THEREFORE** — The player has to decide how to actually conduct the search — not simply "find what's
findable," but reckon directly with the fact that finishing a section either delivers relief or forecloses
hope, and there's no way to search without eventually delivering one or the other to specific, real families
waiting on the result.

**BUT** — A genuine, harder complication surfaces as the search continues: time and resources are limited,
and searching the most damaged, least-promising sections — where completing the search would mostly just
convert someone's ambiguous loss into certain loss, with little realistic chance of good news — means
diverting effort away from more intact sections, where finishing the search is far more likely to deliver
an actual, welcome confirmation to a living family. The player has to choose which families' uncertainty
gets addressed first, and by what standard: likelihood of good news, historical significance, simple order,
or something else entirely.

**THEREFORE** — What began as an archival recovery errand becomes a real, felt triage of whose grief or
relief gets resolved first, with real people on the other end of every section searched or left for later.

**BUT** — The reversal cuts against a clean answer either way. Prioritizing the sections most likely to
yield confirmations delivers the greatest total number of relieved, comforted families — but effectively
deprioritizes the families already facing the worst odds, the very people whose loss is closest to certain,
leaving them waiting longest or never reached at all, abandoned to permanent ambiguity because helping them
was statistically the least efficient use of the search. Working through the ruins in the registry's own
original order — fair, unbiased, no favorites played with anyone's grief — delivers fewer total confirmations
and moves slower, meaning some families who could have received quick, certain good news instead wait
longer, or the search runs out of time before reaching them at all.

**THEREFORE** — Consistent with this project's own No Good Endings law, the resolution is the player's own
choice in how the search is actually conducted: prioritize likely successes, maximizing total relief
delivered at the cost of effectively abandoning the families with the worst odds to the longest wait; or
proceed in fair, unbiased order, honoring every family's claim equally at the cost of delivering less total
good news, more slowly, to people who might otherwise have gotten a clear answer in time. Either way, the
registry itself remains genuinely, permanently incomplete — the player's choice shapes only whose specific
uncertainty gets addressed, and in what order, not whether some names simply never come back at all.

## Self-check (per the method's §7)

Reading only the conjunctions: SETUP → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE. No repeated
conjunction in a row. Each BUT changes the shape of the problem: the first BUT turns "recover some records"
into "recovering them delivers relief to some people and confirmed loss to others, by accident of damage
pattern"; the second BUT turns "search everywhere" into "limited time forces a real choice about whose
uncertainty gets addressed first"; the third BUT turns "pick a search order" into "every order costs some
specific, real family something, whether through delay or through being deprioritized entirely." Deleting
any one Complication changes what the player is actually choosing at the end, so none reads as a disguised
And Then.

## Worth Your Attention

This candidate's core asset is that its central dilemma is emotional and informational rather than material
— unlike Candidate #1's siligel scarcity, there's no physical resource being rationed here, only the order in
which real people's uncertainty about their own family's fate gets resolved, which makes the triage
uncomfortably intimate in a way a supply shortage isn't. It's also the first Palmer subnet candidate built
entirely around Esperanza's own established fact that its living memory now runs through robots rather than
human descendants — meaning the people the player is ultimately serving, in-fiction, are as likely to be
long-lived robot caregivers carrying this grief across generations as any human descendant with a personal
stake of their own.
