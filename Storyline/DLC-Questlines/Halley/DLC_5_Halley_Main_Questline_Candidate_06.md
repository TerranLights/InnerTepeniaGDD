# DLC 5 (Halley Subnet) — Main Questline Candidate #6: "Whoever Holds the Runway"

**What this is:** a sixth prospective But/Therefore chain for DLC 5's main questline, produced by running
`../DLC_Main_Questline_Design_Method.md` against Halley subnet, revisiting Troll — already the anchor of
Candidate #1 — from a completely different angle: not who materially controls the airfield, but what that
fight has been doing to the city's own older civic identity for generations. **Not canon, not chosen, not
the only candidate** — the 6th of the "at least 10" the method calls for. Uses the Linear Escalation Chain
schematic (`../But_Therefore_Quest_Design_Method.md` §5a).

**Uses 1 of Halley subnet's 8 cities (Troll alone)** — a solo candidate, matching the pattern already used
for Princess Elisabeth (Candidate #3). Assumes Candidate #1's own faction conflict as background context
that may or may not be independently in progress or resolved; this chain's own stakes don't require it to
have gone any particular way.

**Confirmed independent of Salagéa Aparast** — consistent with the developer's own standing note that DLC 5
must be completable whether or not the player recruits her. Like Candidates #1–5, this chain doesn't touch
her personal questline at all; she remains a separately-scoped, optional recruitable companion running on
her own track.

---

## Inputs actually used (Step 1 of the design method)

- **Troll's own Full Extrapolation, Section III** — genuine St. Roald (Amundsen) veneration persists at
  Troll, tracing back to the 1939 Queen Maud Land claim and the Polar Institute's own founding-era history,
  but has been increasingly overshadowed by the airfield-conflict identity in recent generations: a quieter,
  more private practice among older residents, while the city's dominant public identity has shifted almost
  entirely to "whoever holds the runway." An explicitly live, unresolved tension.
- **Troll's own Full Extrapolation, Section IV** — the two holidays developed directly from that tension:
  St. Roald's Landing (older, quieter, tied to founding Norwegian heritage) and Runway Day (newer, more
  civically dominant, celebrating the airfield's own founding or expansion) — the contrast between the two
  observances mirroring the city's own identity shift directly.
- **Troll's own Full Extrapolation, Section I** — the airfield's own fractured control: one faction holding
  the runway and tower, another the fuel depot, another trucking dispatch — none holding all of it, and all
  three plausibly hungry for any usable infrastructure or land they can fold into their own position.
- **Troll's own Full Extrapolation, Section V** — Chief Airfield Engineer Kristoffer Adeyemi-Solberg, the
  founding-era Norwegian Polar Institute figure credited with the airfield's original operational capacity —
  a natural ancestral figure for a present-day keeper of the older Norwegian civic tradition to be descended
  from or connected to.
- **Explicitly excluded:** naming a winning faction in the airfield's own central conflict, and any claim
  that this chain resolves Candidate #1's own stakes — the two are deliberately independent, since the
  method calls for distinct candidates, not a single continuous storyline told across files.

## The Chain

**SETUP** — An elder among Troll's residents, a present-day keeper of the older St. Roald veneration and
plausibly descended from Chief Airfield Engineer Kristoffer Adeyemi-Solberg's own founding-era line, asks
the player for help protecting a specific site tied to the tradition — a small shrine, or a marker connected
to Amundsen's own historic claim — from being absorbed into the airfield's own ongoing sprawl. It looks like
a straightforward preservation task: keep one old, quiet place from being paved over or requisitioned.

**BUT** — The site turns out to sit somewhere genuinely useful to the airfield conflict itself — plausible
land for a fuel-depot expansion, a dispatch yard, or a fresh stretch of taxiway, exactly the kind of thing
any of Troll's three contesting factions would want. Protecting the shrine means going back into the same
fractured, three-way conflict Candidate #1 centers on, but for a stake none of those factions actually care
about on its own terms — a bureaucratic nuisance to be negotiated around, not a cause any of them are
invested in either way.

**THEREFORE** — The player negotiates the site's protection, faction by faction, treating it as a genuinely
solvable problem: get an exemption, a rerouted expansion plan, or a formal boundary that keeps the shrine
standing regardless of how the larger airfield question eventually resolves.

**BUT** — It works, physically — the shrine survives, land untouched, the immediate threat resolved — and
that's exactly when the deeper problem becomes visible, because saving the site was never actually the hard
part. Troll's younger residents, raised entirely inside the "whoever holds the runway" civic identity
(Section III), don't participate in the veneration and mostly don't know what the shrine is for. The site is
physically safe and functionally empty. Demolition was never the real threat — irrelevance was, and no
negotiation with any faction was ever going to touch that, since the airfield conflict didn't erase the
tradition by destroying it, it simply took up all the civic attention the tradition used to live in, one
generation at a time.

**THEREFORE** — What the player actually has to decide isn't "can this be saved" — it already has been,
physically — but what saving an empty, functionally ceremonial site is actually worth, and whether trying to
force it back into living relevance is honest or just a different kind of paving-over.

**BUT** — The reversal cuts against a clean answer either way. Formally preserving St. Roald's Landing as a
protected, recorded civic institution — a status independent of whichever faction eventually wins the
airfield fight — genuinely guarantees the site and the observance survive on paper, the way Sanay's own SANAE
research heritage survives as institutional memory rather than lived work. But it also means accepting, out
loud, that the tradition is now history rather than practice, a decision some of the elder's own community
might experience as a second, quieter loss layered on top of the first — better than demolition, but still
a kind of ending, formalized rather than fought.

**THEREFORE** — Consistent with this project's own No Good Endings law, the resolution is the player's own
choice alongside the elder: formalize St. Roald's Landing as a protected, institutional observance,
guaranteeing its physical and ceremonial survival at the cost of accepting it's no longer a living civic
practice; or leave it exactly as informal and private as it's always been, refusing the "museum piece"
framing even at the risk that an unprotected, undocumented tradition genuinely could disappear the next time
any faction needs the land for something the shrine can't defend itself against. Neither choice undoes what
the airfield conflict has already done to Troll's own civic attention over the generations it's had to do it
in.

## Self-check (per the method's §7)

Reading only the conjunctions: SETUP → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE. No repeated
conjunction in a row. Each BUT changes the shape of the problem: the first BUT turns "protect a quiet site"
into "protecting it means renegotiating with the same three-way airfield conflict, for a stake none of them
care about"; the second BUT turns "the site is saved" into "saving it physically didn't save what it was
for"; the third BUT turns "formalize the tradition" into "formalizing it is itself a kind of loss, not a
clean fix." Deleting any one Complication changes what the player is actually choosing at the end, so none
reads as a disguised And Then.

## Worth Your Attention

This candidate's core asset is that it reuses Troll's own established central conflict (Section I, already
Candidate #1's anchor) without repeating Candidate #1's own engine at all — here the airfield fight is
background pressure on a civic-identity question, not the stake itself, and the actual climax has nothing to
do with who wins it. It's also the first Halley subnet candidate whose real antagonist is neither a faction
nor a resource shortage but the simple, generational passage of attention away from an old practice toward a
newer, louder one — a genuinely different register from the resource fights, credit disputes, and triage
choices in Candidates #1, #2, #4, and #5, closer in spirit to Candidate #3's meditation on what's owed to a
population too small (or, here, too historically distant) to fully speak for itself anymore.
