# DLC 2 (Byrd) — Main Questline Candidate #20: "What's Actually in the Ledger"

**What this is:** a civic-records transparency candidate, distinct from Candidate #02's archaeological
pre-exile mystery — this one is about Byrd's own recent, Tepenian-era administrative history, not the deep,
pre-exile layers. No Cradle content. **Not canon, not chosen.**

---

## Inputs actually used

- **Attribute #66** — a formal city records/archive hall, distinct from the deliberately-absent centralized
  archive for pre-exile finds (Finding 3's own established exception) — ordinary civic recordkeeping exists
  and is thorough.
- **Finding 27** — Reading Rooms staff give a clean, honest "it isn't here" when something genuinely isn't
  documented, rather than deflecting.
- **Course of Events chain #1** — the founding-era "descend carefully, in verified stages" compromise
  hardened into inherited procedure — an improvised, not fully documented, early administrative habit.
- **Finding 7** — specialized institutional knowledge and, by extension, institutional advantage plausibly
  concentrates among founding-descendant families relative to the far larger, later-arrived majority.
- **Finding 44** — formal civic administration and practical elevator-chokepoint control are already
  established as two only-partially-overlapping loci of power in Byrd.

---

## The Chain

**SETUP** — The player is asked (independently, or as a follow-up to Candidate #04 or #10) to look into a
discrepancy in Byrd's own civic records — an old resource or space allocation decision that reads
differently in the official ledger than residents' own living memory suggests.

**BUT** — The Reading Rooms staff are honest and thorough about everything else, but this specific stretch
of the record is genuinely incomplete — not hidden, just never properly documented in the first place,
consistent with chain #1's own improvised, not-fully-formalized early administrative habits.

**THEREFORE** — The player has to piece together what actually happened from multiple partial, informal
sources — the same scattered-evidence method chain #9 already establishes for pre-exile finds, applied here
to recent civic history instead.

**BUT** — What emerges is suggestive, not conclusive: a pattern consistent with founding-descendant families
having quietly benefited from ambiguous early decisions more than the historical record can definitively
prove, an accumulated advantage (Finding 7) rather than any single documented act of unfairness.

**THEREFORE** — The player has to decide how to handle a real but genuinely ambiguous finding — going public
risks starting a real civic conflict on incomplete evidence; staying quiet leaves a possible historical
unfairness formally unaddressed.

**BUT (reversal)** — Even treating the finding as conclusive raises its own fairness question: correcting a
generations-old, informally-accumulated advantage means asking people alive today — who didn't personally
make the original ambiguous decisions — to give something up for choices made before they were born.
Neither disclosure nor discretion is a clean answer.

**THEREFORE (resolution)** — Per No Good Endings: push the finding into the open despite its ambiguity,
risking real civic conflict built on incomplete evidence in the name of transparency; or keep it as a quiet
historical note, avoiding a possibly unjust confrontation at the cost of leaving a real, if unprovable,
historical imbalance unaddressed.

## Self-check

SETUP → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE. The first BUT turns "check the records" into
"the records themselves are genuinely incomplete." The second BUT means what's found is suggestive, not
proof. The third BUT means even certainty wouldn't make correcting it simple. No Complication is deletable
without losing the final choice's weight.

## Worth Your Attention

This candidate gives Finding 7's own demographic tension a genuinely uncomfortable, morally ambiguous
register — unlike Candidates #04, #10, and #15, which each stage the tension as a live, present-day dispute
with clear present-day stakeholders, this one asks whether the *past* itself owes anyone anything, a harder
and more unsettled question the rest of the pool doesn't directly address.
