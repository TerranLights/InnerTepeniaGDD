# DLC 6 (Janbogo/Ross Subnet) — Main Questline Candidate #8: "As Many As Still Fit"

**What this is:** an eighth prospective But/Therefore chain for DLC 6's main questline, produced by running
`../DLC_Main_Questline_Design_Method.md` against Janbogo/Ross subnet, built around Scott's own established
founding choice — deliberate smallness — placed under genuine, sympathetic pressure from the subnet's own
established post-war tragedies. **Not canon, not chosen, not the only candidate** — the 8th of the "at least
10" the method calls for. Uses the Linear Escalation Chain schematic
(`../But_Therefore_Quest_Design_Method.md` §5a).

**Uses 1 of Janbogo/Ross subnet's 7 cities (Scott alone)** — its first true solo candidate; it previously
appeared only alongside Fort McMurdo in Candidate #2's Erebus crisis.

---

## Inputs actually used (Step 1 of the design method)

- **Scott's own Mega-Init** — founded on Scott Base's deliberately modest infrastructure, "kept smaller by
  choice, not necessity"; overwhelmingly residential, quiet, family-oriented, the direct experiential
  counterpart to Fort McMurdo's constant operational churn three kilometers away.
- **Scott's own Local Cultures, Section 5** — Scott's whole civic identity organized around quality and
  precision over scale; New Zealand, the founding-operator nation, has diluted to Notable tier (3.52%), "one
  of the deepest founding-nation dilutions documented anywhere in Tepenia," yet the culture "shows no sign
  of having noticed the demographic shift" — a genuinely lived, functionally-transmitted civic template, not
  a ceremonial one (consistent with Cross-City Patterns, Pattern 2's own framework, though Scott isn't one
  of that pattern's four directly-tested candidate cities).
- **Scott's own Local Cultures, Section 6** — hospitality as warm, personal, small-city intimate; "people
  know each other's names here in a way genuinely impossible at Fort McMurdo's scale" — an intimacy this
  chain treats as literally dependent on population scale, not merely correlated with it.
- **The subnet's own established post-war tragedies** — Zukelli, destroyed near its absolute population
  peak (Candidate #1's own grounding); Denison, destroyed outright with a diaspora now scattered as far as
  Concordia (Candidate #3); Cape Adare, destroyed late in the war as a symbolic target (Candidate #4) — all
  three giving this chain a genuine, sympathetic source of displaced people with real, established reasons
  to need somewhere stable and family-oriented to go.
- **Explicitly excluded:** any claim about which specific destroyed city's survivors seek resettlement in
  Scott, or how many — left open, since the point is the structural dilemma itself, not a specific settled
  headcount.

## The Chain

**SETUP** — Displaced residents from the subnet's own destroyed cities — plausibly some mix of Zukelli's,
Denison's, and Cape Adare's own scattered survivors — request resettlement in Scott specifically, drawn by
its established stability, its family-oriented civic character, and its precision-industry economy, real
qualities that could genuinely help people rebuilding a life from nothing. The player is asked to help
negotiate the resettlement — a straightforward, humane logistics task on its face.

**BUT** — Scott's entire civic identity is founded on staying small by deliberate choice, not necessity
(Mega-Init), and its celebrated intimacy — "people know each other's names" — is a direct, literal function
of that smallness, not a value that survives being scaled up by policy. A substantial, genuinely helpful
influx of new residents doesn't just add population; it threatens the specific mechanism that makes Scott
what it is. This isn't a dressed-up objection to newcomers — it's a real founding-level value colliding with
an entirely sympathetic, worthy need.

**THEREFORE** — The player has to help Scott's own community actually work through this directly — not
simply approve or reject the resettlement, but negotiate how much growth, if any, the city can absorb without
losing the thing the resettlement was drawn to in the first place.

**BUT** — There's no safe number to negotiate toward, because Scott's intimacy was never a policy that can
be tuned to a target population — it's an emergent, lived-in-daily-practice culture, the same kind of
functional transmission that makes founding cultures durable elsewhere in the subnet (Cross-City Patterns,
Pattern 2), and nobody can actually predict in advance whether it survives real growth or quietly stops being
true once enough new residents arrive that the city feels less like something people are welcomed into and
more like something being built fresh alongside strangers. Meanwhile, capping the number sharply to protect
that uncertain intimacy means turning away specific, real people with nowhere as good left to go — survivors
of tragedies the subnet's own history already establishes in detail.

**THEREFORE** — What looked like a resettlement logistics problem becomes a genuine wager: risk the one
thing that makes Scott worth moving to, or protect it by turning away the people it would most help.

**BUT** — The reversal is that both outcomes are real, and neither is safely reversible once chosen. Accepting
a meaningful number of newcomers and genuinely trying to integrate them — not just house them, but actually
extend Scott's own real intimacy to them — is a compassionate gamble with an outcome nobody can guarantee:
the culture might absorb the growth and come out changed but still genuinely itself, the way Janbogo's own
established absorption culture manages, or it might simply stop being what made it worth choosing in the
first place. Limiting resettlement to protect Scott's own identity with real confidence leaves specific,
named tragedies — the same ones this subnet's own history has already made vivid — without the one stable
home in the region genuinely suited to help them rebuild.

**THEREFORE** — Consistent with this project's own No Good Endings law, the resolution is the player's own
role in how far Scott actually goes: a genuine, meaningful resettlement, accepting real uncertainty about
whether the city's founding intimacy survives its own growth, in service of people who have nowhere else this
good to go; or a deliberately limited intake, protecting Scott's own precious, functionally-lived civic
culture at high confidence, at the direct cost of turning away people whose need is exactly as real as
Scott's own claim to what it would be risking. Neither choice is presented as obviously correct, and unlike
Candidate #6's calm administrative decision, this one carries genuine uncertainty about its own outcome even
after the choice is made — nobody, including the player, actually knows which way Scott's intimacy breaks
until it's already happened.

## Self-check (per the method's §7)

Reading only the conjunctions: SETUP → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE. No repeated
conjunction in a row. Each BUT changes the shape of the problem: the first BUT turns "house some refugees"
into "housing them threatens the exact thing that makes Scott a place worth housing them in"; the second BUT
turns "find the right number" into "there is no safe number, because the thing at risk can't actually be
protected by careful tuning"; the third BUT turns "decide how many to accept" into "the decision carries
real uncertainty about its own outcome, not just a known trade-off." Deleting any one Complication changes
what the player is actually choosing at the end, so none reads as a disguised And Then.

---

## Worth Your Attention

This candidate's core asset is that it puts real, sympathetic pressure on a founding value the project's own
material treats, elsewhere, as an unambiguous strength — Scott's deliberate smallness and its genuinely
lived, functionally-transmitted intimacy — using only tragedies this same subnet's own prior candidates
already established as real (Zukelli, Denison, Cape Adare), rather than inventing a new crisis from nothing.
It's also the first Janbogo subnet candidate whose resolution carries genuine outcome uncertainty rather than
a foreseeable trade-off — the player doesn't choose between two known costs, but between two real risks, and
the story is honest that nobody, including its own protagonist, actually knows which way it breaks until
after the choice is already made.
