# DLC 6 (Janbogo/Ross Subnet) — Main Questline Candidate #14: "Whatever the War Merged, Peace Has to Decide"

**What this is:** a fourteenth prospective But/Therefore chain for DLC 6's main questline, produced by
running `../DLC_Main_Questline_Design_Method.md` against Janbogo/Ross subnet, built around an administrative
service-merger/de-merger decision. **Not canon, not chosen, not the only candidate.** Uses the Linear
Escalation Chain schematic (`../But_Therefore_Quest_Design_Method.md` §5a).

**Uses 5 of Janbogo/Ross subnet's 7 cities** (Fort McMurdo, Scott, Janbogo, Cape Adare, Dumont d'Urville).

---

## Inputs actually used (Step 1 of the design method)

- **Janbogo Cross-City Throughways, Throughway 2** — the established fact that Fort McMurdo's and Scott's
  wartime emergency infrastructure was forced to genuinely merge, on top of their pre-existing, voluntary
  Erebus monitoring cooperation.
- **Janbogo Cross-City Patterns, Pattern 3** — the established "quiet, intimate city living beside a much
  busier neighbor" shape, appearing twice: Scott beside Fort McMurdo, and Cape Adare beside Janbogo — both
  smaller cities carrying a genuinely self-contained civic character that doesn't need their busier
  neighbor's scale to feel complete.
- **Janbogo Cross-City Throughways, Throughway 3** — Denison's own established destruction and its position
  on Hwy 183 near Dumont d'Urville, used here only for the general fact that Dumont d'Urville lost whatever
  informal proximity-based support a neighboring city might have offered, not for any claim about a specific
  pre-war arrangement between the two.
- **Explicitly excluded:** any specific claim about what Fort McMurdo's, Scott's, Janbogo's, or Cape Adare's
  pre-war independent emergency systems actually consisted of — this chain treats the merger's own current,
  functioning state as the baseline, not a specific historical system being compared against.

## The Chain

**SETUP** — With the acute crisis of the war years past, a decision is needed about the emergency-response
mergers it forced: should Fort McMurdo's and Scott's now-combined systems, and Janbogo's and Cape Adare's own
similarly merged arrangement, be formalized permanently, or should each city's own independent, pre-war
services be restored? The player is asked to evaluate both cases and recommend a path forward — a
straightforward, if consequential, administrative-policy task.

**BUT** — The merged systems have become genuinely, measurably more effective than either city's own separate
systems ever were — but maintaining that permanently means Scott and Cape Adare, both cities whose own
established character doesn't need a busier neighbor's scale to feel complete, would permanently lose full
independent control over a core civic function, folding it into their larger neighbor's own administration.

**THEREFORE** — The player has to consult both cities in each pair, weighing whether continued efficiency is
worth a real, lasting loss of full civic independence in this specific domain.

**BUT** — The reversal lands once de-merging is actually costed out: restoring fully separate systems isn't
a return to the old, adequate status quo. Post-war population and resource realities mean neither Scott nor
Cape Adare could fully replicate what the merged system currently provides on its own — a full de-merger
would leave both smaller cities with genuinely worse emergency coverage than they have right now, not simply
their old, acceptable baseline back.

**THEREFORE** — What began as weighing efficiency against autonomy becomes a harder question: whether full
independence is worth accepting an objectively worse outcome than what currently exists, not an even trade
between two comparably good options.

**BUT** — The reversal cuts against a clean answer either way. Formalizing the merger permanently locks in
real, measurable safety and efficiency for everyone involved, avoiding the genuinely worse coverage a full
de-merger would produce — but permanently folds a core civic function Scott and Cape Adare once controlled
independently into their larger neighbors' own administration, a real, felt loss for cities whose established
character specifically doesn't lean on a busier neighbor by choice. Restoring full independence honors that
self-contained character and gives both smaller cities back complete control over their own emergency
response — but means knowingly accepting worse coverage than what they currently have, a real, material
safety cost paid for institutional independence.

**THEREFORE** — Consistent with this project's own No Good Endings law, the resolution is the player's own
role in whether these wartime mergers get formalized or unwound: formalize them, securing better coverage at
the cost of Scott's and Cape Adare's own full independence; or restore separate systems, protecting that
independence at the cost of real, accepted safety risk.

## Self-check (per the method's §7)

Reading only the conjunctions: SETUP → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE → BUT → THEREFORE. No repeated
conjunction in a row. Each BUT changes the shape of the problem: the first BUT turns "recommend a policy"
into "efficiency and independence are genuinely opposed, not both achievable"; the second BUT turns "weigh
the trade-off" into "de-merging isn't a neutral return to baseline, it's a real downgrade"; the third BUT
turns "merge or restore" into "there is no option that doesn't cost either real independence or real,
accepted safety risk." Deleting any one Complication changes what the player is actually choosing at the end,
so none reads as a disguised And Then.

## Worth Your Attention

This candidate's core asset is that it takes Pattern 3's own established relationship shape — a smaller city
content beside a busier neighbor, needing nothing from that neighbor's scale — and asks what happens when
wartime necessity already broke that self-containment once, whether or not anyone involved actually wanted
it to. It also gives Dumont d'Urville a genuinely different kind of stake than the other four cities in this
chain — not a party to either merger, but a working contrast case for what happens when a city loses a
neighboring capacity with no merger, and no choice, at all.
